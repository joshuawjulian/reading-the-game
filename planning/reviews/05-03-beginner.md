# 05-03 Odd and Hybrid Fronts — beginner reader review

Reviewer persona: casual NFL watcher, never played, has read 01-01 through 05-02 in order.
Read the qmd prose start to finish and looked at all 23 PDF pages (60 dpi, pages 8/15/16 again at 130 dpi).
Overall: strong, mostly very clear chapter. The odd/even test, the Okie job list, the two-gap vs one-gap
figure, and the "roster label vs picture" data chart are excellent. The problems are a few internal
contradictions (counting "men on the line", 3-3-5, Fig 7 caption, tite "free" Mike), one zone-rules leap
that a careful reader of 03-02 will trip on, and some figure/label inconsistencies. ~9,100 words including
captions and footnotes (spec ~5,000); it reads well but the "roster vs picture" lesson is repeated a lot.

## 1. Terms used before explained, or never explained

- **"three or five men on the line" vs. this chapter's own counting** (Odd and even section: "the classic
  versions have three or five men on the line (the 3-4 and the old 5-2) rather than four"; same in the
  `Odd front` glossary entry). Everywhere else the chapter counts standing edges as on the line (opener:
  "Four players at the line ... two of them standing up"; tite = "five on the line"). By that count the 3-4
  Okie in Fig 2 has *five* on the line, not three. Either say "three or five with hands down" or "the 3-4
  (five on the line once you count its standing outside linebackers)". As written I was confused which
  count is "the line".
- **Is a one-gap 3-4 / a G front odd or even?** The chapter's first test is "is anybody head-up on the
  center?" Then Fig 3 (right) shades the nose to a 1 and Fig 10 (right) moves him to a 2i. By the chapter's
  own test both are now *even* fronts, but it never says so. "The roster says 3-4. The football says Under"
  half-implies it; one sentence ("by the center test this is an even front") would close the loop and
  strengthen the main lesson.
- **"alley"** — Fig 5 caption ("fill the alley (shaded)"), Drill 1 answer ("beat the ball to the alley"),
  Drill 2 answer. Owned by 05-05 (alley player); only met in passing in 01-03. Needs a half-line gloss
  ("the lane between the edge and the sideline-side defenders where a bounced run goes").
- **"hybrid edge"** (history paragraph, linked to 11-04, no gloss). Right next to this chapter's own
  "hybrid defense" and "stand-up edge", so I wondered: is a hybrid edge just a stand-up edge? One clause.
- **"simulated pressures of Part 7"** (Why stand? #1) — linked, no gloss of what one is.
- **"Eagle defense", "Oklahoma 5-2", "53 defense"** are bolded (book convention = defined term) but only the
  53 is linked; fine, but bold-without-link reads as "you should know this".
- **"cheats down"** (Fig 6 label "safety cheats down over Y") — slang, never defined; obvious enough from
  context, but "moved down closer to" is plainer.
- **"other / unlisted"** in the Fig 8 legend — never explained (what are those snaps?).
- **4i job (one-gap or two-gap?)** — never stated in this chapter. Glossary says "cover ... both B gaps";
  "What the tite gives up" says the tite needs "two 4is with the length to play from an inside shade"
  ("Rare players, again. Like the two-gap 3-4"). 05-01's Drill answer said the 4is are "most likely
  one-gap". Say it here.

## 2. Leaps — missing or assumed "why"

- **The Mike is "free" in the tite — but the arithmetic says he owns a gap.** "Six men, seven gaps" section:
  nose 2 (A, A), 4is 2 (B, B), edges 2 (outside gaps) = 6; the seventh gap (C on the TE side) must be the
  Mike's. Yet the text says "the linebacker runs free", "He doesn't need to beat a block; he only needs to be
  right about where the ball ends up", Fig 5 "the one linebacker (ringed) runs free". Untouched ≠
  unassigned. 05-01 told me "one inside linebacker fills the gap left over ... the other is the free one",
  and in nickel there is only one. A gap-letter chart of the tite (like Fig 3) would fix this, and Fig 4's
  back bending "outside the tackle" (= the C gap) into the Mike would then make perfect sense.
- **"(or one and a half)" muddies the six-for-seven math.** "One nose who holds both A gaps (or one and a
  half) is the extra defender" — if he holds 1.5 the count is 6.5 of 7. Explain what fills the other half
  (the Mike? the backside 4i?).
- **"4is and the nose are inside their blockers, the easiest kind of reach"** (Fig 5 right label and caption;
  Drill 2 answer). I applied 03-02's covered/uncovered rule for outside zone right: the play-side 4i is on the
  right tackle's *inside* shoulder, so for the *right guard* he is in the play-side gap, outside him — which by
  this chapter's own rule ("to beat the reach, line up outside the blockers") is the *hard* reach. Who exactly
  reaches the 4i, and why is it easy? This is the chapter's central inside-vs-outside argument, so the
  blocker-by-blocker "who blocks whom" needs to be shown, not asserted.
- **Bounce outside: good or bad for the tite?** "It turns the inside runs ... into outside runs that its edges
  and safeties can chase down" vs two paragraphs later "The perimeter ... the defense outside them is thin ...
  Runs that start outside ... go straight at that thinness." Both may be true (a *bounced* inside run is slow
  and late; a *designed* outside run has blockers out there), but the chapter never draws that distinction,
  so they read as a contradiction.
- **"The guard can't reach him and the tackle can't leave him"** (tite vs inside zone bullet) — why can't the
  guard reach a man one gap over? One clause (angle / he's already engaged on the nose double).
- **G front mechanism**: "forces the center to step sideways to block the nose, which slows down the
  center-guard double team on inside zone and gives the linebacker behind the nose a cleaner path." Why does a
  sideways step slow the double, and why is the linebacker's path cleaner? Asserted, not shown (Fig 10 right
  shows only the alignment).
- **Bear connection promised, never delivered.** 05-02 says the bear idea "is alive in the tite front" and
  calls the tite "the bear's cousin, covering the tackles instead of the guards". 05-03 never mentions the bear.
  One sentence ("why cover the tackles' inside shoulders instead of the guards like the bear? ...") would build
  on what I just learned and answer an obvious question.
- **Why the nose "can play *either* A gap, so the offense can't know where he'll go"** (Odd and even section)
  — then the two-gap section says he doesn't "go" anywhere, he controls and reads. Reword so it doesn't
  contradict two-gap play.
- **3-3-5 contradiction.** Bullet: "In the NFL, the 3-3-5 is usually a five-man line in nickel." Next
  paragraph: "in the data ... '3-3-5' often means neither: a four-man line with one edge listed as a defensive
  end and the other as a linebacker." And Fig 8's table shows the 3-3-5 label with the same box count and
  four-man-rush rate as the others. Which is it? Suggest: "coaches who *call* a 3-3-5 mean a five-man line;
  the *roster count* 3-3-5 usually just means mixed labels."

## 3. Diagrams I couldn't decode / captions

- **Fig 7 (fig-245) caption is wrong for the picture.** "Same alignments" — left is 5-3-1-5, right is

  techniques or change the caption to "same four-man shape".
- **Fig 4 (tite vs inside zone strip):** (a) the backside (left) E goes *upfield and wide* into the backfield
  in panels 3–4, while the text says "the backside edge is squeezing down the line behind them" — picture
  contradicts text; (b) offensive-line labels C and RT are hidden under the defenders' squares in panels 2–4;
  (c) caption says "two safeties deep" but neither safety appears until the SS pops in at panel 4 (FS never);
  (d) panel 4 says "the unblocked Mike fills: a one-yard gain" but the Mike is still ~1.5 yd from the runner and
  there is no tackle mark. Also page 7 is half blank because the strip got pushed to page 8.
- **Fig 9 (same personnel, two fronts):** the "How to read the diagrams" key says defensive linemen are
  labelled with technique numbers, but here they are "T", "T", "E", and the ringed E keeps the label "E" after
  he puts his hand down as a 4i. I had to read the caption to map T→3/1/4i. Panel 4 is a tangle: I couldn't tell
  which four rushed without the caption; ring or tag the dropping Mike in panel 4 and the four rushers. At
  print size the panels are small; labels are barely legible at 60 dpi.
- **Label letters change across figures:** OL are "LT LG C RG RT" in Figs 2, 4, 6, 9, Drill 1 but "T G C G T"
  in Figs 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, Drills 2–3; Fig 2 labels the fullback/tailback "F" and "T", and "T" also means an
  offensive tackle (Fig 7) and a defensive tackle (Fig 9). Pick one scheme; at least don't reuse "T" for three
  different players.
- **Fig 2 (Okie plate):** OL circles overlap so "LT LG C RG RT" are crammed; otherwise excellent.
- **Fig 5:** good, but the "4is and 0: inside their blockers, easy to reach" label is exactly the claim I
  couldn't reconcile (see §2).
- **Fig 8:** great chart. Explain the grey "other / unlisted". The table splits across pages 13–14 (header
  repeats; fine).
- **Go deeper table** ("Mike ... 4.4 yd, 3.2 yd, 2.3 yd"): what number would mean "a blocker reached him"? No
  threshold or comparison, so I can't interpret falling distances (he's moving toward the line anyway).

## 5. Can I do the "You'll be able to…" items?

1. Odd vs even by the center: **Yes**, for the clear cases. Unsure for a shaded nose (one-gap 3-4) and the
   G front — chapter never classifies them (§1).
2. Diagram the Okie; two-gap vs one-gap by alignment and first step: **Yes.** Fig 3 + Drill 3 make this work.
3. Recognize a tite, why it walls off inside runs, what it gives up: **Mostly.** I can spot it and list costs,
   but I can't do the gap accounting (who owns the 7th gap, is the Mike free) or say whether the 4is one-gap.
4. Identify 4-2-5 / 3-3-5 / 2-4-5 by hands and edges; why label and picture disagree: **4-2-5 and 2-4-5 yes;
   3-3-5 no** — I came away with two incompatible meanings (§2).
5. How a defense picks a front for inside vs outside zone: **Rule of thumb yes** ("on/inside blockers beats
   doubles, outside beats reaches"); **mechanism no** — I can't reconcile the "4i is an easy reach" claim with
   03-02's rules.
6. Decode dialect (Okie, Eagle, G, reduced): **Yes** for Okie/Eagle/G; "reduced" is defined but only ever
   pictured as the tite (and the Under from 05-02 by reference). A one-side reduction picture isn't needed, but
   say "the Under in Even Fronts' Fig X is a reduced front".

## 6. Knowledge gaps — what I still wonder

- **How often is the tite / any odd front actually played in the NFL in 2025?** The chapter measures roster
  labels (Fig 8) but never gives a single number for the alignments it teaches. Even "no public data tags the
  front; here's what charting sites report" would help.
- **What does an odd front do when the tight end isn't attached (2x2, empty)?** Five blockers vs a tite — does
  the tite still make sense, or does it become a four-man front? Fig 6 shows only the 4-2-5 vs 2x2.
- **The tite against gap runs** (power/counter with a pulling guard): "That is the tite's answer to inside
  zone, power, duo" — only inside zone is shown. What happens when the guard pulls and the 4i is left?
- **Tite gap chart**: who owns which gap, including the Mike and the backside edge (squeeze vs contain).
- **Odd-front strength rule**: even fronts set strength to the TE (05-02). The Okie is balanced, but the
  one-gap 3-4, the tite's 9-technique edge and the G tag all go "toward the tight end". Is there a strength rule
  for odd fronts, and what happens when the offense shifts the TE?
- **On TV, how do I tell a 4i from a 4 from a 5?** The Watch-for-it list asks me to; from the high sideline
  camera a shoulder's width is hard to see. A tip (look at which side of the tackle's helmet the lineman's
  helmet is) would make the checklist usable.
- **Is the tite a one-gap or two-gap front?** The chapter says both branches "meet in one front" but never says
  how the 4is play.
- **Who runs what in 2025/2026?** The film rooms stop at 2024 Eagles; a one-line current pointer (with season
  labels per FACTS-current) would help a reader watching this Sunday.
