# 06-02 Man Coverage — beginner reader review

Reviewer persona: a casual NFL watcher who has never played and has read everything before this chapter in curriculum order (Parts 1–5 as they exist, incl. 02-05, 02-06, 03-04, 04-01/03/04/07, and 06-01). I read it start to finish in the PDF (`pdfs/06-02-man-coverage.pdf`, 22 pp; built 14 s after the last .qmd save, so it's current), rasterized at 60 dpi with pages 8, 9, 11 and 20 at 130 dpi, and checked the source for figure coordinates.

**Overall:** this is one of the strongest chapters so far. The spine ("the free defender is the whole game"; "play away from your help") is clear and memorable, the Cover 0 arithmetic lands, and the situational chart plus the source-break caveat is exactly what an analytically minded reader wants. I could do every "You'll be able to…" item and got all three drills right before opening the answers. The problems:
(a) **two terms collide**: "in-out" is used for banjo *and* for a bracket type, and "switch" (from 02-06) now also means banjo;
(b) **the reshuffle is never explained**: the strong safety covers the tight end in Fig 1, then is the robber in Figs 3–4 and Drill 1, and the "deep safety and robber rush" in Cover 0 while the drawing has them covering. Who takes the tight end when the SS robs?;
(c) **the banjo strip doesn't show what the caption claims** at print size;
(d) **Drills 2 and 3 copy Figs 5 and 8**, so they test recognition, not transfer. Drill 3's picture is also a two-high shell, and the chapter never says how a bracket differs from 2-Man / Cover 2;
(e) **repetition**: Ty Law is told three times, the press-means-man misconception is repeated from 06-01, and Surtain/Metcalf and the Patriots' 2019 man share are each told twice.
The chapter also runs well over its ~5,000-word target (roughly 7,000 words of prose by my count).

## 2. Leaps (a missing or assumed "why")

1. **Who covers the tight end when the strong safety becomes the robber?** Fig 1: the SS has Y, and the Mike is the free man. Fig 3: the SS is the robber "who started deep". Fig 4: the SS is the robber and the *Mike* has Y. Drill 1: the SS is free again. The text says "classically he is a safety who lines up deep … and comes down", but never says the matching rules change so that a linebacker picks up the tight end. A beginner who just learned "a safety or linebacker takes the tight end" can't square these pictures. **Add one sentence** to "Who takes whom" or to the robber bullet: "If a safety is going to rob, a linebacker takes the tight end, and the 'free' man moves from the linebacker level to the safety level."
2. **Cover 0, "The players who would have been free (the deep safety and the robber) rush the quarterback instead."** In Fig 5 the FS and SS *cover* R and Y, and the *linebackers* rush. So the literal claim is wrong for the picture. Say it as arithmetic: "the two defenders who would have been free become two more rushers; usually that means linebackers rush and the safeties drop down to cover." (Also 02-06's Corn Dog figure says that in Cover 0 the FS who had the blocking back "just sits", while this chapter says "his man defender has nobody to cover and joins the rush". Both happen; say so, or the reader sees a contradiction.)
3. **"Why it exists: Cover 1": "with one safety deep, the other is free to come down, so the defense can still stop the run."** In this chapter's own Cover 1 (Fig 1) the "other" safety is in man on the tight end. How is he helping against the run? I think the point is that the man-on-the-TE safety is near the box anyway, but the text doesn't say that.
4. **Why is first down a zone down?** The chart shows man at only 25–36% on first down. The text explains third-and-long (let them catch it and tackle) but never explains first down. I'd guess run threat and play-action: man defenders have their backs to the backfield. That ties back to the "What it costs" paragraph. One sentence.
5. **"Third down invites a blitz."** Why? The reader knows third down is high leverage, but "invites" assumes that offenses must throw, so a blitz is safer. Say so.
6. **"A third of a second is two or three steps of a receiver's route: the difference between a dig and a slant."** A dig breaks at ~12 yards, a slant at ~1–3 yards. Two or three steps is not that gap. I stopped and re-read. Use "the difference between a slant and a quick out" or "between the second read and the first".
7. **Banjo counters (p. 11–12):** "both receivers breaking the same way, one a beat later than the other, so the inside defender takes the first and the second has a head start on the outside defender". I had to draw this on paper to follow it. It's the most important "how offenses beat it" idea in the section, and it's the only one without a picture. Even a two-panel mini-diagram would do it.
8. **"That is why bunch formations are a red-zone staple."** This comes before the reader learns that man coverage dominates the red zone (two sections later). Either move the sentence or add "where man coverage is most common (see below)".
9. **Answer 2: "a rub … which the press defenders can't banjo easily without deep help."** Why does banjo need deep help? It's never stated. Is it because a banjo concedes a step, or because the outside man can't carry a vertical? Explain or cut.
10. **The Cover 1 free safety's own job.** The chapter says he "plays the deep middle as help for all of them" and "arrives from the middle" on a post. But one man can't help on four or five vertical routes. What does he actually read: the QB, the deepest inside threat? And what happens on two posts at once? This is the most obvious "how does that work?" question, and nothing answers it. (A single sentence like "he reads the quarterback and helps on the deepest inside route; two vertical routes into the middle stress him, the same way a high-low stresses the robber" would close it.)
11. **Cover 0 inside leverage, "the quick one inside, toward the quarterback, where the receiver has the most room to run after the catch."** "Toward the quarterback" is odd. And why is there the most room inside? Because the rushers have vacated the middle and nobody is in the hole. Say so.

## 6. Knowledge gaps (what I still wonder)

1. **What does the Cover 1 free safety actually read**, and what happens when *two* receivers run deep into the middle? (See leap #10.) This is the natural next question after "the robber has a high-low weakness".
2. **How do I tell a bracket from a two-high shell / 2-Man pre-snap?** (Drill 3.)
3. **Pass interference in man coverage.** The chapter covers the pre-throw contact rule, but a beginner's first broadcast question about man coverage is "he never turned around: is that interference?" A sentence on DPI and "playing through the hands" (Rule 8 §5 is already cited in the contact footnote) would answer it.
4. **How common is Cover 1 by itself?** The chapter calls it "the most common man coverage in football" and gives man shares, but never a Cover 1 share (e.g., "about one dropback in four"). One number would anchor it.
5. **What happens on a run play against Cover 1?** The defense still has to fit the run with half its players turned to receivers. Who has the quarterback on a read option? (03-04 raised this; a pointer to 05-05 or 07-02 would do.)
6. **What does a robber do when the ball goes outside?** Does he become a pursuit player and tackler? A short line helps the reader track him on a replay.
7. **Banjo vs pattern-matching.** The banjo is called "a small zone rule inside a man call", and 06-01 taught pattern matching ("zone that turns into man"). How are they different? Connections hints at 06-05, but a sentence here would stop the two ideas blurring.
8. **Why do some elite corners *not* shadow even in man-heavy defenses?** The Sherman contrast explains zone teams, but Gilmore in 2019 (man-heavy) is described as "often on the opponent's top receiver". Was he a full shadow or not? The film room leaves it ambiguous.
