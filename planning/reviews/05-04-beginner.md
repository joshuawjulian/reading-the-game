# 05-04 Linebackers and the Second Level — beginner reader review

Reviewer persona: casual NFL watcher, never played, has read 01-01 through 05-03 in curriculum order.
Read the qmd prose and captions start to finish; looked at all 25 PDF pages (60 dpi), and every figure page
again at 100 dpi (pp. 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, 21, 22, 23).

Overall: a very good chapter. The guard-key table, the "linemen tell the truth sooner than the ball" idea, the
fast/slow flow split, the scrape-vs-zone-read timing/path/leverage list and the play-action "no climb" tell are
exactly what this reader wanted. The problems: (a) the opening scene, Fig 3 and "Back to the huddle" describe
three slightly different plays; (b) "which guard does he key?" is never answered, and Fig 3 contradicts the rule
as stated; (c) "off-ball" vs "outside" linebacker is never defined though two charts depend on it; (d) the
tackle chart is narrated more strongly than it shows; (e) several figures (3 panel 4, 4, 5 plug, 9, 11, Drill 3)
don't let the reader see the thing the caption says to notice. Length is roughly 8,000+ words of prose plus
captions and footnotes against a ~5,000 spec; it reads well, but the "a yard behind his own line, inside-out,
beat the 315-pound guard to the spot" refrain repeats a lot.

## 1. Terms used before explained, or never explained

- **"off-ball linebacker"** — first used at qmd 597 ("Off-ball linebackers usually stand about four to five
  yards deep") with no definition, then carries Fig 8 ("Off-ball linebacker" vs "Outside linebacker / edge"),
  the 22% stat, Fig 12 ("Off-ball linebackers at the Combine"), the Roquan Smith "first for an off-ball
  linebacker" record and its footnote. Not owned anywhere in the term index. One sentence early ("off-ball:
  stands off the line, behind the linemen, as opposed to the edge rushers some teams also call linebackers")
  would fix a real confusion (next bullet).
- **"outside linebacker" means two different things.** Fig 2 caption: "walk both outside linebackers out of the
  box" = the 4-3 Sam and Will. Fig 8 legend/caption: "Outside linebacker / edge ... outside linebackers include
  3-4 edge rushers" = pass rushers with their hand down or standing on the edge. A reader who just learned
  Sam/Will = outside linebackers will read Fig 8's pink line as Sams and Wills. Say it explicitly.
- **"inside linebacker"** (qmd 671 "For an inside linebacker, the two classic keys...") — in a 4-3 is that
  only the Mike, or the Mike and Will? In nickel, both? Never said.
- **"split flow"**, **"inside-out"**, **"leverage player"**, **"hawk tackle"** are bolded (book convention =
  a defined term) but have no glossary link/entry. Inside-out especially is used ~8 times and is the core rule
  of the chapter; it deserves the glossary treatment or plain italics.
- **"Smith"** in the opening ("Smith is gone before the back even gets the ball") — who? A real player
  (Roquan?) or a generic name? A beginner assumes a specific play he should be able to look up.
- **"landmarks"** (Fig 9 caption "drop to their landmarks") — unglossed; owned by 06-01.
- **"deep thirds"** (Fig 9 caption) — Cover 3 was only named in 02-05; one clause ("three deep defenders each
  taking a third of the field") would help.
- **"the field"** as a strength rule (green-dot list item 2: "the tight end, the trips side, the running back,
  the field") — field vs boundary was in 02-02, but here it reads like "the field" in general. Say "the wide
  side of the field".
- **"Legion of Boom"** (Film room Seattle) — no gloss.
- **"kicks out the Sam"** (Fig 3 caption) — kick-out was in 03-03, fine; but "hinges"-type blocking language
  is absent, good.
- **"option route"**, **"banjo"** (Drill 2 answer) — option route is from 04-01, fine; banjo is glossed only
  by the parenthetical "or switching the nickel back onto the back", which is not quite the same thing. One
  clause: "two defenders trade who-covers-whom based on where the receivers go".
- **"run-fit triggers"** ("You'll need" box) — italicized as if a term I should recall from 04-05; it is never
  used again in this chapter, so either use it in the play-action section or drop it.
- **Footnotes cite internal files**: [^threestrike] "as summarized in this course's fact sheet
  (planning/FACTS-current.md §6, rated likely)" and [^wagner] "the course fact sheet (planning/FACTS-current.md
  §2)". A reader has no access to these; it looks like a leftover.

## 2. Leaps — missing or assumed "why"

- **Which guard is the key?** Text (qmd 671): "the two classic keys are the **guard** in front of him and the
  near running back." But Fig 3 has the Mike, aligned right of center over the strong A gap, reading the *left*
  guard three yards away, and the Will reading the same guard. Drill 1 has the Mike reading the *right* guard.
  Play-action (Fig 11) has him reading the *uncovered* guard. So is it the guard in front of him, the uncovered
  guard, the backside guard, or both guards? Give the rule (or rules, by front) and say why the Mike in Fig 3
  watches a guard who is not in front of him.
- **The guard key vs draws and screens.** The key table says "Steps back and sits, hands up ('high hat') ->
  Pass: get to his drop or his man", and the "Why it exists" box says screens are built to make keys lie. But
  the draw is listed under *slow flow* ("an iso, a draw, a delayed handoff") — on a draw the guard high-hats.
  How does a linebacker who believes the guard not get killed by a draw? The chapter names the problem and
  never answers it (the back's set? depth of the QB? "high hat with no rush lane"?). Same for screens: what does
  a linebacker see that tells him "screen" rather than "pass"?
- **"Because the rules and the physics of blocking tell them how to move"** (qmd 677) — which rules? The
  ineligible-downfield rule is only explained 600 lines later in the play-action section. Either move that
  rule up here or say "(see play-action, below)".
- **Stack alignment: no trade named.** Open and walked-out each get "the cost is...". Stacked gets only
  benefits (shielded, disguise). The Takeaways even say "stacked behind a lineman, he is shielded and hard to
  read" — what does he give up? (Farther from outside gaps? Lineman in his way if he has to scrape? Can be
  caught in the wash if the lineman gets driven back?) AUTHORING asks for the trade every time.
- **"say whether a run calls for a fill, a plug, a scrape or a run-through"** (objective 3) vs the text
  "Which one a linebacker uses depends on the call, the front and the play." So from a run alone, I cannot say
  which. The chapter gives me: plug = behind a slanting lineman; scrape = outside run/zone read; fill = iso; run-
  through = zone, when called. Either rephrase the objective or add a 4-row "if you see X, expect Y" table.
- **The tackle chart is narrated as "three bands" but doesn't show LBs dominating any band.** Fig 8: at 3-4
  yards the defensive line still has ~39% vs off-ball LBs ~22%; at 5-6 defensive backs ~30% vs LBs ~26%; at 7-9
  DBs ~50%. The LB line is never on top. Text: "Runs that get past the line run into the linebackers" and the
  shaded band is labelled "the second level". The honest reading is "the LB share *peaks* at 3-9 yards" — and
  the chart raises a question the text skips: why do defensive backs make ~30% of tackles on 5-6 yard runs and
  have a median tackle at 5 yards? (Box safeties? the nickel?) Answer that, since it is the chapter's own theme
  (safety-sized linebackers, big nickel).
- **"Why missed tackles cluster at the second level"** (spec objective; heading "Why the second level is where
  tackles are made, and missed") is argued, not shown. No number is given at all — not even a cited PFF or BDB
  missed-tackle rate by position. And in print, "The cell below (not run in this book, because the data is not
  downloaded) counts missed-tackle rates by position" (p. 12) points at nothing: code is hidden in the PDF, so
  the sentence dangles directly into "Linebackers in coverage".
- **Pursuit math claim.** "In this example the linebacker is the faster man, and he still never gets closer
  than about two yards." An analytically minded reader knows a faster pure-pursuit chaser *does* close the gap
  eventually; it just takes far too long. Say "within the 3 seconds/15 yards shown" or "not before the runner is
  20 yards downfield". Also in Fig 7 the grey path visibly touches/crosses the back's path near the X — explain
  that crossing his path at a different time is not meeting him (match the time dots, e.g. color/number the
  same-time dots on both paths).
- **Opening scene vs Fig 3 vs "Back to the huddle".** Opening: the back takes "two steps to his left", the LG
  pulls right (a counter), and the Mike "arrives in the hole at the same moment as the back and a pulling
  guard." Then: "That is the read in the chapter's opening scene, and @fig-key-pull plays it out" — but Fig 3
  is *power* from an I with the tailback going straight right, no counter step. Back to the huddle: "because his
  trip to the hole was shorter than the guard's, he got there first." Same moment or first? Counter or power?
  Make Fig 3 a counter (back's false step left) or say "a simpler version without the false step".
- **Fig 3 says the Mike beats the guard, but the "five yards vs eight yards" race** ("starting five yards off
  the ball and two gaps over, has about five") — the picture's Mike starts over the strong A gap and the hole
  is outside the right tackle; that looks like 4-5 yards lateral + 4 yards depth, i.e. 6ish. Fine to be rough,
  but the "two gaps over" phrase doesn't match the picture (A to C is two gaps; the hole is C/D outside Y?).
- **Hook "eleven or so square yards of grass"** — eleven square yards is a 3x4-yard patch; the second level in
  the diagrams is ~10 wide x 5 deep. Typo for "eleven or so yards"? It made me stop.
- **"The disappearing Sam": the chart doesn't show him disappearing.** Rendered text: base 31% in 2016, 22% in
  2023, 31% in 2025 — flat across the chart's window. The text admits the drop "happened earlier, before this
  data begins", but a reader looking at Fig 12 sees "Sam came back". Either lead the section with the 54% (2008)
  -> ~25% story using the cited numbers (a two-point annotation on the chart would do it), or retitle the chart
  "the recent rebound".
- **Play-action cost trade, good — but the Will is unexplained in Fig 11.** In both rows the Will retreats deep
  identically; is he keying the guard too? Why isn't he fooled? One clause.
- **Run-through "it wrecks zone schemes"** — and the walked-out conflict, scrape exchange etc. fine. But "a
  linebacker who runs through a gap on a counter step... is out of the play" — then when *would* a coordinator
  call run-through vs read? Answered vaguely ("against teams that run a lot of zone"). OK as-is; 05-05 may do
  more.

## 6. Knowledge gaps — what I still wonder

- Which guard each linebacker keys in a 4-3 Over / Under / nickel, and whether the Mike and Will read the same
  man.
- How a linebacker survives the draw and the screen when his guard key says "pass".
- What a linebacker actually *does* with a climbing guard he couldn't beat: take him on with which shoulder,
  how he gets off the block ("slip him" is said three times but never shown).
- Are tackle totals a good way to judge a linebacker? The chapter has the data (a LB tackle at 5-6 yards is
  arguably a defensive failure upstream) and fans quote tackle counts constantly — one paragraph would land.
- Missed-tackle rates by position: even one cited number (PFF/BDB 2024) would close the "missed" half of the
  objective.
- The 3-4 inside linebackers (05-03 just taught the 3-4): do they have different names/keys than Mike/Will?
  The chapter is entirely 4-3/nickel labelled.
- How the Sam plays in base when he *is* on the field (over the tight end, keys the TE/tackle) — the outside
  linebacker keys get one sentence.
- After the radio cuts off at 15 seconds, how does the green dot get a *new* call if the offense substitutes
  late? (Hand signals from the sideline? wristbands?)
- How a defense picks which linebacker is the green dot when it's a safety — what changes for the Mike then?
