# 07-02 Blitzing — beginner reader review

Reviewer persona: a casual NFL watcher who has never played and has read everything before this chapter in
curriculum order, including the prerequisites 07-01 (The Pass Rush) and 06-02 (Man Coverage), plus 04-02 (Pass
Protection), 02-05 and 05-02. I read the chapter start to finish in the PDF (`pdfs/07-02-blitz-and-man-pressure.pdf`,
22 pp, built one minute after the last .qmd save, so it is current). I rasterized it at 60 dpi, re-rendered every
page at 110 dpi to read the figures, and checked the source for figure coordinates.

**Overall:** strong, close to pilot quality. "Three counts and one question" is a great spine, and the blitz-math
table is the best single teaching device in Part 7 so far. The overload pair (Fig 3 strip, then Fig 4 "played by the
rules") is exactly the right move: same play, two outcomes. The mug plate (Fig 6) makes "one look, a menu of
threats" concrete. The data section's argument, "a style, not a strength", lands for an analytical reader. I could do
every "You'll be able to…" item, and I got the gist of all three drills before opening the answers. The problems
that matter:

- (a) **Drill 3's answer gets its own count wrong.** It says "five defenders in man on four receivers". Once the
  Will hugs, only four are left in coverage. The caption's "six potential blockers" is also off: with the attached
  TE it is seven.
- (b) **The chapter contradicts itself on where the hot throw goes.** "Into the space the blitzer left" (the body,
  and Answer 2: "throw to the side the free rusher would come from"). Versus "away from an unblocked rusher the
  quarterback can see coming" (Watch for it). And the Cover 0 figure's hot throw (H's out) isn't in the blitzer's
  space at all.
- (c) **The "style, not a strength" stickiness argument doesn't discriminate.** Footnote [^carry] admits that
  *overall* EPA/dropback allowed has almost no carry-over either (+0.04). So "EPA on blitzes isn't sticky" says
  nothing special about blitzing.
- (d) **The figure legend is broken by purple rings and by the hug path.** Purple rings appear in six figures with
  four different meanings and are never in the legend. The Will's hug rush in Fig 1 is drawn dashed orange, which the
  legend reserves for "dropping or rotating into coverage".
- (e) **The "man coverage leaves no hole" claim is undercut two sections later** by the nickel blitz, whose
  vacated slot receiver *is* the hot throw.
- (f) **Length.** About 9,400 words of prose against a ~5,000 target. "Widest man / longest path" appears 17 + 6
  times.

## 2. Leaps: a missing or assumed "why"

1. **Where does the hot throw go?** (Blitz math, "Where does the quarterback throw…"; Watch for it, "At the snap";
   Answer 2; Fig 2.) The chapter gives three rules that point in different directions:
   - "usually in the space the blitzer came from"
   - "away from an unblocked rusher the quarterback can see coming"
   - "plan to throw to the side the free rusher would come from" (Answer 2)

   In Fig 2 the hot throw is an outside-breaking route chosen because of *leverage*, not vacated space. I need one
   reconciling sentence. Against zone, throw where the blitzer left. Against man, throw to the matchup or the
   route that breaks away from leverage. And the "free rusher's side" and the "vacated space" are usually the same
   side because the blitzer *is* the free rusher.
2. **"In man coverage… a blitzer leaves no specific area unguarded, only one less spare player"** (Cover 1
   section). This is true only when the blitzer is the spare man. Two sections later the nickel blitz leaves his
   own man and the slot becomes "exactly the hot throw". Say explicitly that a man blitzer is either the spare
   defender (no hole) or a man defender whose receiver someone has to rotate to (a hole, briefly). That distinction
   *is* the family section.
3. **Why did the protection slide away from the overload in Fig 3?** The Will walks up at −1.6 s, which looks like
   plenty of time to reset. The answer ("walks the safety down so late…") comes two paragraphs after Fig 4. Put a
   clause in the Fig 3 caption: "the walk-up came too late to reset the slide".
4. **The opening play's resolution.**
   - "with the back stepping up to the linebacker in the A gap and **the center on the other one**". But the other
     one "turns and runs backward into coverage". So the center blocked air. That is the point, but say it: "the
     center was committed to the other mugger, who dropped, so the center blocked nobody".
   - The hook says a safety was "creeping down… toward the slot receiver". Why was the slot still open? Say the
     safety was rotating from 12 yards and couldn't get there by the third step.
   - Who "won" the center-vs-QB argument over the Mike? Unresolved.
5. **Stickiness as evidence** (Data, "Look at it a second way"). Blitz-rate carry-over is 0.55 and blitz-EPA
   carry-over is −0.10. But the footnote says overall pass-defense EPA carry-over is only +0.04. So the contrast
   shows "pass defense is noisy year to year", not "blitzing in particular is a style". An analytically minded
   reader will catch this. Either reframe ("like pass defense in general, blitz success doesn't carry over; the
   habit does"), or compare against something that *is* sticky.
6. **"Blitzes produce more pressure"** (Data, first paragraph) is asserted with no number. The only numbers given are
   sacks (8% vs 6%). 07-01 taught that pressure, not sacks, is the measure. Give a pressure rate for blitz vs
   non-blitz, or soften the claim.
7. **Scatter selection.** Fig 8's caption warns "associations, not experiments", but Fig 7 makes the causal-sounding
   claim "heavy-blitzing and light-blitzing defenses have done equally well" with no caveat. Teams with weak
   rushers or poor secondaries may blitz *because* of that. Add a sentence.
8. **Spagnuolo 2007**: "mostly with a four-man rush, blitzing **only** about a third of the time". The chapter told
   me earlier that today's league rate is about 25%, so "only a third" reads as *more* than average. Add era
   context (2007 league blitz rates were higher, if true), or drop "only".
9. **Bear pressure and the hug.** Fig 5 panel 4 draws the Will in man on the back (dotted). The back blocks the
   Mike. By the hug rule the chapter just taught in Fig 1, the Will should now rush, which makes seven against six.
   The caption says "6 rushers". Either say the Will stays in coverage (and why), or count him.
10. **Fig 1: why is the Mike's path a free run for the back?** It works, but "the back has a short trip" vs Fig 6's
    "the back must step up and stop a linebacker at full speed" sets two different expectations for the same
    assignment. One sentence on why A-gap pickup is easy when the back is *aligned* to that side and hard when he
    must cross would tie them together.

## 3. Diagrams

- **Purple rings are undefined and mean four things.** The legend ("How to read the diagrams") defines only the red
  ring. Purple marks:
  - Fig 1: the Mike, the fifth rusher.
  - Fig 3: the back who errs.
  - Fig 4: the Will, now the man the back blocks. In Fig 3 the Will was ringed *red*, so the same player changes
    colour between consecutive figures of the same play.
  - Figs 9 and 11: "potential rushers".

  Add purple to the legend as "the player to watch", or use it consistently.
- **Fig 1, the hug path.** The Will's hug rush is dashed orange. The legend says dashed orange is "dropping or
  rotating into coverage". His dotted man line to R also crosses that dashed path, right beside the SS's dotted line
  to Y. The cluster around 3/RG/Y/W/SS is the hardest spot in the chapter to decode. Use a solid or differently
  coloured "conditional rush" arrow, and move the dotted W→R link so it doesn't cross.
- **Fig 1, the DL rush arrows** are about 0.2 yd long and almost invisible. I couldn't tell at a glance that the
  four linemen rush.
- **Fig 2, inside leverage is claimed but not visible.** Every man defender is only 0.7–0.9 yd inside his receiver,
  which looks head-up at print size. The whole "throw outside" lesson rests on it. Exaggerate it to about 1.5 yd,
  or draw a small leverage tick.
- **Fig 4, the throw.** The dashed brown ball path passes straight through the Will's marker and the RG. It reads
  as the ball being thrown through a defender. The "hot slant: ball out ~1.1 s" label sits far to the left of the
  throw's end. Move the label next to the catch point.
- **Fig 5, edge pressure.** The end's slant inside and the RT going with him are the key action, but the slant
  arrow is tiny and the "E slants in, RT goes with him" label covers the area where it would show. The blind-side
  claim is contradicted (see §1).
- **Fig 5, bear pressure.** The hug omission is covered in §2.9. The label "M blitzes the C gap" is fine.
- **Fig 6, panel 1.** The Will's rush arrow, the center's block bar and the "C" label overlap. Panel 2's "R still
  looking for the M" label sits well to the right of R.
- **Fig 6, "The look".** The note says "man looks outside", but the outside receivers and corners are off frame, so
  there is nothing to see. Cut it or show it.
- **Fig 3 strip.** Good: six frames that tell the story in print. Frame 3's note, "R's eyes go to the safety", is
  not visible; the back has barely moved. "R drifts outside" would describe what the frame shows.
- **Figs 9–11 (drills).** Fig 9 rings every left-side defender, which does half the counting for me (see §5).
- **Layout.** Large blank areas sit on p6 (two-thirds empty before Fig 3), the bottom of p8, p10 (half empty),
  p16, p18 and p19. These are figure-float push-outs; worth a `fig-pos` tweak or reordering text around Figs 3, 5
  and 6.
- **Field position.** Drill 3 says "at the opponent's 30", but every Play uses `los=40` (BDB x = 40 is the offense's
  *own* 30). No yard numbers are drawn, so the reader can't see the mismatch. Still, check the yard line for the
  caption.

## 4. Drag and repetition

- **"Widest man / longest path / inside-out"** is explained in full at least five times: blitz math, the
  Misconception callout, the Fig 2 caption and text, Fig 4, edge pressure, and Back to the opening play. The word
  "widest" appears 17 times and "longest path" 6. Explain it once, then just use it.
- **"Hot" is defined twice**: in the You'll-need box and again in the body.
- **The family "Watch for it" callout** (after bear pressure) is repeated almost line for line by the Sunday
  checklist. Cut one.
- **The "How offenses answer" table** repeats things the reader already has. It's useful as a reference, but the
  paragraph before the mug's "What do offenses do?" already covers half of it.
- **History.** The Ryan/Johnson/Spagnuolo/Rex/Zimmer/Flores run is good, but it's 6 paragraphs plus a red-dog
  prologue. Rex Ryan gets one sentence with no "what problem he faced". Either give him a "why" or fold him into
  Buddy's paragraph.
- **Super Bowl film room.** The numbers are dense: 7 dropbacks, 6 Cover 0, 3 incompletions, 45 yards, 1 TD, a 7-man
  rush, 11 yards, then the 24-yard McCaffrey catch introduced only in "What to notice". Readable, but this is where
  my attention dipped.
- **Overall**: about 9,400 words against a ~5,000 target. Cutting the repetition above saves around 1,000 without
  losing an idea.

**Drill 1 (the loaded side).** My answer before opening it:

- Left side: E, 1, W (B gap) and NB, plus SS = 4–5 potential rushers.
- The slide goes left (3 blockers), and the back is on the right.
- So the left is short by one or two. Before the snap: move the back to the left, or reset the protection. After the
  snap: hot to H if the nickel comes.

**Matches the answer.** But it was easy because Fig 9 rings exactly the defenders to count. Also, the drill
overloads the *slide* side, which the chapter never discussed: Fig 3 taught that "the man side is the thin side". The
answer's "so on paper the slide side is already short" is the first time the reader meets the idea that a slide side
can be outnumbered too. Either add one sentence in the overload section, or say in the drill that this is a twist.

**Drill 2 (mug on 3rd-and-12).** My answer:

- Probably a bluff: one or both drop, 4–5 rush, since the data says blitzing on third-and-long does slightly worse
  and the droppers clog the crossers.
- QB problem: he must protect against six without knowing.

**Matches.** But Answer 2's "plan to throw to the side the free rusher would come from" surprised me, because
Watch-for-it told me to throw *away* from an unblocked rusher I can see (§2.1).

**Drill 3 (everybody down).** My answer:

- Cover 0 look: 4 DL + M + FS = 6, and the W hugs if R blocks = 7.
- Blockers: 5 OL + R = 6, or 7 if the TE stays in.
- Throw outside fast: H's out, or a fade.

**Right in substance**, but the answer confused me on two counts:

- The caption says "six potential blockers, five eligible receivers" with an attached TE *and* a back. That makes
  seven potential blockers (5 OL + Y + R). It's six only if Y releases.
- Answer 3 says "six blockers against seven rushers… **with five defenders in man on four receivers**". If seven
  rush (4 DL, M, FS, W), the remaining defenders are SS, NB and the two CBs: **four** in man on four receivers
  (X, Y, H, Z). This is an arithmetic error in a chapter about arithmetic. Fix it.

## 6. Knowledge gaps: what I still wonder

1. **What if the offense runs into a blitz?** The chapter is all pass. I'd like one paragraph: a blitz on a run
   down is a run blitz (gap-shooting), it can blow up a run or leave a huge hole, and box math changes. The mug
   section hints ("check to a run") but nothing more.
2. **Mobile quarterbacks.** If the free rusher comes and the QB just escapes, what does a blitz do about it? Spy,
   contain, rush lanes from 07-01? One sentence.
3. **How do eleven defenders know the rules?** Who calls the blitz (the coordinator via the green-dot helmet
   radio?), and how does the hug rule get communicated? The Madden box hints at it but doesn't say how the call
   arrives.
4. **Pressure rate on blitzes vs four-man rushes.** See §2.6. The number the chapter's own argument needs.
5. **Delayed blitzes.** The hug is one. Are there others, like a linebacker who waits a beat and then comes? Worth
   a line, since it's a classic broadcast moment.
6. **How does the blitzer beat the back?** Fig 6 says the back must "stop a linebacker running at full speed". Does
   the linebacker try to run around him or through him? A sentence on blitz pickup from the blitzer's side would
   close the loop with 07-01's rush moves.
7. **Can the QB tell Cover 0 from Cover 1 when both safeties are disguised?** The chapter links disguise (06-06)
   only implicitly. One sentence pointing to 07-03 and 06-06 would help: "the defense's next move is to make these
   looks lie".

## Small things

- "the deep middle stays protected… cheap in risk" (Cover 1) vs Cover 0 "gave up the most 20-yard plays (Man
  Coverage has the chart)". Fine, but give the number once here instead of sending the reader back.
- Fig 7: "went from middling to elite". MIN 2023 is 15th, so okay. Maybe give the 2024–25 ranks in the caption
  too, for those reading the figure alone.
- Fig 8: the 4th-down interval runs off the left edge of the axis (x from −0.3). Clip it, or widen to −0.4 so the
  reader sees the whole interval.
- Opening hook: "close enough to touch him" and "brought the house". Nice voice, keep them.
