# 10-02 Third Down: beginner-reader review

Reviewer role: casual fan who has never played, has read only Parts 1-9 and 10-01 in curriculum order.
Read the .qmd start to finish and looked at all 20 pages of `pdfs/10-02-third-down.pdf` (rasterized at 60 and
110 dpi). I tried the three Predict-the-play drills before opening the answers.

**Overall:** strong and readable. The "distance is information" idea, the four buckets and the
past-the-sticks chart all land, and I came away able to bucket a third down and explain the sticks route.
The main problems are a few **internal contradictions** a careful beginner will notice (Cover 0 short vs
long; the "at the line" dip vs Welker; blitz conversion rates), **one unexplained statistic** (correlation),
**one hook question that is never answered**, and **four diagram defects** (one label collision that hides a
player, two line-through-label cases, and a strip with no depth scale).

## 2. Leaps, and contradictions I couldn't resolve

1. **Is Cover 0 a short-yardage call or a long-yardage call?** The Cover 0 section says "It is above all a
   **short-yardage** call", but its own reason 3 argues the opposite: "on third-and-2 that can still convert…
   but on third-and-6 the routes fast enough to beat the rush are mostly short of the line." That is an
   argument for Cover 0 at *longer* distances. Then the Spagnuolo film room says Cover 0 is "kept for the down
   when a quick throw short of the line doesn't hurt", which also means longer distances. As a reader, I
   genuinely don't know which it is. The chapter needs one sentence reconciling the league habit (short
   yardage, for run defense and because the short completion converts anyway) with the KC/Flores habit
   (longer distances, where the hot throw lands short).
2. **Cover 0 reason 1 is mis-titled.** The heading "The cost of a missed tackle is lower" doesn't match the
   explanation, which is about the *cost of a completion* being low because short completions convert anyway.
   A missed tackle in Cover 0 costs a touchdown at any distance.
3. **"At the line" is the worst place to throw, but Welker lived there.** The past-the-sticks section
   says throws aimed right at the line show a dip, and reason 2 says "A ball caught right on the line is caught
   in front of a defender who is already there." Then the Patriots film room praises Welker, whose "typical
   target was thrown **exactly to the line to gain**" and converted 48%. The parenthetical about air yards
   doesn't resolve this. I need to be told why Welker beat the dip: the route found the gap between the
   sticks-sitting defenders, or Brady threw it before the defender could close. Otherwise the two sections
   contradict each other.
4. **Hedging on the dip isn't consistent.** The chart text says the dip "is very likely, at least in part,
   the sticks-sitting defender". The Sticks-defense section then says flatly: "This is the reason for the
   dip." Keep the hedge in both places.
5. **Offenses convert *more* against the blitz?** The Rush-three section reports third-and-7+ conversion
   at "21% against three or fewer rushers, 24% against four and **31% against five or more**". It caveats only
   the three-man number. A beginner immediately asks why defenses blitz most at 7-10 if blitzing gets
   converted on most often. That needs a sentence: it's the same distance confound, and/or the blitz's payoff
   is sacks and drive-ending negative plays rather than conversion rate.
6. **Medium bucket vs the data.** The Medium paragraph says the defense "plays tight man coverage and
   **blitzes** to force the ball out". The defense chart then shows the blitz rate at 4-6 (27%) is the *lowest*
   of the first three buckets, and the pressure section says "At 4 to 6, blitzing is more dangerous." Tone
   down the Medium paragraph to match.
7. **Sticks defense, the "cures" are mismatched.** "The cure for the first problem is pressure; for the
   second, a fast defense; for the third, there is no scheme." The problems were (1) everything short is open,
   (2) the quarterback gets time, (3) tackling. Pressure cures *time* (problem 2), and a fast, rallying
   defense cures *everything short* (problem 1). As written, the first two are swapped.
8. **Sticks route, reason 3 (the spot).** "A receiver who catches the ball a yard past the line can be driven
   backward and still get the spot past it; one who catches it on the line needs every inch." If forward
   progress protects the receiver, the one who catches it on the line is protected too. What's the real
   point? Is it that the ball, not the feet, gets spotted, so a catch with the ball short of the line fails?
   Or that the spot is an eyeball judgment? Rewrite or drop this one; reasons 1 and 2 are enough.
9. **Predict 3's answer sends the throw toward the spy.** "Where to throw: the middle of the field at 6 to 8
   yards, where the robber would have been." But the spy (M) is standing *in the middle at 5.5 yards*,
   directly between the quarterback and that window. I need the "why": the spy's eyes are on the quarterback,
   not the receiver, and he won't drop. Otherwise the answer looks like throwing at a defender. One sentence
   would fix it.
10. **The hook asks a question the chapter never answers.** "The quarterback looks to the sideline, then
    **points at a safety and shouts**." Nothing explains it: calling out the protection, identifying the
    possible blitzer. Either answer it in the pressure section or cut it from the hook. The hook also says
    "five defenders rush", but the figure that answers the hook (Cover 1, third-and-7) rushes four.
11. **"Two price lists"** ("Read the chart as two price lists laid on top of each other") is a metaphor
    with no payoff; I didn't know what the "price" was. Plain "two sets of bars, runs and dropbacks" is
    clearer.

## 5. Can I do the "You'll be able to…" items?

- **Bucket a third down with rates, by run and pass: yes.** Short 60%, medium 44%, long 31%, very long 15%.
  Runs beat passes at 1-2 yards and lose from 4 on. Easy to remember.
- **Predict past-the-line, and when short is right: yes.** The four exceptions are concrete and memorable.
- **Name the offense's tools and their distances: mostly.** It's scattered across sections. A small
  table (distance → offense tool → defense call) would make it stick. Right now I had to assemble it from the
  Watch-for-it bullets.
- **Recognise the package by personnel, then name the call: partly.** I can count DBs and spot "nobody
  deep", which gives Cover 0, and a lone linebacker staring at the quarterback, which gives a spy. But I
  can't tell a sticks defense, a simulated pressure or a blitz apart *before the snap*. The chapter never
  shows the pre-snap picture of a sticks defense (Fig. 6 shows it after the drops), and it says, correctly,
  that good defenses disguise it. So the skill as written ("name the call it is most likely to play") really
  rests on *distance*, not personnel. Either add a pre-snap tell for each family or reword the objective.
- **Read the conversion chart and explain why third down is less predictable than it feels: half.** I can
  read the chart. I can't fully evaluate the "noisy" argument because correlation isn't explained (§1).

**Predict-the-play, attempted cold:**

1. **3rd & 2, nobody deep.** My answer: Cover 0 blitz; the offense wants a slant or quick man-beater, or a
   run. **Correct.** The title "nobody deep" and the caption "Nobody is deeper than that" nearly give it
   away; consider dropping the title phrase so the reader has to count.
2. **3rd & 13, everybody back.** My answer: three rush plus B as a swing man, drop to the sticks; call a draw
   or screen. **Correct, but only because the picture is nearly identical to Fig. 7**: the same E-N-E-B front
   and the same lone M. This drill tests recall, not transfer. Vary the look: show four down with one rusher
   who drops, or two-high instead of the Fig. 7 shell.
3. **3rd & 5, running QB.** My answer: spy and Cover 1, correct. For the throw I guessed "option route
   breaking *outside*, away from the spy", the opposite of the answer's "middle at 6-8". The answer
   didn't convince me (see §2.9). This is the most valuable of the three drills, but its answer needs the
   "why".

## 6. What I still wonder (and think this chapter should answer)

- **What was the quarterback pointing at in the hook?** That is, how an offense identifies the blitz and sets
  protection on third down: sliding, keeping the back in, "hot" versus "sight" adjustments.
- **Penalties on third down.** The footnote admits first downs by penalty are excluded. But defensive
  holding and pass interference on third-and-long are a huge part of how drives survive, and a fan sees it
  every week. One paragraph on why defenses that play man on third down risk it would help.
- **Scrambles.** The spy section implies quarterback scrambles are a major third-down weapon. How often do
  third downs get converted by scrambles? One number would anchor the spy section.
- **Offense personnel and formation on third down.** The chapter covers the defense's package well, but
  offenses going to empty backfields or 10/11 personnel to see the blitz is missing. So is the counter of
  staying in heavy personnel to punish dime (hinted at by the draw).
- **Does the blitz actually work on third-and-long?** (§2.5.) After this chapter I'd guess it doesn't, which
  is presumably not the intended takeaway.
- **The arms race at the sticks.** If offenses set routes at 8 on third-and-7 and defenses sit at the sticks,
  what does each side do next (sitting at 9, routes at 10, back-shoulder throws)? The chapter's "spine" claim
  ("the offense's gains and the defense's answers have stayed roughly matched") would be more vivid with
  one example of the move and counter-move.
