# 05-01 beginner-reader review

Reviewer role: a casual NFL watcher who has never played and has read Parts 1–4 (01-01 to 04-08) in order.
Read start to finish from `pdfs/05-01-dl-techniques-and-gap-control.pdf` (20 pp., rendered after the last
qmd edit), with figures checked at 130 dpi.

**Overall:** strong. The opening hook ("one wins by *going*, the other by *staying*") is exactly the
question the chapter answers. The three-jobs list, "fight pressure with pressure", and "read the blocker's
head" are the kind of rules I can use on Sunday. The problems are below, most serious first. The biggest
is that **the two-gap figure contradicts the two-gap argument** (item 2.1 / 3.5).

## 2. Leaps: a missing or assumed "why"

**2.1 The two-gap linebackers are "clean" only if the arithmetic adds up, and it doesn't.** "Why it exists"
(after Fig 5): "the two inside linebackers … run to the ball, unblocked, because the big men in front of them
are occupying all five offensive linemen." Three linemen can't occupy five blockers. Fig 5's own caption
says "The guards are uncovered and will climb to the inside linebackers", and frames 3–4 show LG and RG
sitting *on* the Mike and the Will. So the picture shows exactly the opposite of the claim. The missing step
is that the nose and the 4s have to be good enough that a guard *must* help (double the nose, or help on a 4),
or the run goes through them. Only then are the backers clean. State it. Ideally make the two-gap strip show
a guard coming to double the nose while a linebacker runs free.

**2.2 How can a shaded player two-gap?** I was taught that head-up = two-gap and shaded = one-gap ("that is
what an odd number or an 'i' usually signals"). Then the Drill 1 answer says "many 4-3 teams ask a
1-technique to play both A gaps", and the "Why it exists" paragraph on two-high defenses says "ask a big nose
to play both A gaps". The mechanics of a shaded two-gapper (he's already in one gap, so how does he cover the
other?) are never given.

**2.3 Is the 4i a one-gapper or a two-gapper?** The 4i/5 section asks for long men who "lock out an
offensive tackle at arm's length and still see past him to the ball", which is two-gap language. The 4i is
also described as "in the B gap, like a 3", which is one-gap language. Fig 1's label says "owns B or C".
Which is it, and does it depend on the call? One sentence would settle it.

**2.4 Reach block: penetrate or step with him?** Fig 4 shows the one-gap 3 beating a reach by *shooting
the gap*. Fig 6 panel 2 and "Against a reach" say the answer is to "step with the blocker, keep his outside
arm free, and press him back", and to stay on the man. Are these the one-gap and two-gap answers, or the
same answer at different moments? As written, I can't tell which one the 3-technique should do.

**2.5 "If nobody blocks you, something is coming" vs. the read option.** The rule is "close inside and get
small at once". But 03-04 taught me that the read option *deliberately* leaves the end unblocked, and an end
who crashes inside hands the quarterback the keep. That's also the bootleg the contain section warns about.
The chapter's own rules collide, and it never says how a lineman knows which one applies (who has the QB,
the call, a scrape exchange). At minimum, add a sentence and a link to 03-04.

**2.6 Slant toward the run: does the defense win or not?** "What it costs" says "Slant toward the run and
the defense arrives at the hole before the blockers do". The next sentences say zone blocking "was built to
absorb" movement. Drill 2 is a slant toward an inside-zone run, so I didn't know which rule wins. The answer
says the play side is crowded *and* the back cuts back, which fits the second rule better than the first.
Reconcile them in the body ("against gap schemes …; against zone …").

**2.7 Read the blocker's head: whose left?** The legend says "*Left* and *right* always mean the offense's
left and right." The head-read paragraph switches to the defender's view: "If the center's head slides to
*your* left". The Fig 5 caption says "the nose throws the center to *his* left". In frame 3 the center ends
up on the offense's left (page left), which is the nose's *right*. Pick one frame of reference. I had to
redraw it on paper.

**2.8 Where the 3-technique lines up, and why.** The film room ("He moves to whichever side the defense
wants its 3-technique") and Watch-for-it ("note which side the 3-technique is on") both depend on knowing
*how* a defense picks his side: to the tight end, to the field, to the formation's strength? The chapter
gives no rule. Add one sentence and a forward link to 05-02 (Over/Under).

**2.9 How does the lineman know the call?** One-gap or two-gap, slant or angle, box or spill: "the
defense's call decides". Who calls it, when, and can I see it before the snap? 01-06 taught stance tells
(heavy hand). The Joe Greene story is literally a tilted stance. Neither is connected to spotting a slant or
angle *before* the snap.

## 3. Diagrams

**3.1 Fig 1 (technique map).** The silhouettes look almost identical at print size. Wilfork is a hair
wider, but "the nose outweighs the edge rusher by about 70 pounds" is not *visible*, and that comparison is
the figure's whole job. Exaggerate the width scaling, or put the weight in large type under each figure.
The caption also never says why the 5 and 4i sit on the left tackle and everything else on the right side.

**3.2 Fig 2 (wide-9).** The w9's rush arrow is no wider than the 5's (the two arcs look mirror-symmetric),
so "the widest possible arc" isn't shown. The lane label ("the run lane he leaves: the Sam must fill it")
floats top-right, away from the shaded box. The comparison isn't controlled: the 5 has no tight end on his
side and the w9 does. To make the point, shade the 5's side of the line as well, showing the narrow lane next
to him.

**3.3 Fig 3 (gap accounting).** Clear and the best figure in the chapter, apart from the B/B collision
(1.6).

**3.4 Fig 4 (one-gap strip).** The text says the guard is "stepping sideways to reach him". In frames 2–4
the RG moves almost straight **backward**, ending more than a yard deep in the backfield. The LT and LG
trails drift back and *left*, although outside zone has "every lineman step to the play side". I can't see a
reach in this picture, only a line that collapsed. In frame 2 the ring around the 3 also covers the RT's
label.

**3.5 Fig 5 (two-gap strip).** (a) The guards end up on the linebackers (see 2.1). (b) The only visible
difference between "ball right" and "ball left" is where the Q is standing. The R is directly under the
nose in both frames, so "commits to the right A gap" vs "the left A gap" is a few pixels. (c) The caption
says "his left" (see 2.7). (d) The strike/lock-out is the key technique, but nothing shows the hands or the
"yard off his body". A tiny arm-bar mark would help.

**3.6 Fig 6 (block reactions).** Panel 1: only the 5 gets a response arrow. The 3, whose reaction the
caption describes first ("pushing back outward"), has none, and the puller the 5 is looking for isn't drawn.
Panel 2: the C does nothing, and there is no "wanted" path (dashed) against the actual path, so "the runner
has to go even wider" isn't shown. Panel 3 is good.

**3.7 Fig 7 (edge/contain).** Readable. In the right panel the short "crashes?" arrow is easy to miss, and
the block bars on the OL are unexplained. Should I care what they do?

**3.8 Fig 8 (slant/angle).** The caption says "The linebackers replace them (dashed)", but the linebacker
arrows are drawn **solid**. In the right panel, the "45°, still upfield" label sits beside the **S**, so it
reads as describing the Sam rather than the 3's arrow.

**3.9 Go deeper table (p. 19).** The pandas index column ("0", "1") prints in the PDF, and the "defender"
header is misaligned with its column.

**3.10 Drill figures.** Drill 3 shows an R (see 1.10). The title of Drill 2, "inside zone right meets a
slant right", is fine.

## 4. Drag / repetition

- **The 2011 Eagles stats appear twice, nearly verbatim**: in the wide-9 history paragraph ("tied for the
  league lead with 50 sacks, and Jason Babin … had 18") and again in the Eagles film room. Cut one.
- **The wide-9 history paragraph is heavy.** Washburn's tenure years, Schwartz's three jobs, Super Bowl LII,
  Garrett's DPOYs and sack record, plus a parenthetical caveat. That's a lot of names and dates for one
  idea. Keep Washburn's quote and one sentence on Schwartz/Garrett.
- **The 3-technique and 4i paragraphs largely repeat 03-01's "numbers that became position names".**
  Compare 03-01's "hard for the tackle to get outside of … muddles which of those two should block him" with
  05-01's "The tackle can't easily reach him … ties up two blockers' decisions". A spiral is fine, but say
  "you met this in 03-01; here's what's new" and lead with the new part (body type, one-gap vs two-gap).
- **Reggie White paragraph:** tacked on after four film rooms, with no concrete what-to-notice. It is the
  fifth example in a row, and by then I was skimming.
- **Joe Greene history:** a good story, but "23 times in 60 snaps" belongs in the footnote, and O.J.
  Simpson's 49 yards is detail I won't keep.
- **Nose = heaviest / two-gappers need rare bodies** comes up in the nose section, the two-gap costs, the
  misconception box, the Drill 1 answer and the takeaways. One or two of these could go.

## 5. Can I do the "You'll be able to…" items? (drills attempted before opening the answers)

1. **Alignment → job + body:** *Mostly yes.* I can name the nose/3/5/wide-9 jobs. On a broadcast I would
   still struggle to tell a 3 from a 4i from a 5 (about a yard apart), and a 9 from a wide-9 with no tight
   end (1.7). The chapter gives no TV-angle advice for this.
2. **One-gap vs two-gap by first step:** *Partly.* "Into the gap vs into the man" is a usable test. But the
   misconception box and the Drill 1 "catch" say alignment can mislead, and 2.2/2.3 leave me unsure about
   shaded two-gappers and the 4i.
3. **Down block / reach / double team:** *Yes for down block and double team. Shaky on reach* (2.4).
4. **Edge and contain:** *Yes.* This is the clearest section.
5. **Spot a slant or angle and say where the defense is vulnerable:** *Yes in a diagram.* Live, I'd need
   the pre-snap tell question (2.9) answered.

**Drill 1 (one-gap or two-gap?).** My answer: A is two-gap (head-up 0-4-4, big bodies, Mike and Will
free). B is one-gap (shaded 5-1-3-9, quick 3-tech, everyone has a gap). **Correct, but too easy:** the two
fronts are *identical* to the two halves of Fig 3, which I'd just studied with gap ownership drawn in. This
tests recall, not transfer. Use different fronts: a 4-3 with a 2i or a head-up 1; a tite 4i-0-4i; or a
shaded front with a 340-pound nose, so the reader has to weigh alignment against body type.

**Drill 2 (slant meets inside zone).** My answer: "Slant toward the run, so the defense gets there first
and the run is stuffed. If not, the back cuts back left, and the Will has to be there." I hedged because of
2.6. **Half right.** I got the Will and the cutback, but my first instinct came from the body text and was
wrong. Also: after a slant right, the left C gap seems uncovered (the 5 slanted into the B). The answer
doesn't say who has it.

**Drill 3 (who has contain?).** My answer: the two ends; rush outside the tackles but not past the QB's
depth; the QB escapes between the end and the DT, most likely on the wide-9 side. **Fully correct**, and
the answer added a useful idea ("a box: two sides that must hold, a front that must squeeze"). I was
confused by the R in an "empty-looking" backfield.

## 6. What I still wonder (should this chapter answer it?)

- How does a two-gap 3-4 actually keep the linebackers clean when the guards are uncovered? (2.1, must fix)
- How does a lineman tell the trap ("nobody blocked you") apart from the read option or a bootleg, where
  being unblocked means "stay home"? (2.5)
- How does a defense choose the 3-technique's side? What's the rule I can apply pre-snap? (2.8)
- Can I see a slant coming? Stance, weight, a lineman cheating his alignment, a linebacker's call? (2.9)
- Where do the 2i, 6 and 7 from 03-01's chart fit? Are they just rare, and why?
- What happens to these jobs against 0- or 2-tight-end formations (fewer or more gaps)? Fig 3 only covers
  one tight end.
- What does the two-gap nose do on a pass? Does he rush at all, or just push the pocket? "Often leave the
  field on third down" is all I get.
- "Teams pay a one-gap 3-technique more than almost anyone else": one real number (a cap hit or a
  positional-market rank) would make that land for a numbers person.
- The hook asks which of the two great linemen you're watching. The chapter never returns to the hook to
  answer it (03-01 had a "Back to the replay" section, which worked well).
