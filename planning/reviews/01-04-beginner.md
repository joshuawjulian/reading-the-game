# 01-04 What a Play Really Is: beginner-reader review

Reviewer role: a casual NFL watcher who has never played and thinks of a play as a Madden card. I had read only
01-01 (field, scoring, clock, timeouts, play clock), 01-02 (downs, LOS, EP/EPA, success rate, drive, red zone) and
01-03 (positions: QB/RB/FB/WR/TE/OL, DT/DE/edge, Mike/Will/Sam, CB, slot corner/nickel back, FS/SS, eligible
receivers, alignment vs assignment). Source: `chapters/01-foundations/01-04-what-a-play-really-is.qmd` (line
numbers below are qmd lines) and `pdfs/01-04-what-a-play-really-is.pdf` (19 pages, read at 110 and 200 dpi).
Length: about 9,700 words in the PDF including footnotes. The spec target is about 5,500.

---

## 0. The three biggest problems

1. **The receiver letters X, Y, Z and H are never explained, and every field diagram depends on them.** The
   letters first appear in the tag "X Dig" (l.402, 444: "the X receiver, alone on the left"). From then on Figures
   7–12 and all the prose say Z, H and Y. The only decoding comes in passing: "the tight end (Y)" at l.907 and "the
   slot receiver (H)" at l.907. The reader has to work out Z alone. The defensive labels (E, T, W, M, C, FS, SS,
   **$**, L) have no key anywhere. I had no idea "$" was the slot corner until the prose said "the outlined slot
   defender". The curriculum gives X/Y/Z/H to 02-02, so this chapter needs a one-line gloss plus a label key under
   the first field diagram.
2. **In the PDF, the Predict-the-play answers print open right under each question** (pp. 16–18). I could not
   test myself because the answer was in view before I had finished reading the question. Drill 2 also gives its
   answer away inside the picture (see §5).
3. **The sequence of "both coaches call blind" never adds up.** Here is what the chapter says, in order:
   (a) both sides choose "at the same moment, without seeing each other's choice" (l.124, 785–789);
   (b) the defensive caller "waits a beat to see which players the offense is sending on" (l.595), so he chooses
   *after* the offense;
   (c) the offense's caller knows "which players the defense has on the field" (l.786), but if the defense
   substitutes after seeing the offense, how does he know that when he calls?
   (d) the no-huddle lets the offense "look at the defense, and then choose or change the play while the radio is
   still on" (l.699–700), which breaks the blind call and is exactly what the 15-second cut-off is said to prevent
   (l.612–619).
   Nobody tells me who sends the offensive substitutes on, when they go on relative to the call, or how the defense
   can see them by :34 (Fig. 4 has "sees subs" running from :40 to :34, *while* the offense is still picking its
   call). I finished the chapter unsure whether the calls really are simultaneous.

## 2. Leaps (a missing "why")

- **"Note the order: the more players a word affects, the earlier it comes" (l.449).** The example contradicts
  this. Motion ("Zip") affects one player and comes *before* protection (six players). As written, the rule is
  false on its own example.
- **"if the play is a pass" (Fig 3, l.436).** The call already says "Stick", which is a pass concept. Why would the
  protection be conditional? Do run calls carry a protection number? The chapter never shows how a *run* call is
  built, so I can't split "I Rt 26 Power" from the call sheet into parts, even though the objectives say I should.
- **Erhardt-Perkins vs Shanahan verbiage (l.470–481).** EP is described as "a whole pass concept is a single word".
  But in the chapter's own example the concept is already one word ("Stick"). So what is actually different? I
  can't tell the two traditions apart from this text. A side-by-side of the same play in both styles would fix it.
- **Combinatorial maths (l.459–461).** 20 × 10 × 3 × 30 = 18,000 before tags, which is "tens of thousands", not
  "thousands". Analytically minded readers will check.
- **Openers vs situational boxes (l.379–384).** If the first 15 plays are called "more or less in order whatever
  happens", what happens when opener #3 comes up on 3rd & 9? Does the script yield to the situation? The two ideas
  sit side by side without being reconciled.
- **Why the radio cut-off makes snaps come late (l.617–619).** "That's also why the snap tends to come late" does
  not follow from the cut-off. The real reason (the QB and the offense want to see the defense commit) needs
  stating. Also: if the QB reaches the line with 12 seconds left, why not snap at once and catch the defense
  unset?
- **No-huddle vs the cut-off.** The cut-off exists so the coach can't "watch the defense line up and tell the
  quarterback exactly what to do" (l.613–616). The no-huddle then lets the coach do almost exactly that before :15
  (l.699–700, Commanders film room). This is the most interesting idea in the chapter, and it is left unconnected.
  Why doesn't everyone do it?
- **"A run into a defense that sent extra players toward the line… a long pass against two deep safeties" (l.793–
  794).** Why are these bad matches? One clause each would do ("more defenders near the line than blockers";
  "deep safeties are there to stop long passes").
- **Burning a timeout vs taking 5 yards (l.626–628).** Why is a timeout worth less than five yards? Name the trade.
- **Deliberate delay of game "to give a punter… more room" (l.623–624).** Why would five *worse* yards help a
  punter? (To avoid kicking into the end zone? To get more room for a kick?) Unexplained.
- **The read on Stick (l.907–917).** "He can't guard both receivers" ignores the linebacker. In every diagram M
  stands about 5 yards deep, right inside the spot where Y sits. Why isn't M covering Y? The answer is the zone
  split (M's area is inside, the slot defender owns the space outside), but it isn't given. Also, "if he *chases* H"
  sounds like man coverage, while the same play is later called a zone defense (l.945). Is the slot defender
  chasing a man or guarding an area?
- **Six blockers (l.450, Fig 9).** 01-03 taught me that tight ends block. Y is on the line right next to the right
  tackle. Why isn't he one of the protectors on this play?
- **1944 → messenger guards → radio ban → 1994 radio → 2008 defense (l.297–313).** If coaching from the bench was
  legal from 1944, why did Brown need messenger guards? Why did the radio take until 1994, and why did the defense
  wait 14 more years? Why did quarterbacks stop calling their own plays (l.315)? Each step is a fact with no why.
- **Why only one green-dot helmet per side (l.672)?** No reason is given (fairness? interference?).
- **Lions film room (l.1084–1086).** It says to watch for changes in "fourth-down aggressiveness" as a play-caller's
  fingerprint, but the chapter says fourth downs are the *head coach's* decision (l.213), and Campbell was head
  coach before and after the switch. So that fingerprint can't come from this change. Also, "nothing about the
  Lions' players changed" is asserted with no evidence of what *did* change. A before/after chart (pass rate on
  early downs, pace) from nflverse would turn this into a real film room. As it stands it is an anecdote.
- **"On some runs, it decides whether the quarterback hands the ball off at all" (l.1061).** This is the option,
  with no forward link (03-04).

## 3. Diagrams

**Fig 1 (staff map, p.2).** Mostly readable. Problems:
(a) "Any of the three *outlined* boxes" is ambiguous, because every box has an outline. Say "green-outlined".
(b) The dashed connector from defensive position coaches to "Analytics / game management" reads as "analytics
reports to the DB coaches". The QC connector likewise implies QC reports to offensive position coaches, while the
text says QC reports to the coordinators.
(c) The green outline and the dashed "varies" outline on the same boxes take a moment to separate.

**Fig 2 (call sheet, p.4).** The 5.6-pt monospace is at the edge of legibility in print. The caption explains the
idea well. My vocabulary concern is in §1.

**Fig 3 (anatomy, p.5).** Clear. But the "three receivers" vs tight-end question applies here, and so does the
"if the play is a pass" question.

**Fig 4 (play-clock strip, p.7).** This is a full page of four nearly identical swimlanes, and one annotated panel
would teach the same thing (drag). Specifics:
(a) The prose says the huddle runs ":30 to roughly :18" (l.599); the figure draws it from :26 to :19.
(b) The defensive lane has a blank from :20 to :13, and the final "adjusts" is never explained.
(c) "sees subs" starts at :40, while the offense is still choosing. This is the sequencing confusion from §0.3.
(d) "set · read" uses "read" for a pre-snap look.

**Fig 5 (communication flow, p.8).** This one is good. "No-huddle *backup*" is wrong, though: for a no-huddle team,
signals are the main channel. The defensive "huddle or line call" left me unsure whether defenses huddle at all,
and the text never says so directly.

**Fig 6 (no-huddle bars, p.10).** Clear. The dashed "league: 8%" line clashes with the prose ("only a few percent",
l.765). Most bars sit near 3–5%, and Washington pulls the mean up. Say so, or plot the median.

**Fig 7 (Madden vs coach, p.12).** This is the key figure, and I could only half decode it:
(a) There is no key for X/Y/Z/H or for the defensive letters, and "$" means nothing to me.
(b) Pre-snap motion (Z's arrow inward) and post-snap routes are drawn in the same style, so I couldn't tell
"before the snap" from "after".
(c) The orange line (line to gain from 01-02) and the green dashed line from Q to the slot defender (the read)
aren't in any legend.
(d) The offensive linemen's angled block marks aren't explained in the caption. "Slide left" only appears four
paragraphs later.
(e) The rotated yard numbers made me think the field was sideways.
(f) The coach's diagram still draws every route as a fixed line. Only one branch (Y's dotted line) shows the "rules"
idea. A second visible branch for H or the back would make the contrast land.
(g) The label "turn in vs man" vs the prose "keep moving away from him" (l.921): which is it?

**Fig 8 (frame strip, p.13).**
(a) "-1.2s" isn't explained as "before the snap" until the end of the caption.
(b) Between "snap" and "+0.9s" the slot defender mostly moves *down toward the line*. "Widening with H" is hard to
see.
(c) At +0.9 and +1.8 the defensive linemen's boxes overlap or sit *behind* the offensive line, so it looks like
the rushers have beaten the blockers.
(d) There are no yard numbers or line to gain, so I lost the sense of depth (six yards for Y).

**Fig 9 (four jobs, p.14).** Panels 1–3 work.
(a) Panel 2 shows Z with no motion, aligned wide. That is not "the same snap" as Figs 7–8.
(b) Panel 4 says "seven drop into zones", but no zones are drawn, so I can't see who owns which area, and that is
the thing I needed for the read. Shaded zone areas would answer my M-vs-Y question.
(c) In panel 1, the green highlight rings cover the block marks.

**Fig 10 (drill 1).** Clear.

**Fig 11 (drill 2).** The inward arrow on the slot defender *shows* the answer. See §5.

**Fig 12 (drill 3).** I counted 12, and that worked. But Y is aligned off the line as a slot receiver, which
contradicts "Y = tight end" from earlier. "L" is undecoded. The sideline he is "leaving toward" isn't marked.

## 5. Could I do the "You'll be able to…" items? (and the drills, done before reading the answers)

**Drills (attempted before opening the answers, simulating the HTML; in the PDF the answers were already visible).**

- **Drill 1 (12 seconds left):** My answer was "audible, or call a timeout". **Mostly right.** I didn't think of
  "snap it as called" as an *option*. The fact that a timeout brings the radio back appears only in the answer and
  is never taught in the body.
- **Drill 2 (slot defender sits):** My answer was "throw to H". **Right, but trivially so.** The body already said
  "If he stays inside on Y, H is open in the flat" (l.911), and the picture draws the defender's inward step with
  an arrow. This tests whether I can reread a sentence, not whether I can predict. Better: show the *pre-snap*
  picture plus the defender's alignment and leverage, and ask which receiver will likely be open and what would
  change it. Or ask what the QB does if *two* defenders are there, which the answer only mentions in parentheses.
- **Drill 3 (twelve defenders):** My answer was "snap it quickly?", a guess from the "freeze the defense" bullet.
  I **could not justify it**: the body never says that 12 men on the field is a penalty, never mentions a "free
  play", and never mentions accepting or declining penalties, the dead-ball foul, or the umpire holding the ball.
  The drill tests rules the chapter doesn't teach. Either teach the substitution and 12-men rule in the no-huddle
  section, or make the drill about something taught.

**Objectives.**

1. *Trace the chain:* **Yes.**
2. *Staff and who calls, why the booth:* **Yes.**
3. *Split a call into formation, motion, protection, concept and tags:* **Only for the one worked example.** I
   could not split any other line on the call sheet (e.g. "I Rt 26 Power", "Ace Lt Jet 18 Sweep"), and I don't
   know what a *run* call's parts are. I'd need one more worked call, ideally a run, or a short exercise.
4. *40-second clock, :15 cut-off, delay of game:* **Yes.** I have a small doubt about when the 25-second clock
   applies; "certain stoppages" is vague, but fine for now.
5. *Blind, simultaneous calling:* **Partly.** I can repeat it, but the sequencing contradictions in §0.3 mean I
   don't believe I understand it.
6. *Watch the 3–7 seconds as eleven jobs:* **On the diagrams, yes. On TV, no.** The chapter itself says the
   broadcast angle hides the safeties and the shape of the defense (l.258–260), so how do I watch eleven jobs on a
   broadcast? Name the tool: All-22 / coaches' film, end-zone replays, or "watch one group per replay". Also,
   "3–7 seconds" is never justified, since the example's ball is out in about 1.2 s.

The **Watch-for-it** item "is the quarterback changing the play at the line on those snaps?" asks me to spot
audibles, which the chapter defers to 01-05. Say "guess" or move it.

## 6. What I still wondered afterwards

- Who decides the offense's **personnel**, how the substitutes know to go in, and when that happens relative to
  the play call. This is the root of the "blind" confusion.
- Can the quarterback **talk back** to the coach? What happens if a radio **fails** mid-game (is there an equity
  rule)? Do coaches talk to the QB between drives, or use tablets?
- What was the QB in the hook **pointing at the linebacker** for? The hook promises "This chapter answers all of
  it", but the pointing, and what he was shouting, go to 01-05. Soften the promise or answer in one line.
- **Do defenses huddle?** How do all eleven defenders get the call: a huddle, the green dot shouting, or signals?
- Does a **run play** also work as "rules, not paths"? All the after-snap material is one pass play. One sentence
  and one small diagram about a run (blockers block areas, the back reads a block) would complete the idea.
- How **big** is a real call sheet, game plan or playbook (number of plays)? The illustrative sheet has about 30.
- Can the head coach **overrule** the coordinator's call mid-drive?
- Why do **college** sidelines use picture boards while NFL ones don't? Is it about radios?
- What does the **X dig** actually do on this play? "Someone is open somewhere else" is never shown.
- How often does the **read** actually change the target? Something like "the ball goes to the first read about
  X% of the time" would suit this reader, but nothing quantifies the "family of outcomes".

   "win-probability… introduced in 01-02" claim.
6. Explain the Stick read against the full zone picture: why M isn't on Y, and why "chase" is the wrong word in

   one or two panels.
