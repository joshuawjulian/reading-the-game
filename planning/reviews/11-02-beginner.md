# Beginner review: 11-02 1978 and the Passing Revolution (1978–1999)

Reviewer role: casual NFL watcher, never played, has read Parts 1–10 and 11-01 in order. I read the
.qmd start to finish and looked at all 22 pages of `pdfs/11-02-the-1978-liberation.pdf`, rasterized at
60 dpi, with pages 7, 9, 10, 11, 13 and 20 also at 110 dpi. I tried the drills before opening the
answers.

**Overall:** this is a strong chapter. The opening question pays off, the four-panel league chart is
the best thing in it, and the "what did this take away, and from whom?" thread holds the
twenty-year story together. The problems are concentrated in four places. Two diagrams draw
something different from what the text says (the Walsh slant-flat and frame 4 of the 46 counter
strip). One central causal claim contradicts the chapter's own five-yard rule (the West Coast
section). One drill's picture contradicts its setup (drill 2's down marker). The business section
also has several unexplained leaps.

---

## 1. Terms used before they are explained, or never explained

Almost everything is covered by earlier chapters, and the "You'll need" box does a good job. These
gaps remain:

- **"accrued seasons"** (Free agency (1993): "Today a player with four accrued seasons becomes an
  unrestricted free agent"). This is never defined. It appears in the glossary entry too.
- **"antitrust laws"** ("found that Plan B violated the antitrust laws"). For a non-lawyer, the
  chapter needs one clause saying *why* a rule about player movement is an antitrust problem
  (32 employers agreeing not to compete for workers).
- **"hard cap" / prorated bonuses** ("signing bonuses are spread across the years of a contract so
  they can't be hidden"). Hidden from what, and how? See the leaps section.
- **"deferred … with interest"** in the Denver paragraph. Why deferring pay "got around the cap" is
  never said.
- **"squat in the flats"** (fig-coryell caption). "Squat" is unexplained jargon. The corners' drop
  arrows also point *backwards* toward the line, which makes it more confusing (see section 3).
- **"Oklahoma 5-2"** ("teams that had learned the Oklahoma 5-2 in college stood up its ends").
  05-03 does cover it, but this sentence gives no gloss or link, and a reader arriving from search
  is lost. Add "(a five-man line with two linebackers)".
- **"Killer B's"** and **"No-Name Defense"** are dropped in as names with no content. They are
  harmless, but they read like trivia the reader is expected to know.
- **"chucking rules"** in the 1994 package ("modified line play, the chucking rules and the
  roughing-the-passer rule"). What changed about chucking in 1994? This chapter is about the chuck
  rule, so a reader will want to know.
- **"neutral zone"**: glossed only by its infraction. It's fine, because 09-03 covers it.
- **K-Gun = shotgun?** The text never says the K-Gun was run from the shotgun. Drill 2 shows it in
  the shotgun, so the reader learns the formation from the drill rather than the text.

## 2. Leaps (a missing or assumed "why")

1. **The West Coast/1978 link contradicts the five-yard rule.** This is the most serious issue.
   "Why the 1978 rules made it work. Every route in @fig-walsh crosses a defender's face within a
   few yards of the line, exactly where re-routing used to wreck them. After 1978, once the receiver
   was five yards downfield…" But the slant in @fig-walsh is caught at about 5–6 yards, and the flat
   and shallow routes stay inside 5 yards. Those are exactly the yards where the chapter just told
   me contact is *still legal*. A careful reader will conclude that the 1978 rule did little for
   the slant-flat. Either show a West Coast route that lives past 5 yards (a dig, a shallow cross
   at 6+, a slant caught at 7–8), or say honestly which rule mattered for which routes. For example:
   the hands rule protected the timing, and the Blount rule freed the crossers and digs.
2. **Why didn't the 1974 and 1977 limits work?** "None of it worked." The reader needs one
   sentence on *why one hit was still enough*: one well-timed hit at 8 yards still wrecks a timed
   route. Answer 3 says exactly that, but it is hidden at the end of the chapter. Move it into "the
   1977 problem" section. Relatedly, fig-blount-rule's "before" panel is labelled "Up to 1973", so
   the main figure compares 1978 against the pre-1974 world and skips the 1977 rule that 1978
   actually replaced. Either add a note in the panel ("1977: one hit, but this one still lands") or
   relabel it.
3. **What does "contact within five yards" allow?** Is it one jam or unlimited contact inside 5?
   The text says "a defender may still contact a receiver". Say whether that means more than once.
4. **Where did the third receiver come from?** "If offenses were going to … put a third receiver on
   the field to do it". Every offense diagram up to that point (Coryell, Walsh) has two wide
   receivers, a tight end and two backs. The third receiver arrives without anyone deciding to use
   one. Add a sentence (who went to three wides first, and when) or a 2-WR → 3-WR line in the
   timeline.
5. **The 53 vs. the 3-4: "a 3-4 is a 53 on every snap."** If that is true, why was Matheson's
   package special in 1972? The reader needs the distinction: a 3-4 has *two* standing ends, and the
   53's trick was hiding a 3-4 inside a 4-3 personnel group (the same players, so the offense
   couldn't read it from substitutions). The figure makes this worse. See section 3.
6. **The two-point "worth more than twice one point" line.** "late in a game, when the score makes
   two points worth more than twice one point, the arithmetic flips." To a beginner, two points are
   always worth twice one point. This needs one concrete example (down 8, score a TD: going for two
   ties it, the kick leaves you down 1) and a shift from "points" to "win probability".
7. **Kickoffs from the 30: why do more returns mean better field position?** In 1994 a touchback
   came out to the 20. The reader needs the missing fact that the average return started drives
   past the 20 (and roughly by how much). Otherwise "more returns meant better starting field
   position" is an assertion.
8. **The salary cap's mechanics.** "signing bonuses are spread across the years of a contract so
   they can't be hidden." One sentence would make this land: without proration, a team could pay a
   huge bonus in a year it had cap room and count only a small salary later. The Denver paragraph
   has the same gap. Explain the mechanism or cut it (see section 4).
9. **Lawrence Taylor as an "answer".** In "You'll be able to…" and in Takeaways, Taylor is listed
   as the defense's answer "to slow pass protection". The body actually tells the reverse: Taylor
   was a problem that broke 1970s protections, and the H-back was the answer. The framing is
   muddled. Call him a *player type* the 3-4 created room for, which is what the 3-4 section
   says.
10. **The 46: how does a stack of linebackers on the tight end make it "devastating" against two
    backs?** The chapter defers to 05-02. For a reader whose 05-02 memory has faded, one clause
    would help (two backs plus a TE side = predictable strength; eight in the box beats eight
    blockers).
11. **"Epic in Miami" film room: "both teams were playing the 1970s way, with a safety and
    linebackers waiting to collide with receivers, and on almost every throw nobody is allowed to."**
    This was four seasons after the rule. Were the defenses really still built for the old rules
    in 1982? The claim needs support, or soften it to "built for".
12. **Why defenses put their best rusher on the offense's left.** The text gives the QB's
    blind-side reason, which works. But a reader may still ask how right-handed QBs were so dominant
    that every defense could assume one. Half a clause would cover it.

## 3. Diagrams I couldn't decode, or captions that don't say what to notice

- **fig-walsh (p. 9): the "flat" route isn't in the flat.** H's route ends *inside* X's alignment at
  about 3 yards, between X and the line, not out toward the sideline. W's dashed "widen" arrow ends
  almost on top of the slant's stem. The picture shows W sitting *in* the slant lane, not pulled
  away from it, so it shows the opposite of "the ball goes to X inside him". The caption also claims
  W "widens for the flat and the ball goes to X on the slant, inside him", and I can't see that.
  The label **"W can't cover slant and flat"** sits at the top-left near the corner's drop arrow,
  far from W, so it reads as the CB's label. Fix: take H's flat route outside X's alignment at 2–4
  yards, end W's arrow clearly outside the slant's catch point, and move the label next to W.
- **fig-46-counter strip (p. 13):**
  - Frame 4 note and caption say "W and S are chasing from behind", but **W never moves** (it is in
    the same spot in all four frames), and S ends right beside N at the line. In print S looks like
    he has filled the hole. Make W and S visibly pursue from the right side and behind the back.
  - Frame 4: **E sits on top of the pulling guard's purple ring**, so the kick-out is invisible.
    The SS label collides with H/T. That is a label-collision violation (convention d).
  - Frame 3 "G kicks out E": G is still in the backfield, about 2 yards from E. Fine for +1.2 s, but
    the "kick out" never visibly happens in any frame.
  - The hole itself is never marked. One green dashed band at the point of attack in frames 3–4
    would tell a beginner where to look.
  - Frame 1's "both OLBs to the TE" label sits next to M, 3 yards away from W and S. Put it beside
    W and S.
- **fig-53 (p. 10): the picture shows two standing ends.** There is an "L" standing at the left
  end of the line (the weak outside linebacker at about 1.6 yards), and the "three linemen with
  hands down" leader line points at *that L*, not at the E–N–E. So the figure shows the 3-4 already,
  with Matheson as a second standing edge player. The question "is it four rushers or three?" then
  applies to both standing ends, and the reader can't see what was unique about 53. Fix: put the
  weak-side linebacker at off-ball depth (a 4-3 look with 53 as the only standing end), and point
  the leader at the down linemen.
- **fig-coryell (p. 7):** this is mostly clear. However, the corners' "squat" arrows point back
  toward the line, which looks like a mistake. The Z dig is drawn at about 13.5 yards running
  through the SS, while the caption says 15. The Mike's drop arrow lies on top of the pass line.
- **fig-blind-side (p. 11):** this is good and the clearest figure in the chapter. One quibble:
  the orange "blind" wedge points down and behind the QB, but the rusher's path comes from up-left,
  at the QB's level. A beginner may wonder whether the rusher ever enters the orange area. End the
  rusher's arrow inside the orange wedge.
- **fig-drill-kgun (p. 20): the yellow line-to-gain is drawn 10 yards downfield.** The setup says
  "Buffalo gained 9 yards on first down", which makes this 2nd-and-1, so the line should be 1 yard
  away. A beginner trained by Part 1 to read the yellow line will notice. Also, see section 5:
  2nd-and-1 changes the right answer.
- **fig-league-trend (p. 6):** this is excellent. The caption says what to notice, and the
  panel-by-panel reading is a model.
- **fig-timeline (p. 18):** it is readable, but it introduces facts the text never told: Marino's
  5,084 yards, Fairbanks and Phillips making the 3-4 a base defense, both strikes, replay
  1986–91/1999. Either mention them in the body or accept that the timeline teaches new material.
  A history reader won't mind, but AUTHORING says the timeline summarises.

## 4. Drag and repetition

- **Overlap with 11-01.** The 1974 and 1977 rules, the Isaac Curtis rule, the 17.2 points figure and
  "fewest since 1942" all get full treatment in 11-01 and are retold here. 11-01's drill 3 is
  "third-and-8, 1977", the same question as this chapter's drill 3. Trim "the 1977 problem" to a
  recap paragraph and make drill 3 about something 11-01 didn't do (for example, 1977 vs 1979
  *pass blocking*).
- **Paragraphs with no football lesson.** The Body Bag Game paragraph, the Ryan–Gilbride punch, the
  Denver cap-penalty paragraph (two penalties with dates and dollar figures), the "70 Chip" aside,
  and "the first AFC West champion to call more passes than runs" are each colourful, but together
  they add about 400 words of trivia. Cut the Denver paragraph or give it a lesson (how deferral
  dodges the cap). Cut or shrink the Body Bag paragraph.
- **Repeated phrasing.** "The 1978 pattern again: the rules open a gap, and offenses move in" says
  again what "A rule change opens a gap, the offense pours into it…" already said. Both are fine,
  but the third restatement ("Notice the shape of the exchange…") is one too many.
- **Lawrence Taylor and the Theismann sack** were already told in 01-03 and 04-02. The link back to
  01-03 is there. Shorten the retelling to one line.

5. *The 1994 package and the two-point EP arithmetic:* **Partly.** I can do 0.988 against

6. *Free agency and the cap, plus three effects:* **Yes** for the three effects. I can't explain how
   the cap is enforced (proration, deferral).

**Drills, attempted before opening the answers:**

- **Drill 1 (where does the H-back go?):** My answer was: on the left next to the LT, chip 56, then
  release. **Correct.** But it is too easy. The figure labels 56 "the Giants' best pass rusher" and
  the chapter just told me Gibbs invented the H-back for exactly this. Make it harder: put 56 on the
  *right* (the QB's front side) and ask whether the H-back still goes to him. Or have 56 shift
  pre-snap.
- **Drill 2 (K-Gun vs base):** My answer was: throw to the slot, H, against the linebacker. The
  defense should have started in nickel. **Correct.** However, the setup makes it **2nd-and-1**
  ("gained 9 yards on first down"), and the drawn line to gain says 1st-and-10. At 2nd-and-1, a
  base defense against 11 personnel would also expect run. The answer doesn't address the down and
  distance, which the book has trained me to read first since Part 1. Either change the setup to
  first down or address the 2nd-and-1 in the answer.
- **Drill 3 (1977 vs 1979):** My answer was: 1977, the Mike hits Y at 8 yards; 1979, he can't, and
  the ball goes up the seam between the safeties. **Correct.** But the 1977 half depends on an
  unstated premise ("nobody touched Y at the line"). Under the one-chuck rule, if the end in front
  of Y jammed him, the Mike has no hit left. The question should state the premise, or ask "who
  should take the one hit?", which would be a better question. The Tampa 2 follow-up in the answer
  is good.

## 6. Knowledge gaps: what I still wonder

- Who put the **third wide receiver** on the field as a base package, and when? The nickel story
  depends on it.
- What does the **run-and-shoot** look like? One formation plate (2x2, no TE, one back) would let
  me "recognize it on old film" as promised.
- What did the **1994 chucking-rule change** actually change? It sits in the middle of a chapter
  about the chuck rule.
- **How did the 1978 rules show up in the Steelers' own results?** The footnote mentions that
  Pittsburgh won the next two Super Bowls by throwing more, with Bradshaw the 1978 MVP. That irony
  ("the team the rule was named for adapted fastest") is a great lesson about the trade, and it
  belongs in the body, not a footnote.
- After 1978, **how strictly were the rules called in the 1980s?** "Watch for it" hints that
  officials let a lot go, and the misconception box says strict enforcement came only in 2004. One
  sentence connecting the two would answer what I will actually see on old film.
- **Two-point decisions in practice:** one worked example (down 8, down 2, the
  "down 14, go for two after the first TD" idea), or an explicit "see 10-04 for the chart".
- **Kickoff returns:** the average start after a return versus a touchback in 1993 and 1994.
- **The ceiling, not just the average:** this is a minor wish. The chart stops at league averages,
  and a one-line note on how the *best* offenses (1984 Marino, 1998 Vikings) compared to the league
  would show the ceiling as well as the floor.
