# 11-01 From the Single Wing to the 1970s Scoring Drought: beginner-reader review

Reviewer persona: a casual NFL watcher who has never played and has read chapters 01-01 through 10-04 in
curriculum order, including the listed prerequisites 02-03 and 06-04. I read it start to finish in the PDF
(`pdfs/11-01-single-wing-to-scoring-drought.pdf`, 26 pp., built 21 s after the latest .qmd edit) and in the
source. Line numbers refer to the .qmd file.

**Overall:** a very good history chapter. The "action, reaction, rules" frame works, the people come alive
(Gillman cutting newsreels, the 73-7 quip, the radio helmet), and the Flex plate is the best diagram in Part 11
so far: I could see "every gap has an owner" at a glance. Because I've read Parts 1-10, the football vocabulary
almost never trips me up. The problems are about *reasoning*, not terms. The section on the scoring drought,
which is the chapter's payoff, leaves three "why" questions open that the chart raises on its own. One drill
is spoiled by the text just before it. Three of the four animations (the single-wing power, the umbrella and the
Lombardi sweep) are too crowded in print to show what their captions say.

## 2. Leaps: a missing or assumed "why"

These are the important ones, roughly in order.

1. **Why did scoring fall *in the 1970s*, when the rules that caused it were older?** (L1141-1167.) Reasons 1
   and 2 are rules that had been in force since forever: unlimited chucking "until 1974" and blockers with
   their hands tied. Under exactly those rules the NFL scored 21-22 a game through the '50s and '60s, and the AFL
   scored 23-24. So the rules can't explain the *drop*; they can only explain why the drop was hard to reverse.
   The chapter even sets this up at L1038 ("That makes what happened next all the stranger") and then never
   comes back to it. What changed around 1970? From the text, I can only guess: zone defenses plus
   bump-and-run matured (reason 4), the four-man lines (reason 3), and maybe the AFL's thin early defenses
   catching up after the merger. Say explicitly which causes are *new in the '70s* and which are constants that
   made the new ones decisive. As an analytically minded reader, this is the first question the chart made me
   ask.
2. **Why did the 1977 rule fail when the 1978 rule worked?** The "You'll be able to" bullet (L248-249) promises
   I can explain why the changes of "1972, 1974 and 1977" failed. "Why the fixes failed" (L1271-1280) covers only
   1972 and 1974, and its key sentence describes the 1974 rule ("hit him again once he crossed 3 yards"), which
   1977 had already replaced. One contact per receiver *in all*, plus no head slap (1977), sounds *stricter*
   than 1978's "only within 5 yards." Yet 1977 is the worst season on the chart. The answer I assume (one hit
   still allowed anywhere, such as 15 yards downfield at the break of the route, versus none past 5 yards; plus
   the blockers' hands rule) needs to be on the page. Without it, the 1977/1978 contrast that sets up 11-02
   doesn't land.
3. **The 1974 package's first season is the dip.** L1268: "The package worked for one season: scoring rose to
   20.6 in 1975." But the chart (Fig 9) shows 1974 itself, the first season under the package, falling to 18.2
   from 19.4, one of the lowest points on the curve. A reader looking at the chart sees the "1974: rule package"
   label pointing at a drop. Address it in a sentence (a lag while teams adjusted? fewer field goals before
   more touchdowns made up for them?).
4. **How do goalposts at the back of the end zone raise scoring?** (L1249-1250.) Moving the posts back makes
   field goals harder, so on its face it *lowers* points. The intended logic (fewer cheap field goal attempts,
   more fourth-down tries and touchdown drives, which matches the committee's stated aim at L1247) is one clause
   away. I've read 10-04, so I know the fourth-down argument. Connect it.
5. **"Put those two moves together" doesn't add up** (L783-784). The umbrella (6-1-4) with its two ends
   standing up is *already* 4-3-4 by itself: 4 linemen, M plus 2 ends = 3 linebackers, 4 DBs. Bill George's
   move comes from a *different* parent, the five-man front (5-2 minus the middle guard = 4 linemen; 2 + George
   = 3 linebackers). So did the 4-3 come from two different fronts each losing a lineman and meeting in the
   middle? Or did one front do both? I did the arithmetic and got confused. A two-line arithmetic sketch, or a
   tiny three-panel icon (6-1-4 → 4-3, 5-2 → 4-3), would fix it.
6. **Where did the five-man front come from?** L777 suddenly has "the five-man fronts of the early 1950s," and
   L693 mentions Greasy Neale's Eagle defense in passing. The chapter's own action-reaction rhythm has a hole
   here. The T arrives in 1940, and the next defensive answer we see is the umbrella in 1950. What was the
   defense's answer to the T in the 1940s? (If it was Neale's 5-2 Eagle, it deserves a paragraph in this
   chapter rather than a link.)
7. **The Flex and option blocking contradict each other a little.** L862-864: "A defense built on linemen
   charging into the backfield helped option blocking. The harder a lineman charged, the easier he was to push
   past the hole." But the Flex keeps two linemen *on the ball charging into their gaps* (L901, Fig 7 label "on
   the ball: charges into his gap"), and it was built around Bob Lilly penetrating (L905-906). Why don't those
   two get option-blocked? Presumably because a gap charge into an *assigned* gap is different from a
   free-lance charge, and because the flexed men behind them clean up. Say so. Also explain *why only two* flex
   and not all four (I assume because flexing all four would leave no pass rush at all, but the text only hints
   at that at L909-911).
8. **The Flex mechanism is a metaphor, not a mechanism.** L904-905: "a blocker trying to push a flexed defender
   'the way he wants to go' finds that he doesn't want to go anywhere." Nice line, but spell out the physics:
   the yard of distance means the blocker has to travel to reach him, the defender sees the block coming before
   contact, and since he hasn't committed to a direction there's no momentum for the blocker to use.
9. **Hash marks: why did the change help the run more than the pass?** (L1241.) It's asserted. The figure shows
   the short side gaining 3.6 yards and the wide side *losing* 3.6. Why is that a net gain for the run game in
   particular? Presumably sweeps and option plays to the short side used to run out of room. Say that.
10. **Why were defenses "happy to let an offense throw short in front of them"?** (L940.) This is the premise of
    Gillman's whole idea and it gets no reason. Was it because the rules made short completions slow to turn
    into points, or because a defense could tackle the receiver after a short gain?
11. **"Almost overnight" vs. "within a decade."** The You'll-be-able-to bullet (L241-242) says the T replaced the
    single wing "almost overnight." The text (L569-571) says "Within a decade," with the last holdout giving it up
    after 1952, twelve years later. Pick one framing.
12. **The umbrella's cost: "only four rushers."** (L770-771.) Four rushers is the *modern norm* (Part 7), so this
    reads like no cost at all unless you say "four instead of the six the era was used to." One phrase.
13. **Messenger guards: why guards?** (L677.) Why rotate guards rather than a receiver or a back? (I'd guess
    that guards were the most interchangeable players, or the least disruptive to swap.) It's a natural
    question and one clause would answer it.
14. **"The pass didn't do all of that, but it did most of it"** (L369). The tripling of scoring from 1932 to
    1948 gets attributed mostly to the pass with no evidence. For a data reader that's a claim without a
    number. Either soften it or support it (pass attempts or passing share of yards per game, 1932 vs. 1948).
15. **Ice Bowl "What to notice"** (L925-927). It says the Packers' backs rarely found cutback lanes because "the
    Flex had taken them away." The game was played at −13°F on a frozen field, which is the obvious confound.
    A skeptical reader will dismiss the point. Either pick a different Flex game for this "notice", or name the
    confound ("the ice didn't help, but…").
16. **Stram and "a passing laboratory."** The objective (L247) and section title say the AFL was a *passing*
    laboratory. Of Stram's credited innovations at L1014-1019, only the moving pocket is about passing (the
    I-formation, two tight ends and the triple stack are not). Apart from "threw deep and often" (L1006) and
    the scoring gap, I come away without one concrete thing AFL passing games *did* that NFL ones didn't.
    One example (a play, a concept, a receiver usage) would earn the title.

## 6. Knowledge gaps: what I still wonder

1. What changed around 1970 that hadn't changed before? (§2 leap 1.) This is the question the chapter is built
   to answer, and it doesn't quite.
2. Why did 1977's rules make things worse, and what exactly was different about 1978? (One sentence that
   previews 11-02 without stealing it.)
3. What did defenses do against the T in the 1940s (the Eagle 5-2?), and where did the five-man front with a
   middle guard come from?
4. What did an AFL passing play actually look like that an NFL one didn't? A single plate (a Gillman or Raiders
   vertical concept, or Stram's moving pocket) would make "laboratory" concrete. The chapter has seven field
   plates, and none of them is an AFL play.
5. A data-minded reader wants *passing* numbers next to points: attempts per game, or interception rate, from
   1932 to 1977. They would show the "gamble to weapon" arc in the subtitle and the 1970s squeeze directly.
   nflverse doesn't go back that far, so even three or four sourced data points (1932, 1948, 1965, 1977) in the
   text would help.
6. What happened to the two-way player? Was there a last 60-minute man in the NFL? (Chuck Bednarik in 1960
   would be the sentence of humanity this section is missing.)
7. Why did the colleges go *back* to one-platoon football in 1953 (L612)? It's stated with no reason.
8. Small: why do the hashes in Fig 10 start in 1945 when the text dates hash marks to 1933 (10 yards in, per
   footnote)? Add a clause on the 1945 move.
9. The hook says "Watch an NFL game in 2025," and "What survives" and drill 1 use 2025 too. It's October 2026,
   in the 2026 season. "2025" is fine for data claims (the under-center share), but the hook should be timeless
   ("Watch an NFL game today").
