# 10-03 Red Zone, Goal Line, Two-Point Plays, and Short Yardage: beginner-reader review

Reviewer persona: a casual NFL watcher who has never played and has read only chapters 01-01 through 10-02, in
curriculum order. I read the chapter start to finish in the PDF
(`pdfs/10-03-red-zone-goal-line-and-short-yardage.pdf`, 22 pp., built 20 s after the latest .qmd edit) and in
the source. Pages were rasterized at 60 and 130 dpi. Line numbers (L) refer to the .qmd file.

**Overall:** a strong chapter. The opening hook, the "twelfth defender that never misses a tackle" framing,
the corner-leverage rule ("the corner can take away the fade or the slant, not both"), the Super Bowl XLIX
film room and the sneak-by-team chart with noise bars are the high points, and the voice matches the pilots.
The weaknesses fall into four groups:

- **Numbers that seem to contradict each other.** Without an explanation, the reader can't reconcile them:
  67% vs 81% vs 90% from the 1, and the tush-push Eagles converting *no better* than Brady sneaking alone.
- **One serious labelling collision.** Offensive T and W mean something different from defensive T and W in
  the same pictures.
- **Two diagrams whose key action can't be seen.** In the shovel pass and QB power, you can't see the pulling
  guard.
- **Predict-the-play drills that only test recognition.** Their captions or the Watch box give the answers
  away.

## 2. Leaps: a missing or assumed "why"

1. **Three numbers for "from the 1" that don't add up, with no explanation.**
   - Misconception box (L657-660): sneaks from the 1 score on **67%** of plays.
   - Data section (L1462-1464): sneaks on 1-to-go convert **81%**.
   - Fig 3: first-and-goal from the 1 scores on **90%** of drives.

   A careful reader stops here. The answers are that one figure is per *play* and one per *drive* (four
   downs), and that converting a first down anywhere is easier than scoring at the goal line (the defense's
   back line is the goal line itself, and it brings goal-line personnel). The chapter never says either. Two
   sentences would turn this into a lesson instead of a contradiction. "Why is the last yard before the
   *goal* harder than the last yard before a *first down*?" is exactly the question this chapter should
   answer.
2. **The misconception box doesn't support its own claim.** It says "the numbers say the opposite is closer
   to true" (that is, run, don't pass). Its own numbers are dropbacks 52% and runs 55%, "nearly the same". So
   the numbers say it's a wash, *except* for sneaks. Reword the claim to match the data: "passing and handing
   off are about even; the quarterback sneak beats both."
3. **The push doesn't show up in the numbers, and the chapter dodges it.**
   - Fig 12 puts Philadelphia at about **81%**, right on the league line (80%).
   - The Eagles film room gives Hurts **83% of 179**.
   - Brady, sneaking *without* pushers, has **86% of 187**, and Allen 86% of 181.

   The chapter has just told me (L1381-1387) that two pushers add "a few hundred pounds of driving force at
   exactly the point of the contest". So why doesn't the most famous push team out-convert a solo sneaker? The
   only answer is one hedged line (L1513-1515: defenses "knew it was coming, which makes their rate more
   impressive than the raw number suggests"). That is a hand-wave. The pieces are all there: the Eagles call
   it on about half their 1-yard downs, so they use it in worse looks and longer "1s", and play-by-play counts
   two inches and a full yard the same. They need to be stated plainly as the answer to the obvious question.
   Otherwise the reader concludes the push does nothing. (12-06 owns the Eagles-vs-league delta, but this
   chapter has to at least name the puzzle it creates.)
4. **"The defense can't see it coming any differently" (L1247) vs the tush push being telegraphed.** The
   sneak's fourth advantage is that it's disguised. But the tush push is a *signature alignment* (Drill 3
   answer: "the push's signature alignment"; Watch box: "players stacked directly behind him is a tush push").
   So why does a play everyone sees coming still work at 80%? Say it outright: the push trades disguise for
   force, and on one yard force wins.
5. **Fade vs short throw: 40% vs 68% completion, but which call scores more?** (L740-745). The text says end-zone
   completions are mostly touchdowns and short completions aren't. But it never gives the touchdown rate per
   throw for either group, so I can't judge whether the fade is a good bet. "Choosing a coin flip" at 40% also
   reads oddly. Give the TD-per-attempt numbers.
6. **Why does one-on-one coverage beat the fade as often as it does, and why 5-8 yards from the sideline?**
   (L677-679). "It needs room" doesn't tell me what the room is *for*. It's room for the ball to land outside
   the receiver and still in bounds, and with too much room the safety can get there. One clause.
7. **Why did the defense drop out of zone in Fig 2, if the next paragraph says it plays man?** Fig 2 draws Cover
   3 at the 10 to show the squeeze, then the text says defenses mostly play man and Cover 0 there. That's
   fine, but say "*if* the defense stays in zone, this is what it looks like, and this is why it often
   doesn't."
8. **Why don't more teams sneak, if it converts 81% vs 70% for other runs?** L1508-1512 shows that volume
   varies enormously (KC: 1 in four seasons) and blames "Andy Reid's offense". It never gives the real reasons
   a beginner wants: injury risk to a franchise quarterback, shotgun-based offenses whose QB rarely lines up
   under center, and line quality. Without them, the chart's left panel suggests 25 coaches are being foolish.
9. **Two-point tries: is going for two worth it at all?** Whether to go is 10-04's job, but after reading
   "47% of tries convert" every reader immediately compares that with the 33-yard kick. One sentence ("two
   points times 47% is about 0.94 points, almost exactly what a ~94% kick is worth; 10-04 explains when the
   score tips it") would stop the itch without stealing 10-04's content.
10. **"That is why heavy offensive personnel throws more often near the goal line than you'd guess" (L1227)**
    is asserted with no number, and it runs against the 41%-pass-rate-inside-the-5 figure a page earlier.
    Either give the play-action rate from heavy personnel or soften the claim.
11. **6-2 vs 5-3: when and why one or the other?** The section shows both. It never says what one does better:
    the 6-2 is stronger right at the line, while the 5-3 adds a linebacker who can fill or cover a tight end
    or fullback. So I can identify them but can't say why a coordinator picks one.
12. **The shovel's ineligible-downfield aside (L987-992)** has a hidden "why". The flip must come fast
    because linemen can't be downfield. Is that because a shovel is still a forward pass? Yes, and the text
    says so, but buried in three clauses plus a college exception. Lead with the consequence: "it counts as a
    pass, so the linemen can't run upfield until it's thrown."

## 3. Diagrams I couldn't decode, or captions that don't say what to notice

  between defenders are small" (right) are not something I can *see*. The two panels look like the same
  picture with the top cropped off. Make the at-the-10 deep defenders visibly shallower and closer to the
  underneath layer, or draw the gap between layers with a dimension arrow in each panel (for example
  "6 yd" vs "2 yd"). The midfield "35 more yards" arrow is also tiny.

## 6. Knowledge gaps: what I still wonder

1. **Where are the goal-line *runs*?** Inside the 5, offenses run about 59% of the time (L652), and Fig 9 sets
   up 23 personnel with a fullback and tailback. Yet the chapter diagrams no handoff: no lead/iso, no power
   at the goal line, no outside run against a 6-2. The QB sneak and QB power get full treatment, while the most
   common goal-line play is never shown. Even one panel (an iso with the fullback leading into the gap the
   defense leaves) would close this.
2. **What counts as a touchdown on a run vs a catch?** On a sneak the ball only has to *break the plane* of the
   goal line, even if the quarterback is stopped. A receiver needs control and two feet down in bounds, which
   is why the fade lands near the sideline and end line. This is central to why sneaks score and fades are
   hard. 01-01 may have covered the plane, but this chapter is where it matters.
3. **Penalties at the goal line.** Half-the-distance enforcement, and a defensive pass interference in the end
   zone giving the ball at the 1, change red-zone strategy: they are part of why fades get thrown, since
   contact can draw a flag. One paragraph or a link to 09-03.
4. **What does the *defense* play on a two-point try?** Goal-line personnel, or regular? The two-point section
   is entirely offense, although the objective says "on both sides."
5. **Why is the Eagles' push no more successful than Brady's sneak?** See §2.3. At minimum, name the puzzle
   and point to 12-06.
6. **Why don't teams sneak more?** See §2.8 (injury risk, shotgun offenses, line quality).
7. **Why are 2 yards so much harder than 1 for a sneak (54% vs 81%)?** The chapter says "the play is for the
   last yard" but not *why* the extra yard breaks it: the pile moves about a yard and a half, then stops.
   Fig 11's caption hints at this ("decided in about a yard and a half"). Connect the two.
8. **Are rubs ever run in the other direction?** Fig 6 has the front man slanting and the back man going out.
   In XLIX the front man ran the pick and the back man slanted. A sentence saying the stack can be run either
   way would stop me thinking the film room contradicts the figure.
