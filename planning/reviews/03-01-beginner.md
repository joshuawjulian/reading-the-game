# 03-01 The Language of the Run Game — beginner-reader review

Reader persona: I watch NFL games casually and have never played. I have read 01-01 to 02-06 in
order, so I already have the positions, Mike/Will/Sam, the nose tackle and a one-line
"3-technique" (01-03), downs/EPA/success rate (01-02), the kill call (01-05), personnel and
formations including the I, shotgun and H-back (02-01 to 02-04), base/nickel, one-high/two-high,
the box and box counting (02-05), and motion (02-06). I read the source start to finish and looked
at all 21 pages of `pdfs/03-01-run-game-language.pdf` (rasterized at 60 dpi, and figure pages again
at 110-130 dpi).

**Verdict.** This is a strong chapter. The three-part structure (spaces, where defenders stand,
what blockers do, then counting) is easy to follow. The technique chart is excellent. The
"four blocks win a matchup, four change the numbers" split and "the QB is the missing man" are
the two ideas I will remember. Six problems hold it back:

- **(a)** Drill 3's answer depends on telling a 3-technique from a 4i. In the drawing they are a
  quarter-yard apart, so I can't tell them apart.
- **(b)** Drill 1's picture points the puller and the back at a different gap from the one its
  answer gives.
- **(c)** The Go-deeper data table contradicts what 02-05 taught about the cost of loaded boxes.
- **(d)** The chapter never says the basic thing: the point of attack is often where a defender
  is *standing*, and the blocks are what empty it.
- **(e)** Reach and cut-off, and cut-off and down, are not told apart clearly.
- **(f)** It runs to about 9,100 words of prose against a 5,000 target. Most of the extra is
  history and film rooms.

## 2. Leaps: a missing or assumed "why"

1. **Why does a run aim *at* a defender?** Fig 3 (point of attack) puts the green X in the right
   B gap, but the 3-technique is standing right on top of it. The fullback's lead line and the
   back's path both go straight through the 3. No lineman's block is drawn, and the caption doesn't
   say who moves the 3. The chapter has just said "A 3-technique sits in the B gap and ... owns
   it". So my first real question was: why run into the gap the defense's best interior player
   owns? The missing sentence is: *the point of attack is chosen because of who is there and how
   he can be blocked; blocks move the owner out of his gap, and the run goes into the space they
   make.* Draw the RG/RT blocks on the 3 in Fig 3, or say in the caption that they are left out.
2. **Why do we need both a 1 and a 2i (and a 3 and a 4i, and a 5 and a 7) if they sit in the same
   gap?** The chapter says each gap "holds one or two shaded alignments", and that the shaded
   defender owns his gap. If both own the B gap, why does a coach care which one it is? The answer
   is already in the chapter, in the next section: it changes *which offensive lineman is
   covered*, and so who is free to double and climb. But the two ideas are never connected. One
   sentence at the end of the technique section ("a 3 and a 4i both own the B gap; the difference
   is whether the guard or the tackle has a man on him, which you'll see matters next") fixes it.
   It also explains the 4i bullet below.
3. **"A 4i ... makes the offense's favorite double team, guard and tackle together, awkward to set
   up"** (l.606). Why? Two problems here. The double team hasn't been taught yet (it is block 5,
   two sections later), and the mechanism isn't given. I can't reconstruct it. If the 4i is on the
   tackle, the guard is uncovered and can help, which sounds like it makes the double team
   *easier*. Explain it or cut the clause.
4. **Fig 6 bottom: "the LT twice over."** The 5 and the 4i are both on the left tackle. One man
   can't block two. Yet the next paragraph says "both guards are uncovered ... so the guards are
   the natural climbers". Surely the left guard has to help with the 4i rather than climb? As
   written, the text contradicts the arithmetic it is teaching.
5. **Reach vs cut-off: same footwork, opposite difficulty.** The reach is "a defender on your
   play-side shoulder ... get your head and body outside him". The cut-off is "usually a defender
   on his play-side shoulder ... gets his body between a defender and the ball carrier". To me
   those are the same block. The text then calls one "the hardest block in football" and says the
   other costs "little". The missing why: on the play side the runner is coming *to* the defender,
   so the seal must hold until he passes. On the backside the play is going *away*, so a moment's
   delay is enough. Say that explicitly. It's the whole difference.
6. **Cut-off vs down block.** Panel 4 of the block library shows the backside tackle blocking a
   3-technique, a man lined up *inside* him, on the guard's outside shoulder. By the chapter's
   definition ("a defender lined up inside you, toward the center"), that's a down block. Is the
   difference only which side of the play you're on, and whether you drive him or just get in his
   way? Tell me.
7. **Fig 9 → "the next section explains why that extra man is almost always there."** The QB
   section's count works (9 occupy 9, 2 free). But a beginner wonders: on a *run*, why does a
   cornerback count as "occupied" by a receiver who isn't really running a route? The bullet "take
   one away by running a route that a defender has to cover" only half-answers it. The real reason
   is that the defender doesn't know yet that it's a run. Add a clause.
8. **The hook is never paid off.** The opening analyst sentence ("3-technique over the right guard
   ... guard and tackle double-team him, the tackle climbs to the Mike, the fullback kicks out the
   end, and the back hits the C gap untouched") would be a perfect closing test: re-read it now
   and every word parses. A three-line callout before the Takeaways would make the "you can now do
   this" moment explicit.
9. **Box definition drift.** Hat-math section: the box is "roughly from tackle to tackle (or tight
   end to tight end)". 02-05 said "just outside one end of the line to just outside the other". The
   shaded box in Fig 9 includes the 5- and 9-techniques, which sit *outside* the tackle and tight
   end. Use 02-05's definition.
10. **Film room SB XVII: why was a cornerback in the box at all?** "Watch ... what the motion did to
    the cornerback" assumes I can connect it: man coverage → the corner follows the motion man →
    he ends up on the line → he is the smallest man in the box. 02-06 taught motion as a man/zone
    tell, so one sentence that links the two would make this a lovely payoff. Right now I have to
    infer it, with no diagram and no replay link.

## 6. What I still wonder (knowledge gaps this chapter should answer or point to)

1. **How do I see technique numbers on TV?** The broadcast angle is high and from the side. Which
   replay angle shows shoulders? How big is the difference between a 3 and a 4i in feet? (The
   Watch-for-it asks me to do exactly this on Sunday.)
2. **Real distances.** How far apart are the linemen (centre to centre), and how far off the ball
   is a defensive lineman? The technique chart says it exaggerates the spacing; give me the real
   numbers once (an analytic reader wants them, and they explain why 3 vs 4i is hard to see).
3. **Who decides the assignments at the line?** The opening says "a center has maybe ten seconds
   to tell four other linemen who is blocking whom". How? A one-line forward link to the center's
   calls / Mike identification ([Pass Protection](../04-the-passing-game/04-02-pass-protection.qmd)
   owns it) would close the loop.
4. **What if the defense moves at the snap?** Covered/uncovered is a pre-snap snapshot. If the 3
   slants into the A gap, does the guard's "covered" status change mid-play? (Zone "adapts", gap is
   "fragile", but one sentence on *what* changes would help.)
5. **What is legal?** Why do linemen keep their hands inside? Can you block someone from behind or
   below the waist? A one-line pointer to the cut-block/legality material
   ([03-02](03-02-zone-running.qmd), 09-03) would do.
6. **On power, who is left unblocked, and why is that OK?** Hat math says *someone* is always
   free. Fig 11's gap panel never says who it is (the backside end?). The Drill 1 answer doesn't
   either. Naming the free man on every full-play diagram would reinforce the chapter's best idea.
7. **Why is the backside end the one to leave free?** The chapter says it: "the defender who is
   farthest from the play". It would land harder if Fig 10's caption repeated that reason.
8. **Re-reading the opening sentence.** I want to be told that I can now parse the analyst's
   sentence from the hook. That is the payoff the chapter promised.
