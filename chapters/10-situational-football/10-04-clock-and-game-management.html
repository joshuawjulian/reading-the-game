# --- the two Reid drives: how much clock did the slow snaps cost? ----------------------------------
def slow_snaps(df, team, t_from, t_to, hurry):
    """Snaps on a drive that ended in bounds with the clock running (no stoppage before the next snap):
    how many, the median gap, and the seconds lost against a hurry-up snap `hurry` seconds later."""
    d = df[(df.posteam == team) & df.play_type.isin(["pass", "run", "no_play"]) & df.down.notna()
           & df.half_seconds_remaining.between(t_to, t_from)].sort_values("play_id")
    d = d.assign(gap=d.half_seconds_remaining - d.half_seconds_remaining.shift(-1)).iloc[:-1]
    # the clock ran: a catch or run that stayed in bounds (out of bounds counts too before the last five minutes),
    # and the next snap was not after the two-minute warning stopped the clock
    ran = (d.play_type.isin(["pass", "run"]) & (d.incomplete_pass == 0) & ((d.out_of_bounds == 0) | (d.half_seconds_remaining > 300))
           & ~((d.half_seconds_remaining > 120) & (d.half_seconds_remaining - d.gap <= 120)))
    g = d[ran].gap
    return len(g), float(g.median()), float((g - hurry).clip(lower=0).sum())

The clock is a currency, and the two teams want opposite things

For 56 minutes of a typical game the clock is background noise. Then, at some point in the fourth quarter (and again at the end of the first half), it becomes the most important player on the field. The reason is simple: possessions are scarce, and time is what possessions are made of. A team that is behind needs plays per second: as many snaps as it can squeeze into the time left. A team that is ahead wants seconds per play: the fewest snaps, each taking as long as possible, so the opponent gets the ball back late or never.

Everything in this chapter follows from those two goals, and from one fact you met in the first chapter: the clock keeps running after a ball carrier is tackled in bounds and stops after almost everything else. So the first skill is pricing. How many seconds does each kind of play cost a team in a hurry? You can measure it. The play-by-play data records the game clock at every snap, so the gap between two snaps is what the first play, and whatever happened after it, cost.

Read it as a price list. The cheapest plays are the ones that stop the clock by themselves: an incomplete pass, or a completion where the receiver gets out of bounds. Each costs only the five or six seconds the play takes. The expensive one is the catch in bounds, because the clock keeps running while eleven players run back to the line, the officials spot the ball and the quarterback relays the next call. That costs about 21 seconds if the offense simply runs its next play. Offenses have two ways to cut the bill. A timeout brings the cost back down to the play itself (6 seconds) but spends one of only three. A spike, which you’ll meet properly below, stops the clock about 16 seconds after the previous snap, and spends a down instead.

That is why two-minute offenses throw toward the sideline, why defenses protecting a lead try to tackle receivers inside the field, and why coaches hoard timeouts like cash. A team with 1:53 and two timeouts can afford roughly five or six in-bounds completions and a few incompletions. That is enough for 50 or 60 yards. It is not as much as it sounds.

The out-of-bounds clock rule

One line in the price list has a condition attached. “Out of bounds stops the clock” is true only late in a half. The full rule, which this course calls the out-of-bounds clock rule, is this: when a runner goes out of bounds, the officials stop the clock, put the ball back on the hash, and restart the clock when the referee signals the ball ready for play, well before the next snap. The exceptions are the moments when time matters most. After the two-minute warning of the first half, and inside the last five minutes of the game, the clock stays stopped until the snap. One more case follows the same “until the snap” rule at any time of the game: if a runner goes out of bounds on a play where the ball changed hands (an interception or punt return that ends out of bounds, say), the clock waits for the new offense’s first snap.1

Why the general rule? Because the league wants the clock to keep moving for most of the game (television windows are three hours), and because without it any team could stop the clock for free all game long with five-yard sideline throws. So for 55 minutes out of bounds saves only a few seconds. Why the exception? Because late in a half the league wants a comeback to be possible: the rule hands the trailing team a lifeline, letting it save time without spending timeouts as long as it can get the ball to the sideline. One detail closes a loophole: a fumble that goes out of bounds restarts the clock on the ready signal at any time of the game, so nobody can fumble out of bounds on purpose to stop it.

Two more clock facts that surprise new viewers. A first down does not stop the NFL clock, ever. If you also watch college football, you’ll have seen the clock pause after a first down; since 2023 college does that only in the last two minutes of each half, but the NFL never does.2 And the two-minute warning is a free timeout for both teams. It comes after the last play snapped before 2:00, so a play snapped at 2:05 that ends at 1:58 is followed by a full stoppage.3 Trailing offenses plan around it (they would rather snap at 2:05 than at 1:55); leading offenses try to burn the clock right down to it, so that the warning stops a clock that has already done its work.

CautionCommon misconception

“He stepped out of bounds, smart: that stopped the clock.” In the third quarter, or with 7:00 left in the fourth, it barely did: the clock started again as soon as the ball was spotted, usually 25 or more seconds before the next snap. A runner who fights for two extra yards in bounds early in the fourth quarter is not costing his team time. The same play at 4:30 is a different story.

The two-minute drill

The two-minute drill is the hurry-up offense with a deadline: a team that needs points before a half ends, usually with the clock already inside the two-minute warning. Teams practise it every week as its own script, because almost every rule of normal play-calling changes:

  • No huddle, two plays at a time. The quarterback gets two calls through his helmet radio, so after the first one the offense can line up and snap the second without waiting. The radio cuts out when the play clock reaches 15 seconds,4 and in a hurry-up there often isn’t time for a call at all, so the quarterback carries a menu on his wristband and calls one-word “clock” plays that the whole offense knows.
  • Throw outside. Routes break toward the sideline (outs, comebacks, corner routes) so that the catch ends out of bounds. Receivers are coached to secure the ball, take one more step, and get out: never turn upfield for two extra yards and get tackled in bounds.
  • Middle of the field only with a plan. A catch over the middle is worth taking if it gains a lot or if the offense has a timeout to spend. Otherwise it is the dearest line on the price list.
  • No sacks. A sack loses yards and keeps the clock running. Quarterbacks throw the ball away instead (out of bounds, past the line of scrimmage, or near a receiver) and accept the lost down. (Hold on to that rule: the four-minute offense below turns it exactly upside down.)
  • Know the finish line. The real target is usually not the end zone but field-goal range: the yard line from which the kicker has a good chance, with enough time left to get the field-goal unit on. That normally means a stopped clock. Teams also practise a running-clock field goal for emergencies, under names like “fire” or “Mayday”: the unit sprints on and kicks off a live clock. Kansas City’s special-teams coordinator Dave Toub has said about 17 seconds is as fast as it can realistically be done in a game.5 It works, but nobody plans on it.

What does the defense do about it? It plays the same game backwards: it sits deep enough that nothing gets behind it, keeps its cornerbacks outside the receivers so the sideline routes are contested, and tackles in bounds. That tug-of-war, offense pulling the ball outside and defense pushing it inside, is the whole two-minute drill in one sentence.

ImportantWhy it exists

The two-minute drill exists because the clock rules are asymmetric. The offense can stop the clock (incompletions, out of bounds, timeouts, spikes); the defense can only stop it with timeouts and by forcing incompletions. So the trailing offense, uniquely, gets to control the pace of the end of a half. The drill is the set of habits that turns that control into points, and it has to be rehearsed because the habits contradict normal football: throw away from the open middle, give up yards for seconds, and never take a sack.

The drill has a long history in big games. In the 1958 NFL Championship Game, Johnny Unitas drove the Baltimore Colts into position for Steve Myhra’s tying field goal with seven seconds left, and the Colts won in the first sudden-death overtime in NFL history (sudden death: the first team to score in the extra period wins).6 In January 1987 John Elway’s Broncos went 98 yards in Cleveland, starting with 5:32 left (“The Drive”), to force overtime in the AFC Championship Game. The most famous two-minute drive of the modern era, Tom Brady’s in Super Bowl XXXVI, gets its own look later in the chapter, because it began with a famous broadcaster telling him not to try it.

The spike

A spike is the quarterback taking the snap and immediately throwing the ball into the ground. It is an incomplete pass, so the clock stops. Why isn’t it intentional grounding, which is exactly “throwing the ball away to avoid a loss with no receiver nearby”? Because the rulebook carves out an exception for it: a quarterback under center (the rulebook says within one yard of the snapper) may stop the clock legally if, immediately upon receiving the snap, he begins a continuous throwing motion and throws the ball directly into the ground. If he waits, he may not: a delayed spike is grounding. Nor may a quarterback in the shotgun: a spike from five yards deep is intentional grounding.7 That rule gives you one of the best tells in football. When a team that has been in the shotgun all day sprints to the line and the quarterback steps up under center, the spike is coming.

The spike play itself takes about 1 second of clock. (The price list’s 16 seconds for “catch in bounds, then a spike” is snap to snap, and almost all of it is the sprint back to the line before the spike.) What a spike really costs is a down. Spiking on first down is cheap (second-and-10 is fine in a two-minute drill); spiking on second down leaves only one real play before fourth down; spiking on third down is almost always a mistake. Teams spike about 72 times a season in all. You will see it most often in the sequence the price list describes: an in-bounds completion, a frantic sprint to the line, the quarterback under center, and the ball slammed into the turf with 15 to 20 seconds gone.

When should a team with a timeout spike instead of using the timeout? When there is time for more plays after it. A timeout is the more valuable token, because it keeps the down. Coaches spend timeouts late, for the field goal (the kicking unit needs a stopped clock to run onto the field) or for a catch in the middle near the end, and use the spike to keep them.

NoteGo deeper: the fake spike

Once everyone expects a spike, the threat of one is a weapon. On November 27, 1994, against the Jets, Dan Marino ran to the line at the Jets’ 8 with under 40 seconds left, shouted “Clock! It works only because the real spike is so common.

Timeouts and the 10-second runoff

Timeouts are the scarcest resource in the two minutes: three per half, and none carry over. Their value depends on when they are used. A timeout early in a half saves the 20-odd seconds between plays, but those seconds rarely matter. The same timeout in the last minute can be the difference between a field goal and no field goal. That’s why you’ll hear announcers criticise a coach for “wasting” a timeout on a substitution mix-up in the third quarter: it is the end-of-half timeout he’s spending. The flip side is the end of the first half. Timeouts left over at halftime simply vanish, so coaches spend them much more freely in the second quarter’s last two minutes than they would in the fourth.

The rules guard the clock against cheap tricks. After the two-minute warning of either half, if the offense stops the clock by breaking a rule (a false start or other foul while the clock is running, intentional grounding, an illegal forward pass, a backward pass thrown out of bounds, spiking the ball after a play has ended), the officials take ten seconds off the game clock, the 10-second runoff, and restart it on the ready signal. The offense may spend a timeout instead, if it has one, and the defense may decline the runoff along with the yardage penalty.

The defense has its own version, with a different penalty. It is never charged a runoff; if it fouls to stop the clock, the clock simply restarts on the ready signal. But a trailing defense out of timeouts might be tempted to jump offside on purpose with, say, 0:20 left, just to freeze the clock. So in the last 40 seconds of a half, if the defense has no timeouts left, a defensive foul that stops the clock gives the offense a choice: take the yardage and play on, or have the half end right there. A leading offense will usually end it.9

Injuries are on the same list. After the two-minute warning an injury stoppage costs the injured player’s team one of its timeouts (unless an opponent’s foul caused the injury, or the play was a score, a free kick or a turnover). A team with none left is treated as having taken an illegal extra timeout: if it has the ball, the defense may run ten seconds off the clock, and if it is the defense, in the last 40 seconds the offense may end the half.10 The rule exists to stop players from faking injuries to stop the clock.

The runoff also applies when a replay changes a stopped clock into a running one after the two-minute warning. If a receiver ruled out of bounds is shown to have been tackled in bounds, the clock should never have stopped, so ten seconds come off, and neither team can decline them (either can use a timeout to prevent them). Either half can end on a runoff, and one famous game did.

  2. One timeout, when the price was high. After a third-down conversion at the 49ers’ 36 with the clock running, the Chiefs called their timeout at 0:40, saving about 20 seconds at the moment the seconds started to matter.
  3. A timeout in the pocket. After Mahomes’s scramble to the 33 stayed in bounds, Kansas City didn’t use its last timeout; it hurried to the line and threw incomplete at 0:21. The timeout was insurance: as long as the Chiefs held it, any catch, anywhere, could be followed by a field goal.

Tied 16–16, the 49ers faced third-and-5 at the Chiefs’ 35 right after the two-minute warning, and threw incomplete. A run that stayed in bounds would probably not have gained the first down either, but it would have kept the clock running: Kansas City would have had to spend one of its timeouts or watch about 40 seconds disappear before the field goal. Instead the clock stopped, the 49ers kicked a 53-yarder on fourth-and-5 at 1:57 to lead by three, and the Chiefs got the ball back with 1:53 and both of their remaining timeouts, which, as the chart shows, was enough. Kicking on fourth-and-5 was defensible (it is at the edge of the “go zone” you’ll meet below, and a field goal at least forced Kansas City to score); the costly call was the third-down incompletion that came before it.

The four-minute offense: when the clock is on your side

Flip the situation. You lead by six with four minutes left and you have the ball. Now every second the clock runs is a second the other team can’t use. The four-minute offense is the two-minute drill in reverse:

  • Run the ball, and run it inside, where the ball carrier will be tackled in bounds. A 3-yard run that keeps the clock moving beats a 12-yard pass that might fall incomplete.
  • Use the whole play clock. Snap with one or two seconds left on the 40-second clock. A first down then costs the opponent about 40 seconds per play, or about two minutes per set of downs, if it has no timeouts.
  • Ball security over yards, and stay in bounds. A fumble or an interception hands the opponent both the ball and the clock. Backs are coached to go down in bounds rather than fight toward the sideline, and the quarterback to slide, or even to take a sack, rather than throw an incompletion that stops the clock. It is the two-minute drill’s “no sacks” rule turned upside down.
  • First downs end games. The point of the drive is not points; it is a fresh set of downs, each worth roughly two more minutes. Teams sometimes stay in bounds or even go down short of the goal line, rather than score and give the ball back.

The defense’s counter is timeouts, used immediately after each running play stays in bounds, and everything it can do to force an incompletion or a punt. A defense with three timeouts and the two-minute warning to come can stop the clock four times; with none, it can only hope for a stop.

The kneel-down arithmetic

At some point the leading team doesn’t need any more yards. It only needs the clock to run out, and the safest play in football is the quarterback kneeling down. Each kneel uses a down and takes about two seconds, and the clock keeps running afterwards, because the quarterback is “tackled” in bounds. Between kneels the offense lets the 40-second play clock run almost to zero. In the play-by-play, consecutive kneels with no timeout between them come about 39 seconds apart.

So the question every leading team asks when it gets a first down late in the game is: can we kneel it out from here? It has three kneels to use, and then one more thing working for it: after the third kneel the offense doesn’t have to snap again until the 40-second play clock runs out. If the game clock hits zero first, the game ends without a fourth-down play. It’s arithmetic.

The rule of thumb that falls out, in the form a coach would use on the sideline: after the two-minute warning, a first down ends the game if the defense has zero or one timeout left. With two, it ends the game from about 1:25; with three, from about 0:45. Each of the first two timeouts is worth about 40 seconds; the third is worth more, because it also takes away that last free run of the play clock. That is why a defense down by four with 2:30 left will start calling timeouts after the first running play, not wait. And it is why a timeout is worth more to the team without the ball late in the game: it is the only thing that converts the offense’s play clock back into usable time.

Two wrinkles. If the first down comes shortly before the two-minute warning, the clock will stop at 2:00 anyway, so start the count at 2:00 and treat the warning as one more defensive timeout (it stops the clock for free, just as a timeout would). And if the arithmetic comes up a few seconds short, so that fourth down arrives with time still on the clock, the offense doesn’t kneel a fourth time (that would hand over the ball at the spot). It snaps and burns the clock: the quarterback takes the snap and runs backward or sideways until the seconds are gone, or the punter does the same, or the team simply punts deep.

One famous finish shows the leading team taking this arithmetic to its logical end. In December 2007, the Eagles led Dallas 10–6 when Brian Westbrook broke a run into the clear with Dallas out of timeouts; he deliberately went down at the 1 instead of scoring, and Donovan McNabb knelt three times to end the game.13 Run the numbers the way the chart does. The clock ran from 2:19 to the two-minute warning. From 2:00, the warning counts as one stoppage and Dallas had no timeouts, so read the chart’s one-timeout row: a first down at about 2:04 or less ends the game, and 2:00 is less. McNabb knelt at 2:00, 1:15 and 0:30, and the game clock ran out during the last play clock. A touchdown would have given Dallas the ball back with about two minutes left; the kneels gave it nothing.

The same arithmetic explains one of the strangest sights in football, a defense that lets the offense score. In Super Bowl XLVI the Patriots led the Giants 17–15, and the Giants had the ball at the New England 6 with about a minute left. New England had one timeout. Played normally, the Giants would run the clock down to the last seconds and kick a chip-shot field goal (a very short, near-certain kick) to win, and Tom Brady would never touch the ball again. So Bill Belichick told his defense to let Ahmad Bradshaw score, and Bradshaw, who tried to stop at the 1, fell into the end zone with 57 seconds left.14 Down 21–17 with 57 seconds and a timeout beat down 18–17 with no time at all. (It didn’t work: Brady’s last drive fell short.) Westbrook and Bradshaw are the same idea from opposite sides: the team with the ball valued the clock more than six points, and the defense paid six points to get the clock back.

The formation’s history is a cautionary tale. On November 19, 1978, the Giants led the Eagles 17–12 with about 30 seconds left and the ball. Instead of kneeling, they handed off; quarterback Joe Pisarcik and fullback Larry Csonka botched the exchange, Eagles cornerback Herman Edwards picked up the loose ball and ran it in, and Philadelphia won 19–17. The “Miracle at the Meadowlands” cost the Giants’ offensive coordinator his job the next day, and teams across the league began kneeling, in a formation built to make that fumble impossible.15

By convention the defense doesn’t fight a kneel-down. When the Buccaneers’ Greg Schiano had his defense charge into the Giants’ victory formation in September 2012, knocking Eli Manning to the ground, Tom Coughlin confronted him at midfield and the incident became a league-wide argument about sportsmanship. Schiano’s defence was that the game wasn’t over until the clock hit zero, which is true; the convention survives because a fumble on a kneel is so unlikely that rushing it mostly risks injuries.16

End of the half: points versus risk

The last minute of the first half has its own logic. There is no next possession to protect against (the second half starts with a kickoff and fresh timeouts), so a team with the ball and time faces a narrower question: is a shot at points worth the risk of giving the opponent the ball with time to use it?

Three questions settle most of these calls:

  1. How far to field-goal range, and how many plays do I have? From the price list: with one timeout, 45 seconds is about four or five plays if half of them go out of bounds or fall incomplete. If those plays can plausibly gain 30 yards, go.
  2. What happens if it goes wrong? An interception or a three-and-out deep in your own territory gives the opponent a short field. Backed up at your own 10 with 40 seconds, most coaches kneel.
  3. Who gets the ball to start the second half? If you will receive the second-half kickoff, scoring at the end of the half is a double score opportunity: points just before the break, then the ball again immediately. That’s a big reason most coaches defer when they win the coin toss.

Put numbers on one. You have the ball at your own 30 with 0:45 left in the first half and one timeout, and your kicker is good from the opponent’s 35 (a 53-yarder). That is 35 yards in about five plays, realistic if the offense throws outside and uses its timeout well, and the downside (an interception at your own 35 with 30 seconds left) gives the opponent a long field-goal try at worst. Most modern coaches would go. Move the same situation back to your own 10 with no timeouts, and it is 55 yards in three or four plays with a turnover now handing the opponent a short field: kneel.

The most famous “points or risk?” argument came at the end of a game. In Super Bowl XXXVI, with the score tied and the Patriots starting at their own 17 with 1:21 and no timeouts, the broadcast’s John Madden said they should run out the clock: “You have to play for overtime now.” Bill Belichick and Tom Brady went for it anyway.17 Madden was not wrong about the risk; he was underrating the price list. Two short throws to the running back in bounds, then a spike on first down at 0:41, the textbook spike: an in-bounds catch, a sprint to the line, the clock stopped for the price of a cheap down. Then a third throw to the back that was pushed out of bounds, an incompletion, and Troy Brown’s 23-yard catch that went out of bounds had the ball at the Rams’ 36 with 21 seconds left. One more catch in bounds, six yards to the 30, a second spike at 0:07, and Adam Vinatieri kicked the 48-yard winner as time ran out.

The Hail Mary

When there is time for only one snap and the end zone is out of field-goal range, there is one play left: the Hail Mary. Four or five receivers sprint to one spot in or near the end zone, the quarterback buys time and throws as high and as far as he can, and everyone converges on the ball. The name comes from the Cowboys’ 1975 playoff game at Minnesota, when Roger Staubach threw a 50-yard winner to Drew Pearson and told reporters afterward that he had closed his eyes and said a Hail Mary.18

How often does it work? Over the last ten regular seasons, throws of 35-plus air yards from 40 or more yards out in the last eight seconds of a half, the play-by-play’s best proxy for a Hail Mary, numbered 217. They produced a touchdown 6% of the time and an interception 20% of the time.19 One in seventeen sounds hopeless, but the alternative at 0:04 is zero, and an interception on the last play costs almost nothing. From deep in its own half (its own 30, say), even a strong arm can’t reach the end zone, so teams switch to a hook-and-lateral or a multi-lateral “river” play and hope someone breaks free.

Two rules make the last play stranger than it looks. First, a half never ends while the ball is in play: if the clock hits 0:00 while the ball is in the air, the play finishes. That is why a team with four seconds left can run a play that lasts eight. Second, a half cannot end on an accepted defensive foul. If the defense commits pass interference or holds on the last play, the offense gets the yardage and one more untimed down. (An offensive foul gives no such extension.)20 That is why you’ll see the pile in the end zone watched by every official on the field, why defenders are coached to go up for the ball and never through the receiver, and why a flag on a Hail Mary is the most dramatic flag in football.

The prevent has a bad reputation. “The only thing the prevent defense prevents is winning” is one of football’s oldest lines, and every fan has watched a team give up 60 yards in 90 seconds while playing it. The criticism is fair when the defense goes soft too early. With two minutes left and timeouts in hand, an offense can take the free 12-yard completions all the way down the field, because the prevent concedes yards to save time and the offense has time to spare. With 15 seconds left and no timeouts, the same coverage is exactly right: there is time for one or two plays, so the only thing that can beat you is a long one. The prevent is a clock-dependent call, like everything else in this chapter. What it trades away is yards; what it buys is the end of the game. The trade is good only when the clock makes yards cheap.

You’ll rarely hear a coach say “prevent.” On the field it usually shows up as a dime (six defensive backs) or quarter (seven) package, with three rushers and deep zones. The vocabulary of the whole last two minutes varies like that from staff to staff: the spike call is often just “clock” (Marino shouted “Clock! Clock!” before his fake), the running-clock field goal is “fire” on one team and “Mayday” on another, and the four-minute offense is also called the four-minute drill. Listen for the idea, not the word.

Two rare plays that decide games

Sidebar plate: the intentional safety

An intentional safety gives the opponent two points on purpose. It sounds absurd until you see the situation it’s for: you lead late, you’re about to punt from your own end zone, and the punt is the most dangerous play you could run. The punter is standing almost on the end line, rushers have the shortest possible path to him, and a block or a long return can lose the game. So instead the punter takes the snap, runs around his own end zone burning seconds, and steps out of the back of it. The opponent gets two points; you then kick off from your own 20. (In Super Bowl XLVII the Ravens’ blockers held the rushers on purpose to buy their punter more seconds, since a hold in your own end zone costs only the safety you are already taking. The rulebook now closes that door: multiple fouls on one down to manipulate the clock cost 15 yards, and the clock is reset to where it was at the snap.21)

In field position alone, the swap is worth about 20 yards, and it also removes the chance of a blocked punt. The usual version, the clock-killer, needs two things. The lead must be three or more, so that the two points don’t tie the game. And the clock must be nearly gone, so that the opponent’s drive after the kick has time for one or two plays at most. That second condition matters most with a lead of exactly three: the safety cuts it to one, so any field goal would now lose the game, and the swap is safe only if there is no time left to get into range. The best-known example is the Ravens’ in Super Bowl XLVII, covered in Downs, Distance, and Field Position.

The boldest safety used a different logic entirely, and the team taking it was behind. On Monday Night Football in November 2003, Bill Belichick’s Patriots trailed Denver 24–23 with 2:51 left and fourth down at their own 1. Long snapper Lonie Paxton snapped the ball through the end zone on purpose: 26–23 Broncos. The free kick from the 20 pushed Denver back to its own 15; New England used two timeouts to force a three-and-out, got the ball back at its own 42 after the punt, and Brady threw the winning touchdown to David Givens with 36 seconds left.22 This was the field-position safety: not a way to end the game, but a bet that two points were a fair price for moving the next exchange of possessions 20-odd yards up the field. It only works with timeouts in hand and a defense that can get a stop, which is why you almost never see it. Modern teams use the simpler version in the final seconds: the Chiefs took one with 15 seconds left in a January 2025 divisional-round win over Houston, and the Eagles with six seconds left against Tampa Bay in 2025.23

Sidebar plate: the fair-catch kick

After any fair catch, the receiving team may choose a fair-catch kick instead of a snap: a place kick (held, with no tee) or a drop kick (dropped and kicked on the bounce) for three points, taken from the spot of the catch, with the defense lined up ten yards away. There is no snap: the holder kneels at the spot itself, not seven yards behind a line. And there is no realistic rush, because, as on a kickoff, the defenders may not cross their line until the ball is kicked. Two details make it interesting. The kick is eight yards shorter than a field goal from the same line of scrimmage (spot plus 10 for the end zone, instead of plus 18), and the period can be extended for it: if time runs out during the punt on which the fair catch is made, the receiving team may still take the kick.24

Fourth down: the go-for-it decision

For most of NFL history, fourth down was a kicking down. Inside field-goal range you kicked; outside it you punted; you went for it on fourth-and-inches near midfield, or when you were losing late. The go-for-it decision, the choice among going for it, kicking and punting, has since become the most visible place where analytics changed football.

The argument is an expected-points argument, and you have the tools for it from Downs, Distance, and Field Position. Take fourth-and-2 at the opponent’s 36:

  • Punt: the opponent takes over somewhere around its own 10, a situation worth a little to it. You’ve traded a scoring chance for field position.
  • Field goal: a 54-yarder. Modern kickers make those about 74% of the time (2023–2025); a miss gives the opponent the ball at the spot of the kick, its own 44.
  • Go for it: teams converted fourth-and-2 58% of the time in 2021–2025. Success keeps a drive alive inside field-goal range; failure gives the opponent the ball at its own 36.

The truer story is the slow arrival of evidence: public models, analysts on every staff, owners asking questions, and a generation of head coaches who had grown up with the arguments.

Why did the rate dip in 2022 and 2023 before rising again? Nobody has a proven answer. One change is easy to see in the data: teams began trying long field goals far more often, 182 attempts of 50 yards or more in 2021, 224 in 2022 and 279 in 2024, as kickers’ range grew. A fourth down at the opponent’s 35 that would have been a go a few years earlier could now be a reasonable kick, so some of the dip is probably the kicking option getting better rather than coaches losing their nerve.

Where did the extra attempts come from? Not from fourth-and-long in your own territory, which still almost nobody tries. They came from short yardage around midfield, where teams used to punt, and from short yardage inside field-goal range, where they used to kick. Fourth-down rules of thumb

You don’t need a model to make a decent call from the couch. These heuristics agree with the public models in most situations, for an average offense and kicker, with the score close and plenty of time left. Keep one thing straight: they are what the models recommend, not what teams do. Teams are still more conservative than the models, so on Sunday you’ll often see a punt or a kick where the rule below says go.

  • Fourth-and-1: go almost anywhere past your own 30, and in many models even deeper. Teams converted 68% of fourth-and-1s in 2021–2025 (this includes the QB sneak and tush push, which convert more often still).
  • Fourth-and-2 or 3: go from about midfield in. Conversion rates were 58% and 50%.
  • The opponent’s 30 to 40 is the “go zone.” It is too far for a sure field goal and too close for a good punt (a punt from the 35 nets only about 25 yards, and 15 if it bounces into the end zone), so the models usually say go there even on fourth-and-4 or 5 (converted 50%). Teams still go on those only about 30% of the time (the heatmap’s 2023–2025 panel); that gap between the models and the sideline is where the next round of the aggression shift will come from, if it comes.
  • Inside the opponent’s 5: go on fourth-and-goal from the 1 or 2 unless the score says three points matter more than seven. A failed try still leaves the opponent pinned at its own goal line.
  • Long yardage in your own half: punt. Fourth-and-6 to 10 converts only about 34% of the time, and failure gives the opponent a short field.
  • Then let the score and clock override everything. Trailing late, go more; leading late, the value of the clock (and of making the opponent need two scores) can flip a go into a kick.

Two things these rules leave out are what make real decisions hard: your offense and kicker are not average, and the opponent’s offense matters (if they score at will, possessions are worth more, so go). That’s why two smart coaches can disagree about the same fourth down.

CautionCommon misconception

“Going for it and failing proves it was the wrong call.” A fourth-and-2 that converts 58% of the time fails 42% of the time. Judge the decision by the odds when it was made, not by the result; the film room below shows why that is harder than it sounds.

The logic in each “go” box is the same: does two points move the margin across a line that matters? The lines are the numbers the other team can reach in one possession, 3 (a field goal) and 7 or 8 (a touchdown with a kick or a two-pointer).

The yellow boxes are the honest ones: the arithmetic comes out about even, so the situation decides. The one you’ll see most often on television is down 1 after a late touchdown. Kick, and you are tied and probably headed to overtime, roughly a coin flip. Go for two, and about half the time you win right now; the other half you lose. Those are almost the same number, so coaches lean on everything else: a tired defense or a hot offense says go; a road team that fears overtime says go; a great kicker and a strong overtime offense say kick; and if the opponent will get the ball back with time to score again, a lead of one point is worth less than it looks. Down 4 is similar: kicking leaves you down 3, where a field goal ties; a successful try leaves you down 2, where a field goal wins, and a miss leaves you needing a touchdown. Up 2, 9 and 10 are the leading team’s versions: each try either removes one of the opponent’s tying or winning combinations or, if it fails, hands it one.

The famous modern case is trailing by 8 after a touchdown, which happens when you were down 14 and score. The traditional answer was “kick, cut it to 7, and hope for another touchdown and overtime.” The analytics answer is “go for two now.” Make it (about half the time) and you trail by 6, so your next touchdown and extra point win the game in regulation. Miss it and you trail by 8, so you need a touchdown and a two-pointer to tie, but you know that now and can plan the rest of the game around it. Over the two touchdowns, going early wins outright about half the time you get both scores and goes to overtime some of the rest; kicking twice almost always goes to overtime, a coin flip. With a two-point success rate near 48%, going early wins about 60% of the games in which you score both touchdowns, against about 50% for kicking, and it also tells you before the last drive what you need.

Like the fourth-down rules, the chart assumes few scores remain. Early in a game, with many possessions to come, the margin will change again before it matters, so teams simply take the better expected value, which since 2015 has been close to a tie. A team with a great short-yardage offense (the Eagles of the tush-push era, for example) should go for two more often than the chart says.

Overtime

The overtime rules have changed more in the last fifteen years than almost any other part of the game, and the reason is visible in one game. Under the old sudden-death rule (in the regular season from 1974), the first team to score in overtime won. That gave a big edge to the team that won the coin toss, and the edge grew as offenses improved. The league chipped away at it: in 2010 (playoffs) and 2012 (regular season) a field goal on the first possession no longer ended the game, but a touchdown still did; in 2017 regular-season overtime was cut from 15 minutes to 10.31 Then came the January 2022 divisional-round game in Kansas City, the “thirteen seconds” game covered in The Game in Fifteen Minutes: the Chiefs won the toss, drove for a touchdown, and Josh Allen never touched the ball. That spring the league changed playoff overtime so that both teams get a possession. In 2025 it extended the rule to the regular season.

The details are in the rulebook’s Rule 16; the strategy follows from three facts.32

Second possession is information. The team that gets the ball second knows exactly what it needs. If the first team kicked a field goal, the second can use all four downs in its own territory, because a punt is no longer an option worth taking. If the first team scored a touchdown, the second must too, and then decide whether to kick and play on or go for two and win on the spot.

First possession is a potential third possession. If the game is still tied after both teams have had the ball, the next score wins, and the team that received first is the one most likely to get the ball next. In the playoffs, with unlimited time, that potential third possession has real value; in the regular season’s 10-minute period, it often never comes.

The clock is a weapon in the regular season. Ten minutes is short. A first team that drives slowly for a field goal can leave the second team with four or five minutes and the need for points; a long drive by the second team can run the clock out, and the game ends in a tie (which counts as half a win and half a loss in the standings).33

Two edge cases complete the picture. If Team B’s defense scores on Team A’s first possession, with a safety or an interception or fumble returned for a touchdown, B wins on the spot: it has now had the ball too, and it’s ahead. And because the kickoff itself counts as A’s chance, a kicking team that recovers the opening kickoff has used up A’s possession. Since 2026 an onside kick may be declared at any time, so a team could try one to open overtime; onside kicks are recovered rarely enough that this is a curiosity, not a strategy.

That is why there’s no consensus on whether to receive. Analytics researchers still tend to find a small edge in receiving, mostly because of that possible third possession; many coaches and former players on television prefer kicking off, for the information and the free use of fourth down. The edge either way is small, it depends on the two offenses, and the regular season’s 10-minute clock makes the third possession less likely.34

Watch for it on Sunday

TipWatch for it on Sunday: coach the clock

In the last two minutes of each half, call the clock decision before the team makes it. Keep three numbers in your head from the score bug: time left, each team’s timeouts, and the yard line versus field-goal range (add 18).

  • After every play, say “running” or “stopped” before the broadcast tells you, and remember the out-of-bounds rule only bites after the first-half two-minute warning and in the last five minutes.
  • Trailing offense, clock running: predict hurry-up, spike or timeout. Spike on first or second down with time for more plays; timeout when there’s time for only one or two, or when the kicking unit has to come on. The tell for a spike: a shotgun quarterback stepping up under center.
  • Watch the receivers’ feet. On a sideline catch, does he get out, or turn upfield for two more yards? Did the defender push him out (a gift) or tackle him in bounds (a win for the defense)?
  • Leading team with a first down: do the kneel math. After the two-minute warning it’s over if the defense has 0 or 1 timeout; with 2 it needs about 1:25 or less, with 3 about 0:45. If it works, call the victory formation before it lines up.
  • Trailing defense: predict when it calls timeouts. A good one calls them right after the leading team’s first-down and second-down runs, not at the end.
  • Fourth down before the snap: go, kick or punt? Use the heuristics: fourth-and-1 almost always go; opponent’s 30 to 40 is the go zone; long yardage in your own half, punt.
  • After a touchdown late: find the margin and check the two-point chart (down 2, 5, 8 or 10; up 1, 5, 12 or 15: expect them to go). Down 1 is the coin flip: watch which way the coach leans.
  • At the end of the first half: who receives the second-half kickoff? That team will push harder for a late score.

Spike now, or snap a real play? And what has to be true before the field goal?

TipAnswer

Spike now. The field goal has to come with the clock stopped. With no timeouts, the only ways to stop the clock are an incompletion, getting out of bounds, or a spike, and a field-goal unit running on with the clock moving needs about 17 seconds at the very best, which this offense won’t have.

Price the options from the snap of the completion, at about 0:36. Spike: about 16 seconds snap to snap, so the clock stops at about 0:20 and it’s second-and-10. A real play instead: a hurried snap comes about 21 seconds later, at about 0:15. If that play is a catch in bounds, say at the 18, it ends around 0:10 with the clock running, and the spike that would have to follow needs about 16 seconds from that snap: the clock hits zero first. Game over. A sack or a run stuffed in bounds ends it the same way. The spike is the cheap, certain stoppage; that’s why the quarterback is already under center.

After the spike there is a real choice, and coaches split on it. Kick now: a 44-yarder, made about 84% of the time in 2023–2025. Or take one quick shot first: a ball-out-fast throw to the sideline, or a throwaway, costs about six seconds and stops the clock; ten yards turns it into a 34-yarder, made about 97% of the time. The shot is worth it only if it can’t end in bounds or in a sack: with no timeouts and about 0:14 left, a sack ends the game. (If the offense had a timeout, it would save it for the kick and still spike now.)

Can the offense kneel three times and end the game? What should the defense do with its timeout?

TipAnswer

Yes: three kneels end it, and the timeout can’t stop that. Use the kneel chart’s one-timeout row: after the two-minute warning, a first down at about 2:04 or less ends the game, and 1:40 is less. Expect the offense to switch to the victory formation now.

The arithmetic, step by step. Kneel 1: the offense lets the play clock run, snaps at about 1:01 and the kneel ends at about 0:59. The defense calls its timeout. Kneel 2 starts the clock on the snap and ends at about 0:57. With no timeouts left, the play clock runs again: kneel 3 at about 0:18, ending around 0:16. Now it’s fourth down with 16 seconds left and a 40-second play clock, so the game clock reaches zero before the offense has to snap again. Game over, without a fourth-down play.

The defense needed two timeouts here to force a fourth-down snap, and even then it would get the ball back with only seconds left. It doesn’t matter when it calls the one it has; any order gives the same arithmetic. Its only real hope is the one its alignment shows: everyone crowding the ball in case the snap is fumbled, which is exactly what the victory formation is built to prevent.

Punt, kick or go? What do the two teams’ personnel and alignment tell you about the decision?

TipAnswer

Go for it, and both teams have told you so. By every rule of thumb in the chapter, this is a go: fourth-and-2 converts about 58% of the time; the 36 is in the opponent’s 30-to-40 “go zone”, where a punt nets only about 25 yards and a 54-yard kick near this kicker’s limit misses often (the league made 50- to 54-yarders 74% of the time in 2023–2025, and a kicker near his career long will do worse) and hands the opponent the ball at its own 44. It is the exact situation of the chapter’s expected-points table, where going was worth about 0.7 points more than kicking. In 2023–2025, teams in the first three quarters went for it on 57% of fourth-and-2s between the opponent’s 31 and 40, against 36% in 2013–2015.

The offense’s clue is its personnel: no punter or kicker jogging on, the regular offense lined up with the quarterback in the shotgun. Some teams line up to go and then take a delay-of-game penalty or call a timeout if they can’t draw the defense offside, so wait for the snap; but a team that keeps 11 personnel on the field on fourth-and-2 has usually made its decision on the sideline. The defense’s clue is its shell: it has read the same situation, given up on deep help, and put seven men near the line with the corners pressed, to take away the run and the quick throws that need only 2 yards. Expect a quick pass against that press (a slant, or two receivers crossing to shake their corners) or a run designed for exactly 2 yards, from the short-yardage menu in Red Zone, Goal Line, Two-Point Plays, and Short Yardage.

Takeaways

  • The clock is a currency. Late in a half the trailing team wants plays per second and the leading team wants seconds per play. Price every play: an incompletion or a catch out of bounds costs about 5–6 seconds, a catch in bounds about 21, unless a spike (a down) or a timeout pays for it.
  • Out of bounds stops the clock until the snap only late: after the two-minute warning of the first half and in the last five minutes of the game. The 10-second runoff punishes an offense that stops the clock illegally, and can end a half.
  • The two-minute drill throws outside, avoids sacks, spikes on early downs and saves timeouts for the end. The four-minute offense does the opposite: runs inside, snaps at the end of the play clock, and treats first downs, not points, as the goal. After the two-minute warning a first down ends the game against a defense with 0 or 1 timeout left; with 2 it needs about 1:25 or less, with 3 about 0:45.
  • End-of-half calls weigh points against the risk of a short field for the opponent. The prevent defense is right only when the clock makes yards cheap; the Hail Mary scores about one time in seventeen. The intentional safety and fair-catch kick are rare but decide games.
  • Fourth down became a decision: the neutral go rate more than doubled from 2017 to 2025, because the expected-points arithmetic favours going far more often than coaches used to. Fourth-and-1 is almost always a go, the opponent’s 30–40 is the go zone (in the models, if not yet on every sideline), and good decisions still fail often.
  • Two points and overtime follow the score. The two-point chart asks whether two points cross a line that matters (down 2, 5, 8, 10; up 1, 5, 12, 15). Overtime gives both teams a possession; the second team plays with information, the first with a possible third possession.
