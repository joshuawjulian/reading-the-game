def tagged(players):
    """Apply the book's marker tags to a list of players."""
    out = []
    for q in players:
        table = OFF_TAGS if q.side == "off" else DEF_TAGS
        out.append(q.moved(label=table.get(q.key, q.label)))
    return out

def P_(fld, p, d, w):

def carried_ball(df):
    """Simulated tracking with the ball drawn beside its carrier, so his tag stays readable."""
    df = df.copy()
    for _, g in df.groupby("frameId"):
        b, pl = g[g["side"] == "ball"], g[g["side"] != "ball"]
        if b.empty or g["time"].iloc[0] <= 0.2:
            continue
        bx, by = float(b["x"].iloc[0]), float(b["y"].iloc[0])
        dist = np.hypot(pl["x"] - bx, pl["y"] - by)
        if dist.min() < 0.4:                         # someone is carrying it: tuck it beside him,
            others = pl[dist >= 0.4]                 # on whichever side is clearest of other players
            spots = [(bx - 1.05, by), (bx, by + 1.0), (bx, by - 1.0)]
            clear = [np.hypot(others["x"] - x, others["y"] - y).min() for x, y in spots]
            x, y = spots[int(np.argmax(clear))]
            df.loc[b.index, ["x", "y"]] = [x, y]
    return df

Sunday, a few minutes before kickoff. The broadcast cuts to a graphic: eleven faces, each with a name, a college and two or three letters you only half recognize. LT. RG. FS. Sam. Then the game starts and the announcer is talking faster than you can follow: “The nickel is walking down over the slot… the Mike points at the Will… the tight end is the only one in to block…” You know the quarterback. You can probably find the running back. And the other twenty players on the field are, for most viewers, a blur of helmets. This chapter fixes that. By the end, every one of those twenty-two people will have a name, a job and a reason for existing, and you’ll know the one distinction that makes the rest of the book possible: the difference between who a player is, where he stands and what he’s been told to do.

You’ll be able to…

  • Name the defensive positions, from the nose tackle to the free safety, and tell the Mike, Will and Sam linebackers apart by where the tight end lines up.
  • Name the special-teams specialists (kicker, punter, long snapper, holder, returner, gunner) and find them on a field goal, a punt and a kickoff.

  • Explain why a guard and a cornerback have such different bodies, using real combine data.
  • Separate a player’s position from his alignment and his assignment, and explain why good coaches blur all three on purpose.

Twenty-two players, three teams

At any moment eleven players per side are on the field. Not the same eleven, though. An NFL team is really three teams that share a roster: an offense for when it has the ball, a defense for when the other side has it, and a special teams unit for kicking plays (kickoffs, punts, field goals and extra points, which you met in The Game in Fifteen Minutes). A team carries 53 players on its roster, and up to 48 of them dress for a game.1 Between plays (never during one) the coaches can swap in whoever they like, as often as they like; how the defense gets the chance to answer the offense’s substitutions is part of Personnel.

It wasn’t always this way, and how it changed explains why NFL bodies look the way they do. For football’s first half-century most players played the whole game, offense and defense, which meant every player had to be a compromise: big enough to block, quick enough to cover, fit enough to last sixty minutes. Substitution rules were strict, so a player who came out often couldn’t go back in that quarter. The NFL loosened them during the Second World War, when rosters were thin, and made unlimited free substitution permanent in January 1950.2 Within a few years teams were using separate offensive and defensive units (“two platoons”), and then specialists for kicking. The last great player who refused to come off the field was the Eagles’ Chuck Bednarik, a center on offense and a linebacker on defense, who played almost all of the 1960 championship game and ended it by tackling the Packers’ Jim Taylor on the final play.3 The modern exception proves how rare it is: the Jaguars drafted Travis Hunter second overall in 2025 to play both receiver and cornerback, and he took 326 offensive and 162 defensive snaps before a knee injury ended his rookie season in late October.4

Once a player only had to do one job, he could be built for that job. That is the single biggest reason a modern roster contains 330-pound men who can’t run a fast 40 (the 40-yard dash, the sprint every prospect runs before the draft) and 190-pound men who can’t block anyone: each was chosen for a narrow, demanding task. We’ll measure that with real data later in the chapter.

The whole picture first

Here are all twenty-two at once, in a traditional alignment. The offense is in a formation (the way it arranges its players before the snap; formations get their own chapters, starting with Formation Rules) with two backs stacked behind the quarterback, against a classic “base” defense (four linemen, three linebackers, four defensive backs). You’ll rarely see this exact picture on a modern Sunday (we’ll see the more common one shortly), but it’s the clearest way to meet every position, because each one is in its classic spot.

How to read the field diagrams in this book. The offense moves up the page. The blue line across the field is the line of scrimmage, the imaginary line through the ball that neither side may cross before the snap (defined fully in Downs, Distance, and Field Position); the thick yellow-orange line is the line to gain, ten yards ahead here. Offensive players are blue circles, defenders are orange squares, and each wears a short tag. Offense: Q quarterback, R running back, F fullback, X, Z and H wide receivers, Y (and sometimes U) tight ends, and LT LG C RG RT the offensive linemen from left to right (diagrams often leave the linemen’s tags off). Defense: E defensive end, T defensive tackle, N nose tackle, S M W the Sam, Mike and Will linebackers, CB cornerback, $ the slot cornerback, FS and SS the free and strong safeties. Shape and color always tell the sides apart, so a blue C (center) is never an orange CB. A colored ring marks a player to watch.

Take a moment with that picture before we go player by player. A few patterns are already visible:

  • The middle is crowded and heavy; the edges are open and light. Ten big men (five offensive linemen, four defensive linemen, plus a tight end) are packed into about twelve yards of width around the ball. The fastest, lightest players (receivers and cornerbacks) are out near the sidelines.
  • The offense is lined up in rows by depth. Seven players on the line of scrimmage, then the backs behind. That’s not a style choice; it’s a rule, and it decides who can catch a pass. We’ll get to it.
  • The defense is lined up in layers. Linemen within a yard of the ball, linebackers four or five yards back, cornerbacks out wide about seven yards off, safeties deepest of all. The closer a defender is to the ball, the more he’s built to stop the run; the farther away, the more he’s built to stop the pass.

The offensive line: five men who never touch the ball

Start in the middle, because every play does. The offensive line is the five players shoulder-to-shoulder around the ball: a center, two guards and two tackles. On every snap, run or pass, their job is to block. They almost never touch the ball (the center touches it once, at the snap), they’re almost never mentioned on the broadcast unless something goes wrong, and they decide more games than anyone except the quarterback.

From the inside out:

  • The center (C) is in the middle. He starts every play with the snap, handing or tossing the ball back between his legs, and then becomes a blocker like the others, often against the biggest defender on the field, with his head still coming up from the snap. Before the snap he’s usually the line’s communicator: he points at one linebacker and calls him the “Mike” for this play, and every lineman counts from that man to work out whom he blocks. That call, the Mike ID, is explained in Pass Protection.
  • The two guards (LG, RG) line up on either side of the center. They are often the most powerful linemen, built for short, violent collisions with defensive tackles in a phone booth of space. On some runs a guard pulls: he opens his hips at the snap and runs along the line, just behind it, to block on the other side, which is why guards tend to be a bit quicker than you’d expect.
  • The two tackles (LT, RT) are on the outside of the line. Tackles are usually the tallest linemen with the longest arms, because their hardest job is pass protection against the fastest defenders on the field: speed rushers coming from outside, running a wide arc toward the quarterback. A tackle has to slide backward and sideways fast enough to stay in front of a man fifty pounds lighter.

Why so big? Because blocking is mostly physics. A blocker’s job is to move a defender off a spot, or to keep the defender from moving him off his, and mass and leverage win those contests. Offensive linemen at the NFL Scouting Combine (the league’s annual pre-draft testing event) average a little over 310 pounds and about 6-foot-5.5 What they give up is speed: a typical lineman runs the 40 in a bit over 5.2 seconds, which is very fast for a 310-pound person and slower than nearly everyone else on the field.

CautionCommon misconception: the left tackle is always the best lineman

For decades the left tackle was the glamour job on the line, and for a good reason. Most quarterbacks are right-handed, so when they set up to throw they face right and their back is to the left: the blind side. Michael Lewis built The Blind Side (2006) around this, opening with Lawrence Taylor’s 1985 sack that broke Joe Theismann’s leg and tracing how that kind of threat drove left-tackle salaries up.6

That logic still exists, but it’s weaker than it was. Modern defenses move their best pass rusher to whichever side they like, often snap to snap; left-handed quarterbacks flip the whole thing; and the right tackle usually faces more run-blocking in front of the tight end. The best tackle in football can play on the right. The Lions drafted Penei Sewell seventh overall in 2021, played him at right tackle, and in April 2024 made him, at the time, the highest-paid offensive lineman in the NFL on a four-year, $112 million extension. For 2026, after their long-time left tackle Taylor Decker left, they moved Sewell to the left side,7 which tells you both halves of the story: the best tackle doesn’t have to play on the left, but when a team has to choose, the blind side still pulls. Judge a lineman by how often you don’t hear about him.

The offensive line has one more defining feature, the one that matters most to you as a viewer: these five may not catch a forward pass (more on that soon). And they wear numbers in the 50s, 60s and 70s, so you can always find them.

The quarterback

The quarterback (Q) lines up directly behind the center, either with his hands under the center’s backside to take a hand-to-hand snap (under center) or standing about five yards back to take a short toss (the shotgun). He touches the ball on almost every offensive play: he hands it off, fakes a handoff, throws it, or runs with it.

You know the quarterback already, so here’s what you might not. His most important work often happens before the snap. He usually receives the play call through the radio in his helmet and repeats it to the others (What a Play Really Is). At the line he reads the defense and may change the protection or the whole play. After the snap, on a pass, he isn’t just “finding the open man”: he’s working through a planned order of receivers, the progression (Passing Game Fundamentals), while three-hundred-pound men close in, usually releasing the ball in under three seconds. That’s why quarterbacks are the most expensive players in the sport, and why the body type is the least uniform on the field: from 5-foot-10 scramblers to 6-foot-6 pocket passers, what makes a quarterback is mostly invisible (processing speed, accuracy, nerve).

The backs: running back and fullback

Behind (or beside) the quarterback are the backs. The names are fossils, inherited from rugby, where the backs stood in rows by depth: quarter-backs nearest the scrimmage, half-backs behind them, a full-back deepest. (You’ll often read that the fractions measured literal distances, a quarter, half or all of the way back. That’s too neat; the fractions worked more like “first, second and last row.”)8 The formations changed and the names stuck, which is why today’s fullback lines up in front of today’s halfback.

Keep one more meaning of “back” in your pocket, because the rulebook uses it: any offensive player who isn’t on the line of scrimmage at the snap is a back, whatever his position. A wide receiver standing a yard off the line is, legally, a back. That turns out to decide who may catch a pass.

The running back (R; often called the tailback when he’s the deepest back), also called the halfback, is the offense’s main ball carrier on running plays. Most offenses use one at a time. He takes handoffs and finds the hole (the opening his blockers make; coaches call the spaces between linemen gaps and give them letters, as The Language of the Run Game shows), catches short passes (often as the last option, the “checkdown”), and on passing plays he frequently stays in to block a blitzing linebacker (one who rushes the quarterback instead of dropping into coverage) before slipping out. Running backs are among the shortest players on the field but heavy for their height: low center of gravity, thick legs, hard to knock over, quick enough to make a defender miss in a small space.

The fullback (F) is a bigger back, built to block. In the classic two-back offense he lines up between the quarterback and the tailback and leads into the hole ahead of the runner, hitting the first linebacker who shows up. He carries the ball occasionally (often on short-yardage plunges) and catches the odd pass.

You see fewer fullbacks than your parents did. As offenses spread out with three and four receivers, the second back was the obvious player to remove, and many NFL teams now carry no true fullback at all. The ones who remain tend to be unusually versatile, because a player who can only lead-block is hard to justify on a 53-man roster.

Wide receivers: X, Z and the slot

The wide receivers are the offense’s specialist pass-catchers, lined up away from the linemen: “split out” wide near the sideline, or in between, in the area called the slot. Their job on a pass is to run a route (a planned path, with timing, that you’ll learn to read in Passing Game Fundamentals), get open and catch the ball. On a run, they block the defensive backs downfield, a thankless job that turns eight-yard runs into forty-yard ones.

Coaches give each receiver spot a letter, and you’ll hear them on broadcasts:

  • The X receiver lines up on the line of scrimmage, usually on the side away from the tight end. Someone has to: the rules (coming up) want seven players on the line, and on the side without a tight end, X is the end of it. That costs him twice. Only a player off the line may be moving at the snap, so X can’t go in motion (running across the formation before the snap; see The Pre-Snap Phase), and with no cushion and no running start he’s the easiest receiver to jam at the line. So the X is often the team’s strongest outside receiver, the one who can beat that contact on his own.
  • The Z receiver lines up a yard off the line, usually on the tight end’s side. Being off the ball keeps the tight end at the end of the line, and so still eligible (you’ll see why that matters in a moment), gives Z a little room against a jam, and makes him the receiver most often sent in motion.
  • The slot receiver (often called H) lines up between the outside receiver and the linemen, usually off the line. Slot receivers work in the middle of the field against linebackers and safeties, so they tend to be quick, sure-handed and good at getting open quickly in traffic rather than outrunning a cornerback over forty yards.

Treat the letters as a dialect, not a law. In some offenses Y is a slot receiver rather than a tight end, F is the fullback in one system and the slot in another, U is often a second tight end, and many teams flip X and Z by formation. The letter names a spot, not a person, which is this chapter’s big idea in miniature. The full vocabulary, and the older names (split end for X, flanker for Z), belong to Formation Rules and the Language of Alignment.

Receivers are the closest thing football has to sprinters: around 6-foot-1 and 200 pounds on average, with a typical combine 40 of about 4.5 seconds.

The tight end: half lineman, half receiver

The tight end (Y) is the most interesting position on the offense, because he’s two positions at once. In the classic alignment he lines up on the line of scrimmage right next to a tackle, “tight” to the line, as opposed to a split end out wide. From there he can do either of two very different jobs: block a defensive end or linebacker like a sixth lineman, or release into a route like a receiver.

That’s exactly why he matters to the defense. When a wide receiver comes on the field, the defense can count on a pass threat; when an extra lineman comes on, it can count on a run. A tight end who can do both forces the defense to defend both. Bodies show it: tight ends average about 6-foot-4 and 250 pounds, between a lineman and a receiver, and run a 40 in about 4.7 seconds: slower than a receiver, and about as fast as the linebackers who usually cover him. His edge over a linebacker isn’t speed. It’s that he’s a trained route runner with a receiver’s hands and a bigger catch radius, against a run defender trying to cover.

The side the tight end lines up on is so important that it has a name, the strong side of the formation (the other is the weak side). As you’ll see in a moment, several defensive positions are named for which side of the tight end they line up on.

Travis Kelce of the Chiefs, a third-round pick in 2013, is the tight end of his era. He had seven straight 1,000-yard receiving seasons from 2016 to 2022, a record for a tight end (no other tight end had more than four 1,000-yard seasons at the time).10 Watch him and you’ll see the position’s whole point: on one snap he’s in-line blocking a defensive end, on the next he’s split out alone, and the defense has to decide who covers a 255-pounder who runs routes like a receiver.

His older brother, Jason Kelce, was the Eagles’ center from 2011 to 2023, which makes the Kelces a nice demonstration of this chapter’s theme: two brothers, one family’s genes, two completely different jobs. At the combine Travis weighed 255 pounds and Jason, an undersized center, 280. In Super Bowl LVII (February 2023) they played against each other: the Chiefs beat the Eagles 38–35.11

Skill positions, eligible receivers and who may catch a pass

Football broadcasters and fantasy players talk about the skill positions: quarterback, running back, wide receiver and tight end, the players who handle the ball. (It’s a slightly insulting name, as any offensive lineman will tell you, because blocking is a skill too.) The skill positions overlap closely with a rule that matters a great deal to the strategy of the game: not everyone on the offense is allowed to catch a forward pass.

The rules start with how the offense must line up:12

  1. At least seven players must be on the line of scrimmage at the snap. So at most four are off it, in the backfield.
  2. Of the players on the line, only the two on the ends are eligible receivers, allowed to catch a forward pass.
  3. Every back is eligible, and remember what “back” means here: anyone lined up off the line, whether he’s a running back, a receiver a yard off the ball or the quarterback. There is one exception: a quarterback who takes the snap hand-to-hand under center. A quarterback in the shotgun is just another back and may catch a pass. The Eagles won Super Bowl LII (February 2018) partly on that rule: on fourth-and-goal, quarterback Nick Foles walked up behind his right tackle as if changing the call, the snap went straight to the running back, and tight end Trey Burton threw it to the uncovered Foles for a touchdown, the “Philly Special.”13
  4. Everyone else is an ineligible receiver: the interior linemen, any player covered up inside the end of the line, and anyone wearing a number from 50 to 79 who hasn’t told the referee before the play that he’s reporting as eligible.

Notice something about that picture: the X receiver and the tight end are on the line, and the Z and the slot receiver are a yard behind it. Those one-yard differences aren’t style. Every receiver’s spot is a choice about who is on the line, and so who is eligible. Offenses use that on purpose, and officials watch for it. (Formation Rules works through covered receivers, tackles who report as eligible, and the trick formations built on both.)

A related rule you’ll hear about on run-pass plays: ineligible linemen may not go more than one yard downfield before a forward pass is thrown. That rule, and why it shapes modern offenses, is in Run-Pass Options.

ImportantWhy it exists: eligibility

The line-up rule came first, and its target wasn’t the pass. When college football rewrote its rules in 1910, after years of deaths and injuries, it required seven offensive players on the line of scrimmage, to stop the “mass plays” in which a whole team built a moving wedge around the ball carrier: with only four men behind the line, as the rule-makers put it, “the weight of attack is necessarily reduced.”14

Once seven men had to stand on the line, the rules had to say which of them could catch the newly legal forward pass, and the answer (the two ends and the backs) gives the game its balance. The five interior men become pure blockers, so the offense can’t flood the field with receivers; and because eligibility is announced by where players stand, the defense can see who might get the ball before the snap. That’s why the line-up rules are so fussy: a one-yard step changes who can catch the ball.

The NFL has assigned number ranges by position since 1973,15 and the eligibility rule leans on them: a number is something an official, or a defender, can check at a glance.

The system loosened twice recently. In 2021, owners approved a Kansas City proposal letting running backs, receivers, tight ends, linebackers and defensive backs wear single digits (the Chiefs had run short of numbers in the 20–49 range, four of which the franchise had retired).16 In 2023, a Philadelphia proposal added the number 0 for everyone except linemen and let kickers and punters wear 0–49 and 90–99, freeing up single digits for everyone else.17 So you can no longer tell a running back from a cornerback by number alone; a #1 could be either.

But look at what never changed: 50–79 is still the lineman block. On offense, a player in that range is ineligible unless he reports. When you see a broadcast cut to the referee announcing “number 70 is reporting as eligible,” the offense is about to use a sixth lineman as a blocking tight end, and the officials are telling the defense, as the rules require.

Numbers are also how you’ll identify players on the field when the broadcast doesn’t help: find the 50s–70s to locate the line, the 90s to find defensive linemen and many edge rushers, and the single digits and 20s–40s out wide to find receivers and defensive backs.

The defense: front seven and secondary

Now turn around. The defense is easiest to understand as two groups, which you saw bracketed in the first diagram:

  • The front seven: the defensive linemen and the linebackers, the defenders who line up near the ball and play the run first.
  • The secondary: the cornerbacks and safeties, the “defensive backs,” who line up farther away and are responsible first for the pass.

That split is a trade-off the defense makes on every play. Defenders near the ball are quick to the runner but poorly placed to cover a receiver thirty yards downfield; defenders far away are the reverse. A coordinator’s most basic decision is how many to put in the box (the area around the ball where runs are fought: from just outside one end of the offensive line to just outside the other, and about five to seven yards deep) and how many to keep back. The number standing in the box at the snap is the box count, which A First Look at Defense teaches you to take.

Defensive line: tackles, the nose and ends

The defensive linemen line up within a yard or so of the ball, in a three- or four-point stance (one or two hands on the ground), across from the offensive linemen. Their job is the mirror image of the offensive line’s: on runs, hold their ground, fill their gap (the space between two offensive linemen that the defense assigns them), and shed blocks to make tackles; on passes, get to the quarterback.

  • The defensive tackles (T) line up inside, across from the guards and center. They are the heaviest defenders, and in a four-man line the two of them usually split the work. One lines up shaded on the center and is built to absorb blockers, often two at once, so the linebackers behind him can run free. The other, the 3-technique, lines up on a guard’s outside shoulder and is built to shoot through a gap and rush the passer; he is usually the more valuable of the two. Either way, a good one collapses the pocket (the protected space the blockers form around the quarterback) from the inside, which is worse for a quarterback than pressure from the outside: his escape from an edge rusher is to step forward, and an inside rush takes that away.
  • The nose tackle (N) is the defensive tackle who lines up on (or nearly on) the center. In a defense with three linemen he’s the one in the middle, directly across from the center, and he’s usually the biggest man on the defense. The Eagles’ Jordan Davis, a nose tackle drafted in 2022, measured 6-foot-6 and 341 pounds at the combine and then ran the 40 in 4.78 seconds, as fast as the typical quarterback.18
  • The defensive ends (E) line up on the outside, across from or outside the offensive tackles. They’re lighter and faster than tackles, because their two jobs both happen in open space: rush the passer around the edge, and on runs, keep the ball carrier from getting outside them.

Where exactly each lineman stands has its own vocabulary. A lineman is head-up when he’s directly across from a blocker and shaded when he’s offset onto one of the blocker’s shoulders, and coaches number those spots as techniques (“the 3” is the guard’s outside shoulder; “the 1” is a shade on the center). The Language of the Run Game introduces the numbers and Defensive Line Play deepens them.

Aaron Donald, who played for the Rams from 2014 until he retired after the 2023 season, is the archetype of a defensive tackle who broke the mold. At 6-foot-1 and about 285 pounds he was an undersized 3-technique, and teams worried about it before the draft. Then he ran a 4.68 40 at the combine, a time you’d expect from a linebacker. He became the most disruptive interior lineman of his era, three times Defensive Player of the Year.19 He’s useful to remember for a simple reason: he shows what the “size” norms in this chapter are for. They’re not rules, they’re the average solutions to a physics problem, and a player who solves the problem differently (with quickness instead of mass) can be better than all of them.

Edge rushers

The edge rusher is the modern name for the defender whose main job is getting to the quarterback from outside the offensive tackle. You’ll hear “edge” constantly now, and the reason is that the old position names stopped fitting. A 4-3 defense (four linemen, three linebackers, the one in our first picture) calls its edge rushers defensive ends, and they usually put a hand on the ground. A 3-4 defense (three linemen, four linebackers) uses its two outside linebackers as the edge rushers, usually standing up, and its two inside linebackers split the Mike’s job between them. Same job, two names, so the league’s vocabulary converged on “edge.” Even the Scouting Combine created an “EDGE” workout group in 2018 instead of choosing between DE and OLB.20

Edge rushers are among the most valuable players in football, because pass pressure wrecks offenses and the edge is where most of it comes from. T.J. Watt of the Steelers is the model: technically an outside linebacker in a 3-4, 6-foot-4 and 252 pounds at his combine, he tied the single-season sack record with 22.5 in 2021, and that record stood until Myles Garrett, a 4-3 defensive end in Cleveland, got to 23 in 2025.21 How edge rushers win (and why “pressures” matter more than sacks) is the subject of The Pass Rush.

Linebackers: Mike, Will and Sam

The linebackers line up behind the defensive line, usually four or five yards off the ball: the “second level” of the defense. They are the defense’s all-purpose players. On a run, they read the play and fill the gap the runner is heading for; on a pass, they drop into coverage on the short middle of the field or cover a back or tight end; and sometimes they blitz, rushing the quarterback themselves. Their bodies are a compromise between the size to take on a fullback and the speed to cover a running back.

In a defense with three linebackers, each has a one-letter name, and the classic rule is that the letters come from where they line up relative to the offense’s tight end:

  • The Mike linebacker (M) is the middle linebacker. He’s usually the defense’s signal-caller, often the one with the radio in his helmet (the “green dot,” which What a Play Really Is explains), and he’s the player you see pointing and shouting adjustments before the snap. The 49ers’ Fred Warner, a third-round pick in 2018, is the modern ideal: a Mike who runs and covers like a safety.
  • The Sam linebacker (S) is the strong-side linebacker: he lines up on the tight end’s side, and if the tight end moves to the other side before the snap, the Sam goes with him. He’s usually the biggest and most physical of the three, because he has to take on a tight end’s block. He’s also usually the first linebacker to leave the field when the offense spreads out, as you’ll see in a moment.
  • The Will linebacker (W) is the weak-side linebacker, away from the tight end. With fewer blockers on his side, he’s often the hardest linebacker for the offense to reach: someone has to climb off the line or come from the backfield to get to him, and plays to his side often have to account for him specially. So the Will tends to be the fastest of the three, a chaser who runs plays down from behind.

What if there’s no tight end, or two? Then the defense needs another rule for “strong,” and teams differ: the side with more receivers (the passing strength), the wide side of the field, or the side the running back is on. Often the Mike simply declares the strength with a call before the snap, and everyone, the strong safety included, sets to it. Formation Rules lays out the options as formation strength; for now, the tight end rule gets you through most first-and-10 snaps.

These letters are a dialect too: a 3-4 team may call its weak-side edge the “Jack” and its second inside linebacker the “Mo,” a slot defender may be the “Star,” and a sixth defensive back the “Money” (this book’s $ tag for the slot corner is the same pun). Linebackers’ reads and keys are the subject of Linebackers.

NoteMadden vs. real life

In a video game, a position is a slot on a depth chart: put a player at “LB” and the game stands him where linebackers go, every snap. Real defenses name spots, and then decide how players fill them. Some keep the same player at Sam and flip him to the strength wherever it is. Others play field and boundary: one linebacker always takes the wide side of the field and another the short side, so a player stays put while his letter changes with the formation. Either way, learn the positions as jobs defined relative to the offense, not as fixed spots on your screen.

The secondary: cornerbacks, the slot corner and safeties

The defensive backs are the lightest, fastest players on the defense, and the ones who stand between a receiver and the end zone.

  • The cornerbacks (CB) line up across from the outside wide receivers, near the sidelines, anywhere from right in the receiver’s face (“press”) to eight or nine yards off. Their job is pass coverage, either following a receiver wherever he goes (man coverage) or guarding an area of the field (zone). Corners are smaller than receivers on average and just as fast: the typical combine 40 is about 4.5 seconds, tied with receivers for the fastest on the field. The hardest thing in their job is that they must react: the receiver knows where he’s going and the corner has to mirror him running backward or sideways.
  • The slot cornerback ($), also called the nickel back, covers the slot receiver. He lines up inside, between the outside cornerback and the box, so he’s part cornerback and part linebacker: he has to cover quick receivers in the middle of the field and tackle runners and blitz off the edge. We’ll see where he comes from below.
  • The safeties line up deepest, usually 10 to 15 yards off the ball: the last line of defense against long passes and long runs. Most defenses have two:
    • the free safety (FS), who usually plays deeper and in the middle, “free” of responsibility for a particular receiver so that he can go wherever the ball goes; and
    • the strong safety (SS), who lines up on the strong side, often a bit closer to the line, and helps more against the run.

How many safeties stay deep before the snap (one or two) is the most important single clue you’ll learn to read on defense. A First Look at Defense starts that habit, and Coverage Fundamentals builds it out.

Two substitutions happened there, one on each side, and they’re connected. The offense took off its fullback and put on a third receiver. If the defense stayed in its base personnel, somebody would have to line up over that receiver in the slot, and the only candidates are linebackers: a 235-pound run defender against a quick, 190-pound receiver in open space, a matchup offenses hunt for. So the defense takes off a linebacker and puts on a fifth defensive back, the slot corner, who can run with him wherever the slot is. The linebacker who leaves is usually the Sam, the biggest and least coverage-minded of the three, even when the slot is on the other side: the remaining two shift over, and the slot corner goes to the slot. Notice the picture also keeps a defender outside the tight end. With the Sam gone, the defensive end slides out over him, so a run around the right end still has someone to beat.

That fifth defensive back has a name you’ll hear in every broadcast: the defense is in nickel (five defensive backs: a nickel is five cents). On obvious passing downs some defenses go further and play a sixth defensive back, dime (two nickels). The offensive grouping has a name too: one back and one tight end is 11 personnel. Both systems of shorthand get full chapters: Personnel for the offense’s numbers and A First Look at Defense for the defense’s packages (“base,” nickel, dime).

How common is this picture? In 2025, about three NFL plays in five had exactly one back and one tight end on the field, by far the most common grouping,22 and defenses were in their “base” package (with three linebackers) on under 30% of plays.23 In practical terms the slot cornerback is a starter on every defense, even though he isn’t one of the eleven on the “base” diagram. Not every fifth defensive back is a cornerback, though: some defenses use a third safety instead, a bigger player who can also fit against the run (the big nickel).

Notice also what the defense calls its front now: four linemen and two linebackers make six, not seven. “Front seven” is a habit of language from the base-defense era. In nickel, the seventh man in the front on run downs is whoever the defense brings near the ball, and the choice tells you what it fears. A safety walking down into the box before the snap says “we expect a run, and we’ll pay for it in pass coverage”; both safeties staying deep with only six in the box says “pass first, and we dare you to run.”

  • The kicker (K) kicks field goals, extra points and (on most teams) kickoffs. NFL kickers are specialists in the purest sense: they practice one motion and are judged on it in front of 70,000 people.
  • The punter (P) punts the ball away on fourth down, standing about 15 yards behind the line so that rushers can’t reach him before he kicks. Punters are judged on distance and hang time (how long the ball stays in the air), because hang time gives the coverage team time to get downfield.
  • The long snapper (LS) snaps the ball on punts, field goals and extra points. Why not just use the starting center? Because it’s a different skill. A center hands the ball up or tosses it five yards to a quarterback who’s waiting for it; a long snapper, head down and looking backward between his legs, has to fire a tight spiral seven yards to a holder or fifteen to a punter in well under a second, with the laces landing where they should, and then block. On a field goal, the snap, the hold and the kick together are drilled to take about 1.3 seconds.25 A bad snap is the most catastrophic error in the kicking game, which is why every team keeps a dedicated one, and the rules protect him: on a punt or kick, no defender within a yard of the line may line up directly over the snapper.26
  • The holder catches the snap on a field goal or extra point and sets the ball on its point for the kicker. On most NFL teams the holder is the punter: he has the best hands for a long snap among the specialists, and he’s on the field anyway.
  • The returner catches kickoffs and punts and tries to run them back. Returners are usually fast backs, receivers or defensive backs.
  • The gunner is the punt team’s missile: one of two players split out wide whose only job is to sprint downfield and tackle the returner. The return team answers by putting one or two blockers on each gunner, called vices or jammers, to hold him up at the line (Field Goals, Extra Points, and the Punting Game has the whole unit).

Read the top panel from left to right and it sorts the field into families:

  • The speed family (left, 190–215 lb): cornerbacks, wide receivers, safeties, running backs, and, interestingly, kickers, who are built like defensive backs.
  • The hybrids (middle, 220–265 lb): quarterbacks, linebackers, fullbacks, tight ends and edge rushers, plus the long snappers. The bottom panel shows the price of mass: weight and 40 time fall along nearly a straight line (dashed). Each extra 20 pounds costs roughly a tenth of a second over 40 yards, and the positions sit along that line wherever their job puts the balance between force and speed. Edge rushers sit a little above the line (fast for their size), which is why they’re so hard to block; Aaron Donald sits far above it, about three-tenths of a second faster than a typical 285-pounder, which is why he was Aaron Donald.

Why does this matter to a viewer? Because body type is how you’ll recognize positions when the alignment is ambiguous, and how you’ll spot the mismatches offenses and defenses hunt for. A 250-pound tight end covered by a 190-pound cornerback is a mismatch in one direction (the tight end can shield the ball with his body); the same tight end covered by a 240-pound linebacker is a mismatch in the other (the tight end runs routes and catches like a receiver; the linebacker is a run defender trying to cover). Half of play-calling is creating one of those pictures and the other half is avoiding them. And the size bands explain one of the oldest jokes in football analysis: “he’s a tweener,” a player whose body sits between two families and doesn’t clearly fit either job. Some tweeners never find a role; others (Kyle Hamilton, below) become the most valuable players on their team because they can do both jobs.

How much each position is worth, in salary and draft picks, is a separate and fascinating question for Building a Roster.

Position, alignment, assignment

Everything so far has treated a position as if it told you what a player does. It doesn’t, quite. To read a play, you have to separate three different things:

  • Position is who the player is: what the roster says, what he’s built for, which meeting room he sits in during the week. Kyle Hamilton is a safety.
  • Alignment is where he lines up on this play: his spot, depth and relation to the ball and the other players. Hamilton might be 12 yards deep, over the slot receiver, or standing in the box beside the linebackers.
  • Assignment is what he’s been told to do on this play: which gap he fills, which receiver or area he covers, whether he rushes. From any of those alignments, Hamilton might blitz, cover the tight end man-to-man, or drop into a deep half of the field.

Why does the difference matter? Because football is a game of disguise and recognition, and the gap between these three is where the disguise lives. A defense that lines a safety up deep and then sends him on a blitz has used alignment to hide assignment. An offense that lines its running back up as a wide receiver is forcing the defense to decide: do we cover him with a linebacker (who’s good against running backs) or a cornerback (who’s good against receivers)? Everything in this book about reading the game comes down to using alignment to guess assignment before the snap, and then checking yourself after it.

It’s also why rosters are more fluid than position labels suggest:

  • The 49ers’ Deebo Samuel, a wide receiver, was lined up in the backfield and handed the ball so often in 2021 that he called himself a “wide back”; his eight rushing touchdowns that season were the most by a receiver since 1970.29
  • Juszczyk, the film-room fullback, spends much of his time lined up as a tight end or a receiver.
  • Many defenses play a safety at linebacker on passing downs, list their best edge rusher as a “linebacker,” or use a “safety” who plays almost every down near the line, and teams increasingly don’t care what the roster calls him.

Even the official data struggles with this: the participation data behind the two-backs chart lists each player by his roster position, so it records who’s on the field, not where he stood.

NoteFilm room: Ravens 2022–2025 — Kyle Hamilton, one position, every alignment

The Ravens drafted Kyle Hamilton 14th overall in 2022.30 He’s a safety, and an unusual one: 6-foot-4 and 220 pounds at his combine, a height only about one safety in forty measured there since 2010 has matched, built more like a big receiver or a small linebacker. Some evaluators worried about where he’d fit.

Baltimore’s answer was: everywhere. Watch the Ravens’ defense and find number 14 before every snap. On one play he’s a deep safety; on the next he’s lined up over the slot like a nickel back (when he does, the Ravens are playing a big nickel); on the next he’s in the box beside the linebackers, or walked up on the line over a tight end. He blitzes, covers tight ends man-to-man, and plays deep zones, so an offense can’t tell from his position what he’s about to do, and has trouble telling from his alignment. That flexibility is the whole value: one player who can be a safety, a slot corner and a linebacker lets the defense change its shape without changing its personnel, which means the offense can’t use substitutions to force a mismatch. It has made him a first-team All-Pro twice, for 2023 and 2025.31

What to notice: before the snap, where is 14, and does he move? If he starts deep and creeps down late, something is coming. If he starts in the box and races back to the deep middle at the snap, the defense just changed its coverage shell (how many safeties play deep) on you. You’ll learn to name those moves in Disguise, Rotation, and Split-Field Coverage.

Go through it one position at a time, the way you’ll learn to watch a replay:

  • Offensive line: each lineman blocks the man in front of him, except that the left guard and center start by double-teaming the tackle over the center (a combo block), and then the center comes off it to block the Mike.
  • Quarterback: opens, hands off, and (on a real play) carries out a fake so a defender has to think about him for half a second.
  • Fullback: leads into the hole and blocks the first linebacker who shows (here, the Will). This one-on-one is the play’s namesake: the fullback is “isolated” on the Will.
  • Tailback: takes the handoff, stays a couple of yards behind the fullback, and cuts off his block.
  • Tight end and receivers: block. The tight end takes the Sam on the far side, and the receivers’ blocks on the cornerbacks are what turn a five-yard gain into a long one if the runner breaks a tackle.
  • Defensive linemen: take on their blockers and try not to get moved.
  • Linebackers: read the run and fill. The Will takes on the fullback; the Mike is picked up by the center coming off the double-team; the Sam is blocked by the tight end.
  • Safeties: the two players nobody blocks. The free safety reads run and fills the alley (the lane between the blocks and the sideline), and he’s the man the tailback has to beat.
  • Cornerbacks: play their receivers until they’re sure it’s a run.

Now count. The quarterback and the ball carrier don’t block, so the offense has nine blockers for eleven defenders: two defenders always go unblocked, and the offense’s only choice is which two. The usual answer is the two farthest from the hole, here the safeties, because by the time they arrive the runner should be five yards downfield at full speed (coaches call that “blocked to the safety”). The last frame shows exactly that: the play has gained its yards, and whether it gains more depends on one tackle in space. Which defenders the offense leaves alone, and how it makes them wrong, runs through the whole run game (The Language of the Run Game), sets up the box count, and becomes Option Football, where the offense reads an unblocked defender instead of blocking him.

TipWatch for it on Sunday: the one-player game

For one full drive, watch only the center. Ignore the ball. On every snap, say out loud what he did:

  • Before the snap: does he point at a defender or shout something? (He’s naming the defender the blocking scheme counts from.) Does the quarterback point first and the center echo him, or the other way round?
  • At the snap: under center or shotgun? A shotgun snap is a five-yard toss; watch whether the center’s head comes up fast.
  • After the snap: did he block the man over him, help a guard with a double-team, climb to a linebacker, or (on a screen pass, a short throw to a receiver or back with blockers set up in front of him) release downfield to block in space? Did he ever touch the ball again? (He shouldn’t.)

Then, if you have time for more:

  • Count the defensive backs on first-and-10. Four means a base defense; five means nickel, usually a slot corner and sometimes a third safety. The safeties are often off the top of your screen before the snap, so you may have to count them on the replay (How to Watch a Broadcast shows where to look).
  • Find the tight end before the snap and predict which side the Sam linebacker and the strong safety will line up on. Most teams set to him; some set to the wide side of the field instead, and spotting which one a defense does is a real scouting clue.
  • On a punt, find the gunners before the snap and count how many defenders the return team puts on each: one or two?
  • Check a jersey. When a player in the 50s–70s lines up somewhere unusual (out at tight end, in the backfield), listen for the referee’s announcement that he’s reporting as eligible.

The run worry is the strong side. The tight end gives the offense an extra blocker on the left, so more gaps to fill there; that’s exactly why the defense puts its biggest linebacker and its run-support safety on that side. The obvious follow-up: then why doesn’t the offense just run the other way, at the lighter weak side? It does, often, and that’s why the Will has to be the fastest linebacker on the field: the weak side is light by design, and he’s the one who has to cover the ground. If the tight end flipped to the right before the snap, all of those names would flip with him. Watching the Sam move when the tight end moves is one of the first live “reads” you can make.

How many defensive linemen, linebackers and defensive backs are on the field? What does that tell you about what the defense expects, and which defensive players have left the field compared with the base defense?

TipAnswer

Four linemen, one linebacker, six defensive backs. The four squares on the line are the defensive linemen. Only one defender stands in the middle at linebacker depth, inside the box. The other six are defensive backs, and each is lined up on a receiver or deep: a cornerback on each outside receiver, one defender over each of the two inside receivers on the right, and two safeties deep.

Six defensive backs is called dime (two nickels, so to speak), a package taught in A First Look at Defense. Compared with the base 4-3, two linebackers have left, almost certainly the Sam and one of the others, replaced by two extra defensive backs. The linebacker who stays is usually the best cover linebacker, often the Mike or the Will. The defense is telling you it expects a pass: on third-and-9 the offense needs nine yards, a run rarely gets that, and the defense wants as many fast cover players on the field as it can afford.

A caution: the sixth defensive back doesn’t always stand out wide. Many teams put him at linebacker depth inside the box as a “dime backer,” and then you’d spot him by his size and number (a 200-pounder in the 20s or 30s), not by where he stands. And the price of dime is that only five defenders (four linemen and one linebacker) are near the ball, so a draw (a run disguised as a pass, with the quarterback dropping back before he hands off) or a quarterback run is the offense’s natural counter, and you’ll see it on third-and-long more than you’d expect.

Which players may catch a forward pass on this play? The tight end, 87, catches one ten yards downfield. Will there be a flag?

TipAnswer

Eligible: 11, 13, 84, 23 and the quarterback, 9. Not eligible: the five linemen and the tight end, 87. Yes, a flag.

Count the line. Eight players are on it: 11, the five linemen, 87 and 13. (Eight is legal; the rule is at least seven.) Only the two ends of that line are eligible, and the ends are 11 on the left and 13 on the right, because 13 is lined up on the line outside the tight end. That “covers” 87: he’s on the line but not at the end of it, so on this play he’s an ineligible receiver despite being a tight end with an eligible number. The officials will throw a flag: an ineligible player may not catch a forward pass (and, ten yards downfield, he was also well past the one yard an ineligible player is allowed). The penalties themselves are in Contact Rules and Penalties; the catch itself is illegal touching of a forward pass, a 5-yard foul.32

The backs are 84 (a yard off the line), 23, and the quarterback. The quarterback is eligible here, because he’s in the shotgun rather than taking a hand-to-hand snap under center, so he counts as a back, exactly as Nick Foles did on the Philly Special.

This is why the rules care so much about a one-yard step. Move 13 off the line, and 87 becomes the end and eligible again. Offenses use exactly this kind of adjustment to change who’s eligible, and the defense has to notice. More in Formation Rules and the Language of Alignment.

Takeaways

  • Football has three teams on one roster: offense, defense, special teams. Unlimited substitution (permanent in the NFL since 1950) let each player specialize, which is why bodies now match jobs so closely.
  • Offense: five linemen (center, guards, tackles) who block and may not catch passes; a quarterback; one or two backs (running back, sometimes a fullback); and pass-catchers split between wide receivers (X, Z, slot) and the tight end, who is half blocker and half receiver, and whose side names the strong side.
  • Eligibility is a rule about alignment: seven on the line, and only the two ends and the backs (anyone off the line) may catch a pass, never a quarterback under center. Jersey numbers 50–79 mark the ineligible linemen.
  • Defense: a front seven of linemen (tackles, nose, ends/edge rushers) and linebackers (Mike, Sam on the tight end side, Will away from it), and a secondary of cornerbacks, safeties and, in the modern default, a slot cornerback. The closer a defender stands to the ball, the more he’s built for the run.
  • Special teams have their own specialists: kicker, punter, long snapper, holder, returner, gunner.
  • Position, alignment, assignment are three different things: who he is, where he stands, what he does. Reading the game means using alignment to guess assignment, and the best players (and coaches) blur the three on purpose.
