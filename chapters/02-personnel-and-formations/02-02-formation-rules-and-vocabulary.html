Formation Rules and the Language of Alignment

Seven on the line, one strong side, five letters and three quarterback depths: the rules and the words that turn eleven players standing still into a readable picture.

Personnel told you who is on the field. This chapter is about where they stand: the rules every alignment must obey, and the vocabulary coaches use to name it. The next two chapters use both to build actual formations (Classic Formations and Spread Formations).

Seven on the line: the rule underneath every formation

You met the core of it in The Twenty-Two: at least seven offensive players on the line of scrimmage at the snap; only the two ends of the line and the backs are eligible receivers (the quarterback under center excepted); everyone else is an ineligible receiver. This chapter asks the questions underneath that rule. What does “on the line” mean, to the inch? What happens when a formation breaks the rule? And why would a coach ever want one of his own receivers to be ineligible?

On the line, off the line, and the gap in between

Every offensive player at the snap is in exactly one of two places, and the rulebook defines both.1

  • On the line means roughly level with the center. The rule’s test is the helmet: a player is on the line if he faces the defense with his shoulders square and his helmet breaks an imaginary vertical wall, parallel to the line, that passes through the center’s waist. Linemen crouch right up against the ball, so they’re obviously on it. A wide receiver thirty yards away has to stand on it, which is harder to judge.
  • Off the line means in the backfield, at least one yard behind the line of scrimmage. For the offense, the line effectively starts at the back tip of the ball: the ball’s own length, the neutral zone, belongs to neither team, and no offensive player may have any part of his body in it. Every back is off the line: the running back, the fullback, a receiver standing a yard deep, a tight end set as a wing. The single exception is the quarterback under center, who has to be closer than a yard to take the ball from the center’s hands.

The strip between those two zones is no-man’s land. A player standing half a yard behind the line is neither on it nor in the backfield, and the formation is illegal. On a crowded line nobody gets stuck there by accident. But a receiver thirty yards from the ball, looking in at a center he can barely see, can easily end up half a step off. That’s what the receiver in the opening scene was doing: asking the official on his side of the field, by pointing at himself, whether he was on or off. The wing officials (the line judge and the down judge, one on each sideline) watch the receivers on their side for exactly this, and receivers routinely check with them before the snap. The official usually answers with a word or a wave, but he doesn’t have to: he may be busy counting the offense, and the alignment is the receiver’s responsibility.2

Can you see it from your couch? Sometimes. The standard broadcast camera sits high on the sideline, so compare the receiver’s helmet with the helmets of the linemen nearest him: level means on, a visible step back means off. On a replay from the end-zone camera it’s obvious. But you rarely need to judge it by eye, because the count does the work: find the tight end, and the rule tells you which receiver has to be on the line, as the next sections show.

NoteMadden vs. real life

In Madden you can’t line up illegally. You pick a formation from a menu, and the game puts every player on a legal spot. Real formations are built by eleven people walking to marks they memorised, with the play clock running, a crowd screaming and a receiver who can’t hear the quarterback. Every snap, the officials check the count and the depths, and the defense checks them too, because the alignment tells it who can catch the ball. A one-yard mistake is a flag, and a one-yard choice is a clue.

Why seven? The rule is older than the forward pass as we know it. In the early 1900s, college football’s signature plays were “mass” plays: wedges and pile-drives in which most of the offense lined up behind the ball and crashed into one spot with a running start. Players were being badly hurt and killed, and in 1905 the sport came close to being banned. The rule makers spent the next few years reshaping the game, and by 1910 they required seven men on the line of scrimmage, so that most of the offense had to start from a standstill on the line instead of building momentum behind it.3 The seven-man line was a safety rule. Its modern job is different: it guarantees that the offense always has five big, ineligible blockers in the middle, and it forces the offense to declare its receivers by where they stand.

None of this binds the defense. On a scrimmage play it may line up anyone anywhere on its own side of the neutral zone, in any number, and move around before the snap; its only alignment rule is to stay out of the neutral zone.4 That asymmetry runs through the whole book: the offense has to show its hand by rule, and the defense gets to disguise.

When an alignment breaks those rules, the officials throw a flag for illegal formation: a five-yard penalty on the offense for lining up wrongly at the snap. The common versions are the ones in the next picture: fewer than seven players on the line, or a player stuck in no-man’s land.

Notice what panel 3 says about the word “illegal.” Covering your own tight end is not an illegal formation. Eight on the line is fine; the rule says at least seven. The formation is legal, but the tight end can’t catch a forward pass on that play. If the ball is thrown to him anyway, that’s a different foul, an ineligible player touching a pass, and the penalties are in Contact Rules and Penalties.

CautionCommon misconception

“An unusual-looking formation must be illegal.” Almost never. Eight men on the line is legal. Two receivers stacked one behind the other is legal. Two tackles side by side on one side of the center is legal (that’s an unbalanced line, the subject of Spread Formations). A lineman standing at the end of the line and catching a pass is legal, if he reported (below). The legality test is short: seven or more on the line, nobody in no-man’s land, no more than one man moving at the snap (the motion rules from The Pre-Snap Phase), and the right people eligible. Weird is allowed. That’s why offenses get away with so much.

Covered receivers, and why X stands on the line

A covered receiver is a player who is on the line of scrimmage but not at either end of it, because a teammate is also on the line outside him. He’s ineligible on that play, whatever his jersey number and whatever his position. In panel 3 above, the tight end was covered by the Z receiver.

Here’s the elegant part. The covered-receiver rule explains the most basic habit in receiver alignment, one you saw in The Twenty-Two without a reason: the X receiver lines up on the line, and the Z receiver lines up a yard off it.

Look again at panel 1. The formation has a tight end attached to the right side of the line. If the receiver on that side also stood on the line, he would cover the tight end, and the offense’s most versatile player would be a blocker only. So the receiver on the tight end’s side (Z) stands a yard back. That keeps Y as the right end and eligible, and as a bonus it makes Z a back, which means Z is the one who can go in motion (only an off-the-line player may move at the snap). On the other side there’s no tight end, so somebody has to be the left end, and someone has to be the seventh man on the line. That’s X’s job: he stands on the line so the formation has seven and an end on that side. The letters didn’t come first. The rule did, and the letters name the jobs it creates.

The same logic places the slot receiver (H in panel 1). If H stood on the line inside X, X would cover him. So the general rule is: on each side, only the outermost man on the line can catch a pass, and anyone inside him who wants to run a route has to stand a yard back. That’s why slot receivers, wings and backs are all “off the ball”.

ImportantWhy it exists: covering on purpose

Why would an offense choose to cover one of its own eligible receivers, as in panel 3? Three common reasons.

  • To free someone else to move. Only a back may be in motion at the snap. Putting one receiver on the line (and covering the tight end) can let a different receiver line up a yard back and go in motion.
  • To build a formation the defense hasn’t seen. A tight end who is covered is a sixth blocker. On a pass play he may not go more than a yard past the line before the ball is thrown (that would be an ineligible man downfield, the same rule that holds every lineman back).5 So he can’t really run a route, and if the defense doesn’t notice, it wastes a defender covering him.
  • To change who the defense must account for. Every eligible receiver needs a defender. Making one ineligible, and making another eligible, changes the count.

The cost is the obvious one: a covered player can’t catch the ball, and an alert defense that spots it can treat him as a blocker. Covering is a deliberate trade of one threat for another.

Formation strength: which side is “strong”?

The linebacker in the opening scene was shouting about the formation’s strength. Every formation has two sides, and teams need a shared word for which side is which, because almost everything else (who lines up where, which way the run goes, how the coverage tilts) is described relative to it.

Formation strength is the side of a formation treated as its “strong” side, decided by a rule. The side it names is the strong side; the other is the weak side. Offenses use strength to name formations (“Trips Right” means three receivers to the right, and most formation names carry a “Right” or “Left” like it), and defenses call it out loud before every snap, so all eleven defenders set to the same side. What makes this interesting is that there is more than one rule for deciding it.

Tight-end strength (panel 1) is the oldest rule and the one most fronts still use. The strong side is the side with the tight end, or, with two tight ends, the side with more of them (or with the tight end plus a wing). Why the tight end? Because he is a blocker standing on the line. An attached tight end adds a blocker and adds one more running lane to defend: there’s now a gap outside him that didn’t exist before (gaps are named and numbered in Run Game Language). More blockers and more gaps on one side means the run game is stronger there, so the defense puts more of its run defenders there. That’s why two defensive positions are named for it: the Sam linebacker (“strong-side linebacker”) and the strong safety both line up toward the tight end, as in panel 1. The logic only works while the tight end is attached, standing next to the tackle. Flex him out into the slot, as in panel 3, and he no longer adds a gap or a blocker at the line, so most defenses stop counting him for the front and treat him like any other receiver.

Passing strength (panel 2) counts receivers. The side with more eligible receivers lined up away from the ball, usually the side with three in a 3x1 set (a formation with three receivers on one side and one on the other, built properly in Spread Formations), is the passing strength. Coverage cares about this side, because that’s where the defenders who cover receivers are needed. Coaches number those receivers from the outside in: the outermost is #1, the next #2, the next #3. The count includes the tight end, and it includes a back once he lines up or releases to that side: he becomes the next number in the count, #3 beside two receivers or #4 beside three. A back directly behind the quarterback isn’t counted until he picks a side. You’ll see the numbers in the label map below, and they come back in every coverage chapter.

Field strength (panel 3) ignores the offense entirely. When the ball is on a hash mark, one side of the field (the field side) has about six more yards of room than the other (the boundary side). Room is where offenses like to attack, especially on the edges, so some defenses simply declare the field the strong side every snap. It’s common in college, where the hashes are much wider and the difference between the two sides is about thirteen yards rather than six, and it’s the natural tiebreaker in the NFL when the offense is balanced.

ImportantWhy it exists: one word, eleven players

Strength exists so that eleven defenders can line up correctly in two seconds without discussing it. The defense’s call names the front and the coverage relative to the strength (an “over” front shifts the defensive line toward the strength, an “under” front away from it, both built in Even Fronts; “rotate to strength” sends a safety toward it), so the same call works whichever way the offense lines up. The linebacker shouting “Strong right!” is translating the call for this snap.

The offense knows that, so it plays with the definition. It can put the tight end on one side and the receivers on the other (panel 2), forcing the defense to pick which rule wins. It can line up balanced, so the defense has to fall back on the field. And it can shift its tight end across at the last moment, so that the strength the defense declared is suddenly the weak side, which is exactly the shift you watched in The Pre-Snap Phase. Every one of those moves costs the offense something (time, a simpler play, a predictable look), and it buys a defense that is late or wrong.

Which rule do NFL defenses use? It varies by team and by call, and good defenses use more than one. A common pattern is the one in panel 2: the front sets to the tight end, the coverage to the passing strength, with the field as the tiebreaker when the formation is balanced and has no attached tight end.

Real defenses don’t say “strong right,” which is the textbook version. They use code words, and because the front and the coverage can set to different sides, many staffs keep separate words for each. Nick Saban’s defenses made “Rip” and “Liz” (right and left) famous as the call for the passing strength, and other staffs use “Roscoe” and “Louie” or a number; a call such as “Rita Sky!” can carry the strength and the coverage in two words.6 The exact words vary by staff, but the idea doesn’t: one word, both strengths, before every snap. Offenses have the matching dialect problem. In many pro-style playbooks the “Right” in a formation’s name tells the tight end where to line up; in many spread playbooks it names the side of the three receivers (Offensive Systems and Languages compares them).

You don’t need to know a defense’s rulebook to use any of this. You only need to name the candidates before the snap and then see which one the defense chose. That is the drill in “Watch for it on Sunday” below.

Here is the alphabet as most NFL playbooks use it. Teams differ, and the differences are noted.

  • The X receiver, or split end, lines up on the line, usually alone on the side away from the tight end. He is the end of the line on that side, which is why the older name calls him an “end” who has “split” away from the tackle. Being on the line has a cost: he can’t go in motion, and a cornerback can line up right in his face and jam him at the snap (playing press, as The Twenty-Two called it). So the X is often a big, strong receiver who can beat that contact, frequently the team’s best.
  • The Z receiver, or flanker, lines up a yard off the line, usually on the tight end’s side. Being off the ball keeps the tight end uncovered, gives Z a little more room against a jam, and makes him the receiver most often sent in motion.
  • The Y receiver is, in most NFL offenses, the main tight end. (Some spread systems use Y for a slot receiver instead, among them the Air Raid, a pass-first college system covered in Offensive Systems and Languages.)
  • H and F are the move players, letters for whoever fills the flexible spots in a given grouping. In 11 personnel, H is usually the third receiver, in the slot. In 12 personnel, H may be a second tight end. In 21 personnel, F is often the fullback (bottom panel). Some teams swap the two letters, and in some systems F is the slot and H the back. When a broadcaster says “the F,” check what body type is standing there.
  • Two more letters fill out the diagrams in this book. Many NFL playbooks call the second tight end U (others use H or F for him), and the running back gets a letter of his own, usually R, T or B depending on the system. This book uses U and R.

The older names come from the era when receivers were converted backs and ends. A “flanker” was originally a halfback flanked out wide of the formation, an innovation of the Los Angeles Rams around 1949–50, who moved halfback Elroy “Crazylegs” Hirsch out wide to catch passes and turned him into one of the great receivers of the decade.7 The “split end” was an end who split away from his tackle. The words stuck even after the jobs became full-time receiver positions.

The spot names complete the vocabulary:

  • The slot is the area between the outside receiver and the end of the line, and the slot receiver is the one who lines up there, off the line (for the reason you saw above: on the line, he’d be covered). The slot is the best place on the field to get open quickly: the receiver can break inside or outside (a “two-way go”), is hard to jam, and is usually covered by a linebacker, a safety or the slot cornerback rather than a team’s best cover man.
  • An in-line tight end is attached to the line, right next to a tackle, where he is both a sixth blocker and an eligible end.
  • An H-back is a tight end (or a fullback-sized player) who lines up in the backfield, just off the tackle’s outside hip, instead of on the line. From there he can lead the runner through a hole (block for him, a step ahead of him), block an edge defender out toward the sideline, or slip into a route. Joe Gibbs made the position famous in Washington in the 1980s; Run Game Language tells that story.
  • A wing is a player lined up a yard off the line just outside the end man, usually a tight end or an H-back. A wing is a back: eligible, free to go in motion, and well placed to block an edge defender or slip into a route.

Under center

Under center means the quarterback stands directly behind the center with his hands under him and takes the snap hand to hand. It was the only way to play quarterback for most of the NFL’s history, and it is still the best way to do two things.

Run downhill. The back starts about seven yards deep, directly behind the quarterback, and takes the handoff already moving forward at speed, with the whole line in front of him and the choice of going left or right still open. Fake the run. On play-action (faking a handoff, then throwing), an under-center quarterback turns his back to the defense to make the fake, and the linebackers see exactly what a real run looks like. That’s the whole point of the fake.

What it gives up: to pass, the quarterback has to drop back three, five or seven steps while the rush comes, and during the first steps of that drop he isn’t looking at the defense at all. From under center he also can’t see much over 300-pound linemen until he gets depth.

Shotgun

In the shotgun, the quarterback stands about five yards behind the center (four to seven, depending on the team and the quarterback) and the center throws the ball back to him through the air. Now the quarterback is already at passing depth when he catches the snap. He sees the whole defense the instant the ball arrives, he can throw quickly without a drop, and he has an extra beat against the pass rush. Those advantages are why the shotgun became the NFL’s default as the league passed more: in the nflverse play-by-play it was on about one run or pass in five in 2006 and about two in three in the 2020s, a story told with the data in Classic Formations.

The shotgun is older than it looks. The San Francisco 49ers under coach Red Hickey introduced it in 1960, with the quarterback set deep to buy time against a fierce pass rush, and it beat the Colts with it before defenses caught up and the formation faded. Tom Landry’s Dallas Cowboys revived it in 1975 as a passing-down weapon for Roger Staubach, and from there it spread.8

What the shotgun gives up is mostly in the run game. The back usually lines up beside the quarterback, not behind him, so he starts moving sideways and takes the handoff going across the formation rather than straight ahead. The snap travels five yards through the air, so it can be fumbled. And the play-action fake is less convincing, because the quarterback never turns his back.

The shotgun has one rules quirk worth knowing. The under-center exception you met at the start of the chapter covers only a quarterback within a yard of the center. A shotgun quarterback is five yards deep, so he is a back like any other, and an eligible receiver.9 That’s what makes trick plays to the quarterback legal. The most famous is the “Philly Special” in Super Bowl LII (February 2018, the 2017 season): Nick Foles walked up behind the right tackle as if to change the play, the ball was snapped directly to running back Corey Clement, tight end Trey Burton took a pitch from him and threw, and Foles, by rule a back, caught the touchdown on fourth-and-goal.10

Pistol

The pistol is the compromise. The quarterback stands about four yards back, a short shotgun, and the running back lines up directly behind him, two or three yards deeper. Nevada coach Chris Ault designed it in 2005, looking for a way to keep the shotgun’s view of the defense and still give his back a straight, downhill run to either side.11 Ault’s Nevada quarterback Colin Kaepernick took it to the NFL, and by 2012 the 49ers and Washington were running pistol option plays at the pro level (Option Football tells that story).

The pistol’s quiet advantage is that it hides the direction of the run. A back offset to one side of a shotgun quarterback tells the defense something about where the handoff can go; a back straight behind the quarterback tells it nothing. That is the answer to the opening scene’s third question: a quarterback at four yards with the back behind him is in the alignment built for running downhill, and, as the numbers below show, NFL teams use it that way.

Offset backs and the sidecar

An offset back is a running back or fullback aligned to one side of the quarterback rather than directly behind him. Under center, a fullback offset a step to one side of the tailback (an “offset I,” named in Classic Formations) leans the play toward that side. In the shotgun, the back nearly always stands to one side, often right beside the quarterback, a spot coaches call the sidecar. Where he stands is a clue, never a guarantee: on many shotgun runs the handoff goes away from the back’s side, with the back crossing in front of the quarterback, and in pass protection he may block his own side or cross the quarterback’s face, depending on the protection call (Pass Protection). Different teams run different rules, which is why your prediction log should track the back’s side for the team you watch.

But the under-center share has been climbing: in the charting data it rose to about a third of all plays in 2025, up from about 27% in 2023.14 As offenses went back under center, the quarterback’s depth became a more useful clue, not a less useful one.

Splits and landmarks: how wide is wide?

A receiver’s split is how far he lines up from the ball or from the formation. You might expect playbooks to give splits in yards (“line up 12 yards from the ball”), and some do for inside players. For wide receivers, most coaches use landmarks painted on the field instead, because the field isn’t symmetric. When the ball is on a hash, a split measured in yards from the ball would put a receiver near the sideline on one side and nowhere near it on the other.

The main landmark is the numbers: the big painted yard-line numbers. On an NFL field they start 12 yards in from each sideline and are two yards tall, so their tops sit about 14 yards from the sideline.15 Receivers align “on the numbers,” “outside the numbers” (closer to the sideline) or “inside the numbers” (closer to the ball), and the numbers are in the same place on every snap, whatever the hash.

Inside receivers use different references. A slot receiver typically “splits the difference” between the end of the line and the outside receiver, and when the ball is on a hash, the other painted landmark, the far hash mark, is a common reference for the slot on the wide side. And outside receivers into the short side usually have a minimum split, a rule such as “never closer than about six yards to the sideline” or “top of the numbers”, so they keep room for a route toward the sideline. Coaches call a split wider than the landmark a plus split and a tighter one a minus split. The details vary by team; the habit of aligning to paint, not to yardage, doesn’t.

From the outside in, the ladder looks like this:

  • Wide (outside the numbers). The receiver is close to the sideline, far from everyone else, with lots of room inside him and very little outside. A cornerback can use the sideline as an extra defender. Wide splits are common on routes that break inside, toward the middle of the field (slants and the like, built in Part 4), and on plays where the offense simply wants that cornerback as far from the ball as possible.
  • On the numbers. The default. It leaves room to break either way, and it’s far enough out that the cornerback has to commit to covering him.
  • Reduced split (inside the numbers). The receiver is closer to the ball than his normal landmark. That creates room outside him, and it puts him closer to the defenders near the ball.
  • Tight (near the formation). Just a few yards outside the end of the line. Tight splits, and formations built from them, are a modern favourite, and they get a full section in Spread Formations.

Why would an offense move a receiver off his landmark? Because a split is a trade of space. A receiver who needs to run toward the sideline (an out, a corner route) needs room outside him, so he reduces his split to create it. A receiver who is going to block a linebacker or a safety on a run play (a “crack” block, taught in the run game) wants to start close to his target, so he reduces too. A receiver who will break inside, or who wants to be one-on-one with a cornerback and no safety close enough to help, widens out.

That’s why splits are tells, the kind you started collecting in How to Watch a Broadcast. A reduced split often means an outside-breaking route or a block on the inside; a very wide split often means an inside route or a one-on-one shot at the cornerback. Defensive backs are coached to read splits for exactly this reason. And offenses know they are being read, so good ones vary splits on purpose, using the same reduced split for a crack block one play and a corner route the next. As with every tell in this book, a split shifts the odds; it never settles them.

CautionCommon misconception

“A split is a distance from the ball.” Look at the ladder again. “On the numbers” is about 17 yards from the ball on the field side and about 11 on the boundary side. The receiver’s alignment rule didn’t change; the ball did. This is why a boundary receiver often looks “tight” to a viewer when he is, by his own landmark, standing exactly where he always stands. Judge a split by the landmarks, not by the gap to the ball.

Reporting eligible: when a lineman becomes a receiver

Every rule so far has been about where players stand. The last one is about who they are, and it’s the rule behind the trick plays you’ll remember.

Offensive linemen wear numbers from 50 to 79, and in The Twenty-Two you saw why: the number tells the officials, at a glance, that the player is ineligible. But the rules give the offense a way to change that for one play. Reporting eligible works like this:16

  1. Before the snap, the player wearing an ineligible number goes to the referee and tells him he is reporting as eligible.
  2. The referee tells the defense. The rule only says he “will inform the defensive team”; in practice he turns to the defense and announces it, and in the NFL he often also says it over his wireless microphone, so the stadium and the broadcast hear “Number 70 is reporting as eligible.” Until he has done that and returned to his position, the ball can’t be snapped. On TV, you’ll usually hear it; if you don’t, the giveaway is a number in the 50s, 60s or 70s standing where a tight end would.
  3. The player then lines up where an eligible player can: at the end of the line, or in the backfield. (Reporting doesn’t make an interior lineman eligible; alignment still decides that.)

The rule works in the other direction too, and that’s where the count comes in. Seven men on the line means two ends and five players between them, and all five must be ineligible. Normally those five are the linemen wearing 50–79. A player wearing an eligible number may fill one of those interior spots, but because his number no longer matches his job, he must report as ineligible.17 That direction will matter in the film room.

When a lineman reports and lines up at the end of the line, he’s an eligible tackle, or a tackle-eligible (you’ll hear both on broadcasts): usually a backup tackle playing the role of a sixth lineman, as in the jumbo grouping from the last chapter.

ImportantWhy it exists: reporting

The numbering system exists so that the defense can tell who may catch the ball by looking. That only works if exceptions are announced. The reporting rule lets an offense use its linemen creatively (a sixth blocker who can also catch a pass, a tackle who sneaks out on the goal line), and in exchange it makes the offense tell the referee, and the referee tell the defense, every time. The defense still has to cover the man; it just can’t be ambushed by him.

Notice the bargain this creates. Panels 2 and 4 of the picture above look identical on the field. What changes is a sentence said to the referee, so a defense that misses the announcement has to treat every jumbo lineman as a possible receiver, which is exactly the doubt an offense wants.

First, 2025 was a jumbo year: about 1,770 six-lineman snaps, close to double 2024, in the same season that offenses went heavier across the board (the personnel shift in Personnel). Second, the extra lineman now reports far more often. The share didn’t climb every year (it dipped in several), but it roughly doubled over the decade, from about two snaps in five in 2016 to about four in five in 2024 and 2025, the highest of the decade.18 A lineman who doesn’t report is simply a blocker. One who does is a blocker the defense can’t completely ignore, and the offense keeps the option of throwing to him. Most of the time it doesn’t: across the decade, offenses passed on only about a quarter of the snaps with a reported lineman. The report is mostly a threat, and the play is mostly a run.

Why did the habit spread, and why isn’t it 100%? The data can’t say what coaches were thinking, but the rulebook shows what the report costs and what it buys. It buys a sixth possible receiver for the defense to worry about. It costs little: a few seconds while the referee announces it, and a commitment. A reported player has to keep playing an eligible spot, reporting again before every snap, for as long as he stays in the game, until a stoppage such as a timeout, a foul or the end of a quarter (or a snap on the sideline) lets him switch back. An offense that wants him back inside as an ordinary lineman on the next snap, with no time to substitute, may not want that. And the report itself is a message: a reported lineman says “big formation, probably a run”, which, as the Lions film room below shows, an offense can use as bait.

NoteGo deeper: why college and high school are stricter

College football doesn’t let linemen report eligible at all: NCAA rules require at least five players numbered 50–79 on the line on a normal scrimmage play, and a player wearing one of those numbers is never an eligible receiver (the rules loosen only in a punt or field-goal formation).19 That’s why trick plays to linemen are an NFL thing. High school had its own scare: in 2007 Piedmont High in California built a whole offense, the “A-11,” from a kick-like formation in which every player wore an eligible number, until the national high-school rules body closed the loophole in February 2009.20 Rules makers in every league guard the same principle: the defense must be able to tell who can catch the ball.

NoteFilm room: New England Patriots vs. Baltimore Ravens, January 10, 2015

This was a divisional-round playoff game of the 2014 season, played in Foxborough in January 2015. Baltimore led 14–0 in the first quarter and 28–14 in the third, and New England came back both times to win 35–31. The second comeback started with a formation the Ravens had never seen.

On three snaps of a third-quarter drive, the Patriots lined up with only four offensive linemen, so one of the five spots between the ends had to go to a player wearing an eligible number, and he had to report ineligible. On the first and the last of those snaps it was running back Shane Vereen (#34), and he lined up in the slot, on the line just inside receiver Julian Edelman: covered anyway, so ineligible twice over, by his position and by his announcement. Meanwhile tight end Michael Hoomanawanui (#47) lined up at left tackle, with the regular left tackle, Nate Solder, moved inside to guard, which put Hoomanawanui at the end of the line and made him an eligible receiver (the figure after this box counts that line).

Defenders did what they’re trained to do: they watched the man wearing a back’s number in the slot, and treated the player in a lineman’s spot as a lineman. On the drive, Tom Brady hit Hoomanawanui for 16 yards and then, on a snap the official play-by-play records with “NE 34-Vereen ineligible,” for 14 more to the Baltimore 10. Ravens coach John Harbaugh came onto the field to protest that his defense hadn’t been given time to identify who was eligible, and the Ravens were penalized for unsportsmanlike conduct on the bench, half the distance to the goal. Two plays later Brady threw a touchdown to Rob Gronkowski.

Was it legal? Yes. The referee announced each report. Afterwards Brady said the Ravens might want to study the rule book; Harbaugh called it “clearly deception.” The league sided, in effect, with both: the formation had been legal, and it was not going to stay legal. For the 2015 season the NFL added a rule that a player who reports as ineligible must line up inside the formation’s core, roughly between the tackles, so an eligible-numbered player can no longer report ineligible and then split out where receivers stand. Breaking it is an illegal substitution, five yards.

What to notice: the trick didn’t break the eligibility rules; it broke the defense’s shortcut for reading them. Numbers are a convenience. The real rule is position plus announcement, and when an offense separates the two, the defense has to listen to the referee as closely as it watches the field.

NoteFilm room: Detroit Lions 2022–2024

Under offensive coordinator Ben Johnson (2022–2024), the Lions made the eligible tackle an ordinary part of the offense rather than a once-a-season gimmick (the Dallas two-point story is in Personnel). Their reported linemen did catch passes. Right tackle Penei Sewell (#58) reported eligible and caught a 9-yard pass against Minnesota on December 11, 2022, in a 34–23 win; backup tackle Dan Skipper (#70) caught a 4-yarder against the Vikings in Week 18 of 2023 and a 9-yard touchdown against Buffalo on December 15, 2024. From 2016 to 2025, Lions linemen were thrown to nine times, more than any other team’s.

But the bigger effect was on everyone else. Detroit reported a lineman on 170 runs and passes from 2022 to 2024, the third most in the league, and passed on about 35% of them, against about 24% for the league as a whole on the same kind of snap. The throws mostly went to the regular receivers, off play-action: with Skipper reported eligible on second-and-5 in that Week 18 game against Minnesota (January 7, 2024), Jared Goff hit Amon-Ra St. Brown for a 70-yard touchdown.

What to notice: a reported lineman forces the defense to account for one more possible receiver and tells it to expect a run. The Lions used the second message more than the first. When you hear “reporting as eligible,” don’t watch the lineman. Watch the safeties: if they creep toward the line to stop the run, the pass that matters is going over their heads.

Watch for it on Sunday

TipWatch for it: the strength caller
  • Name the strong side before every snap. Say it out loud: “tight end right.” If there’s no attached tight end, name the passing strength (“three receivers left”) and the field side.
  • Then watch the defense set. Against a base defense, which side did the Sam (usually the linebacker standing over the tight end) and the safety who walks down go to? In nickel there may be no Sam: watch which side the defensive tackle lines up just outside a guard (the “3-technique”, taught in Defensive Line Play) and which safety walks down (A First Look at Defense shows how to spot the packages). Did the front go one way and the coverage the other? Write down which rule this defense seems to use.
  • Count the line on unusual looks. Seven or more? Who are the two ends? Is a tight end covered? On a goal line or jumbo look, listen for “reporting as eligible.”
  • Check the quarterback’s depth. Under center or pistol: lean run, and expect a fake if it’s a pass. Shotgun: lean pass, and note the back’s side.
  • Read the outside receivers’ splits against the numbers. A receiver well inside his usual landmark: think crack block or an outside-breaking route. One far outside: think inside route or a one-on-one shot.
  • Log it. Add “strong side,” “QB depth” and “notable split” columns to your prediction log. After a half, check how often the defense set its front to the tight end, and how often the quarterback’s depth called the run or pass.

Is this formation legal? If not, what’s the smallest fix, and who is eligible after it?

TipAnswer

Illegal formation. Count the line: the five linemen and Y. That’s six. U is a wing (behind the dashed line, so a yard off the line), and X and Z are both a yard back, so five players are in the backfield: U, X, Z, R and Q. If the ball is snapped, it’s a five-yard flag.

The smallest fix is one step forward by one player. The usual one is X stepping up onto the line, which gives seven on the line with X as the left end and Y as the right end. Then the eligible receivers are X and Y (the two ends) and U, Z and R (the backs); the quarterback under center is not. Another legal fix is U stepping up to become an in-line tight end on the left. That also makes seven, but now U is the left end and X, still a yard back, is a back. A third fix, Z stepping up, also makes seven, but then Z covers Y, who becomes ineligible: legal, and the wrong fix if you want your tight end in the route (panel 3 of the legality figure).

This is the receiver’s question from the opening scene. It’s why X checks with the official, and why the rule about who stands on the line, more than any playbook habit, decides where X and Z line up.

Name the strong side by each of the three rules. Where is a typical NFL defense likely to set its front, and which way will its coverage lean? Why might the offense have chosen this arrangement?

TipAnswer

By the tight end: left. Y is attached on the left. By the receivers: right, where three of them stand. By the field: left, because the ball is on the right hash, so the wide side is to the offense’s left.

The likely defense: against 11 personnel it will usually be a nickel defense, and the front (the defensive line’s shade and the linebackers) sets to the tight end, on the left. With the field also on the left, there’s no argument about it. The coverage has to deal with three receivers on the right, and here’s the twist: they’re on the short side of the field, the boundary. Most defenses lean their coverage toward the three receivers anyway, often by rotating a safety down toward them or by playing a “trips check”, a coverage adjustment made only against three-receiver sides (those checks are in Disguise, Rotation and Split-Field Coverage).

Why the offense likes it: putting the receivers into the boundary asks the defense which rule it trusts, receivers or field, and either answer leaves something open. If the coverage follows the receivers into the boundary, the wide side is left to the tight end, the back and lots of grass, which is a good place to run or to throw back to Y. If the coverage stays with the field, three receivers face fewer defenders in a tight space. The formation’s job is to make the defense’s own definitions disagree.

Run or pass? If it’s a run, which way? If it’s a pass, what kind? What is Z’s split telling you?

TipAnswer

Lean run, to the right. Three clues point the same way.

  • The quarterback is under center, with the back straight behind him. In neutral situations from 2023 to 2025, offenses passed on only about a third of under-center plays.
  • The strength is right by every rule. Both tight ends are on the right (Y in-line, U on the wing), and the ball is on the left hash, so the right is also the field side, with the most room.
  • Z is in a reduced split, well inside the numbers on the wide side, where a normal split would put him near the numbers about 17 yards from the ball. A receiver that close to the formation is often there to block: to crack down on a linebacker or a safety on a run to his side.

If it’s a pass, expect play-action. About four in five under-center dropbacks used a run fake. The classic partner of this look is the bootleg: fake the run to the right, and the quarterback rolls out to the left, away from the fake, with the reduced Z running a long crossing route back toward him and X, alone wide on the left, running a comeback (deep, then back toward the sideline) or a post behind it. Both receivers’ splits make that possible: Z starts close enough to cross the field in time, and X’s wide split leaves room in front of him. (Play-Action, Boots and Screens builds the play.)

That’s the honest answer to every alignment drill: the picture doesn’t tell you the play, it tells you the odds. Here the odds say run right, and if they’re wrong, the play-action pass was built to punish exactly the defense that believed them.

Takeaways

  • Seven on the line, and the ends are the only eligible linemen. “On the line” means level with the center; “off” means at least a yard back; anything in between is illegal. A player on the line who isn’t at an end is covered and ineligible.
  • The rules make the letters. X stands on the line so the formation has an end on his side; Z stands off it so the tight end stays eligible and Z can motion. Y is the main tight end; H and F are move players whose meaning changes by team and grouping.
  • Strength is a definition, and there are three. The tight end’s side (for the run and the front), the side with more receivers (for coverage), and the wide side of the field (the tiebreaker). Offenses win by making the definitions disagree.
  • The quarterback’s depth is a strong clue. In 2023–2025 neutral situations, about 69% of shotgun plays were passes against about 34% from under center, and about 81% of under-center dropbacks were play-action.
  • Splits are set by landmarks, not yards. The numbers stay put while the ball moves hash to hash. A reduced split often means a crack block or an outside-breaking route; a wide split, an inside route or a one-on-one shot.
  • A lineman becomes a receiver only by reporting. The referee tells the defense. The Patriots’ January 2015 trick exploited the defense’s reliance on numbers, and since 2015 a player reporting ineligible must line up inside the tackles. In 2025 six-lineman snaps nearly doubled, and the extra man reported on about 80% of them.
