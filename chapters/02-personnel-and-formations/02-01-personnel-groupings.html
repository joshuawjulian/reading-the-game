Personnel: Who’s on the Field Before Anything Else

Two digits, said before the huddle breaks, that tell you who the offense brought, what the defense will send back, and how the odds of a run or a pass have already moved.

def ring_by_body(p, skip=()):
    """Ring every back (aqua) and tight end (gold): count the rings and you have the personnel."""
    for k, pl in p.players.items():
        if k in skip or pl.side != "off":
            continue
        if pl.pos in ("RB", "FB"):
            p.highlight(k, color=RB_RING)
        elif pl.pos == "TE":
            p.highlight(k, color=TE_RING)
    return p


def body_count(players):
    off = [q for q in players if q.side == "off"]
    rb = sum(q.pos in ("RB", "FB") for q in off)
    te = sum(q.pos == "TE" for q in off)
    wr = sum(q.pos == "WR" for q in off)
    return rb, te, wr

Third-and-1 at midfield, early in the fourth quarter. The previous play has barely been whistled dead when the broadcast cuts wide: two wide receivers jog toward their own sideline, and two bigger men, a tight end and a fullback, run the other way to join the huddle. Across the field, the defense reacts within a couple of seconds. A cornerback sprints off, a 245-pound linebacker sprints on, and someone near the defensive bench is shouting a single word. The ball hasn’t been snapped, the offense hasn’t even lined up, and the analyst says “they’ve gone to 22 personnel.” He’s right, and both teams have already made a move. This chapter is about that move: who the offense chooses to put on the field, how the defense answers (and why it swapped only one player when the offense swapped two), who that shouting man is, and how much those two digits tell you about what’s coming.

Eleven players, but only five that change

Start with a puzzle. An offense has eleven players on the field, and the label for who they are has only two digits. How can two digits describe eleven people?

Because six of the eleven almost never change. Every normal offensive snap has a quarterback and five offensive linemen (the center, two guards and two tackles, the big men who block and, by rule, can’t catch a forward pass). Those six are the fixed core. The other five spots are the variables, and they can be filled by three kinds of player that The Twenty-Two introduced as the skill positions:

  • Running backs. Players who line up behind the line, mostly to carry the ball or block. The tailback (or halfback) is the runner; the fullback is a bigger back who mostly blocks for him. Both count as running backs here.
  • Tight ends. Big, versatile players, usually 245 to 265 pounds, who often line up right next to a tackle. They block like small linemen and catch passes.
  • Wide receivers. Usually the lightest and fastest on the offense, lined up wide, mainly to catch passes.

Every way of filling those five spots is a personnel grouping: the mix of running backs, tight ends and wide receivers on the field for a play. Coaches, scouts, broadcasters and the data all write it the same way, as two digits:

First digit = running backs. Second digit = tight ends. Wide receivers = 5 − backs − tight ends.

So “one-one,” usually just said “eleven,” means one back and one tight end, which leaves three wide receivers. “Twelve” (one-two) is one back and two tight ends, so two receivers. “Twenty-one” is two backs, one tight end and two receivers. You never need a third digit, because the receivers are whatever is left over. (One grouping, jumbo, bends the arithmetic; it gets its own section below.)

The digits are the shared language of scouting reports, broadcasts and data. Inside a team’s own playbook, groupings often carry word names instead, and the words differ from team to team (one Houston Texans explainer mentions “Pony” and “Rabbit” for kinds of two-back grouping).1 When you hear an unfamiliar word on a sideline microphone, it is probably a team’s private name for one of the groupings below.

CautionCommon misconception

The digits are not jersey numbers, and “11 personnel” does not mean “the eleven players.” It is a count of body types: one running back, one tight end. Say it “eleven” or “one-one,” but read it as two separate digits. Defensive labels count differently: a “4-2-5” means four linemen, two linebackers and five defensive backs. Older names such as “4-3” leave off the defensive backs because four was taken for granted. A First Look at Defense owns those names.

Why count only backs and tight ends? Because those are the two body types that change what the offense can do and what the defense must fear. A tight end is a blocker who can run routes; a fullback is a blocker who can carry the ball or catch it out of the backfield. Each one you add makes the offense bigger and better at running the ball, and takes away a wide receiver, the player most dangerous in the open field. The two digits are, in effect, a dial between “big” and “fast.”

NoteMadden vs. real life

In Madden you pick a formation and the right players appear in it, and the computer’s defense swaps its own players instantly. In the NFL, personnel is a separate decision made before the formation and the play. A coach shouts or signals the grouping (“Twelve! Twelve!”), the players in that package run on, and the defense has to see them, decide, and send its own substitutes. It takes several seconds of the forty-second play clock, in plain view: the first thing you can read.

Reading the deck: the eight groupings you’ll meet

Here are the eight groupings the chapter asks you to know, each drawn in an ordinary, legal alignment. Don’t worry yet about the names of the alignments. Those are formations, the next chapter’s job. For now, count the rings: an aqua ring is a running back, a gold ring is a tight end, and anyone with no ring (apart from the quarterback and the linemen) is a wide receiver.

The letters on the receivers are the names most playbooks use for their spots: X is the receiver usually on the line on one side (the “split end”), Z the receiver off the line on the other side (the “flanker”), Y usually the tight end, and H or F a player the offense moves around, who can be a slot receiver, a second tight end or a fullback depending on the grouping. Look at the 12 card and you’ll see X standing off the line: with a tight end on the line at each end, both receivers have to step back so the tight ends stay the end men, and eligible. Formation Rules and the Language of Alignment explains the letters and that rule properly. In this chapter they’re just name tags; the rings do the counting.

11 personnel (1 RB, 1 TE, 3 WR) is the NFL’s default: it was on the field for about six plays in ten every season from 2016 to 2025. Its appeal is that it can do almost anything. Three wide receivers stretch the defense across the field; the one tight end can stay in to block or run a route; the one back can run, block a blitzer or catch. Because the offense can run or pass from it with almost equal ease, it gives the defense very little to go on. It also forces the defense to answer with a fifth defensive back, which we’ll see is the whole substitution game in miniature.

12 personnel (1 RB, 2 TE, 2 WR) swaps the third receiver for a second tight end. Now the offense can line up with a blocker next to both tackles and run either way, or split one of the tight ends out like a receiver and throw. It is the league’s second most common grouping and, as the data below shows, the closest thing football has to a coin flip.

21 personnel (2 RB, 1 TE, 2 WR) adds a fullback, the old-fashioned lead blocker. This is the grouping of the classic I-formation (fullback directly in front of the tailback) and of the old pro set (two backs side by side behind the quarterback), the standard NFL look of an earlier era. A good fullback gives the offense an extra blocker who starts moving before the defense knows where the play is going.

13 personnel (1 RB, 3 TE, 1 WR) is three tight ends and just one receiver. It is mostly a short-yardage and running grouping, though as you’ll see in the film room, one team in 2025 turned it into a weapon for both.

22 personnel (2 RB, 2 TE, 1 WR) is the heavy end of the everyday menu: a fullback, a tailback and two tight ends, leaving one receiver. The offense is telling the defense, more or less, “we’re going to run, and we’re bringing extra people to block for it.” That’s useful near the goal line, on third-and-1, and late in a game when the offense wants to keep the clock running. It is also, for exactly that reason, a great place to fake a run and throw (more on that trade below).

10 personnel (1 RB, 0 TE, 4 WR) drops the tight end for a fourth receiver. It’s everywhere in college football, where spread offenses grew up, but rare in the NFL (around 1% of plays in recent seasons). NFL offenses want a tight end’s blocking against much bigger pass rushers, and four receivers are easy for a defense to answer: it just sends in a sixth defensive back. A good tight end is harder to match, because no substitute is both big enough to take on his block and fast enough to cover him.

20 personnel (2 RB, 0 TE, 3 WR) puts two backs next to the quarterback in the shotgun with three receivers. It’s a niche look (about 1% of NFL plays or less), but a handy one: either back can take a handoff, block, or slip out for a pass. 00 personnel is five wide receivers and no back at all, an “empty” backfield with nobody beside the quarterback. In the NFL it’s so rare that you may not see it all season.2 (Empty formations, in which the back or tight end lines up wide, are common. That’s a formation, not a personnel grouping, and it’s the subject of Spread Formations.)

There are a handful of other combinations, such as 01, 02, 23 and the occasional 30, but together they make up only a percent or two of plays. If you can read the eight above, you can read practically every NFL snap.

Personnel is who, not where

One more rule before we move on, and it’s the one beginners trip over. Personnel counts who the players are, not where they stand. A tight end who lines up out wide like a receiver is still a tight end, so the grouping is still 12 if there are two of them. A running back who lines up in the slot is still a running back. A wide receiver standing in the backfield is still a receiver.

That’s not pedantry; it’s why personnel matters. The defense chooses its players by who comes on the field, and only then does the offense line up. Send in two tight ends, get bigger defenders; then spread those tight ends out like receivers, and bigger, slower players have to cover them. That gap between personnel and formation is one of the oldest tricks in football, and you can’t see it unless you count first.

The hard case is a player whose body doesn’t match his label. Deebo Samuel was a 49ers wide receiver who, in 2021, also carried the ball 59 times for 365 yards and eight touchdowns.3 With him in the backfield, a grouping that reads “11” on paper played like two backs. The Saints have used Taysom Hill, a quarterback, as a runner, a receiver and a tight end; the data lists him as a quarterback in 2023 and 2025 and as a tight end in 2024, so the same kind of snap gets a different label in different seasons. The Ravens’ Patrick Ricard has played both defensive line and fullback. Offenses use players like these on purpose: the defense picks its substitutes by label, and a player who is two things at once makes every answer partly wrong. Note it in your log whenever you see one.

CautionCommon misconception

“You can tell the position by the jersey number.” Not reliably, not any more. Before 2021, NFL tight ends wore numbers in the 80s (or 40s), and receivers wore 80s or 10–19. Since the league loosened its numbering rules in 2021 and again in 2023, tight ends, receivers and backs can all wear single digits and most of the same ranges4 (The Twenty-Two has the details). On TV, use three other clues. Size: tight ends are usually 245 to 265 pounds, receivers well under that. Stance: a tight end next to the tackle usually sets in a three-point stance, one hand down like a lineman, while receivers stand up. Homework: a glance at the team’s roster before kickoff, plus the analyst, who will often name the grouping for you.

Heavy personnel

You’ll hear broadcasters say an offense “went heavy.” Heavy personnel means any grouping with fewer than three wide receivers: the offense has taken receivers off and put tight ends, backs or linemen on. So 12, 21, 13 and 22 are all heavy (some people keep the word for 13, 22 and jumbo; this course uses the wider meaning, as NFL Next Gen Stats does). Why take a fast player off the field? To block more people, since each tight end or fullback is an extra blocker who can also be a threat; to make the defense get bigger, and so slower in coverage; and to hide the play, because a defense facing players who can run and catch can’t relax against either.

Jumbo: when a sixth lineman comes in

Sometimes even three tight ends isn’t big enough. In a jumbo grouping, the offense puts a sixth offensive lineman on the field, usually lined up exactly where a tight end would be, at the end of the line next to a tackle. (Some teams put him inside instead, with two tackles side by side on one end of the line; that unbalanced line is a formation trick for a later chapter.) He’s a lineman, often 300 pounds or more, so he blocks much better than any tight end. That’s the point: on the goal line, on third-and-1, or when killing the clock with a lead, the offense wants raw size.

Jumbo is where the two-digit arithmetic needs one adjustment. The sixth lineman takes one of the five changeable spots, so only four are left for backs, tight ends and receivers: receivers = 4 − backs − tight ends. The jumbo below has one back and two tight ends, so one receiver, not two.

There is a catch, and it is a rule, not a strategy. Linemen wear numbers in the 50–79 range, and a player wearing one of those numbers is ineligible to catch a forward pass (he can block, but not go out for a ball thrown past the line) unless he reports to the referee before the snap. The referee then announces it, often over the stadium microphone (“Number 79 is reporting as eligible”), so the defense knows. The full rules for eligibility and reporting are in Formation Rules, which owns them; for now it’s enough to know that a jumbo lineman is a blocker by default and a receiver only by announcement.

So is the picture above “12” or “13”? It depends who’s counting. The data used in this chapter labels players by roster position, so it calls this snap “12” (one back, two tight ends) plus an extra lineman, and by the jumbo rule that leaves one receiver. Some charters and staffs count a lineman standing at tight end as a tight end and call it “13”; others write “12 jumbo” or “6 OL.” In the charts below, jumbo plays keep the code their backs and tight ends give them, and are also counted separately as jumbo.

Jumbo is the most predictable grouping in football. In neutral situations from 2023 to 2025, offenses in jumbo threw on fewer than one play in three. That predictability is the cost: when the defense knows a run is coming, it can load up for it. It is also the opportunity, as the Detroit Lions showed, because a defense that is sure of the run is the easiest one to fool with a pass.

NoteFilm room: Detroit Lions 2022–2024

Under offensive coordinator Ben Johnson (2022–2024), the Lions made the eligible lineman a regular part of their offense. Backup tackle Dan Skipper was the usual extra lineman, and he or, on the odd trick play, starting left tackle Taylor Decker could report eligible. In 2022 Detroit used a sixth lineman on about 10% of its snaps, second-most in the league.

The most famous of those snaps didn’t count. On December 30, 2023, at Dallas, Detroit scored a touchdown with 23 seconds left to pull within a point at 20–19 and went for two. Jared Goff threw to Decker, who caught it in the end zone. The officials ruled that it was Skipper, not Decker, who had reported eligible, so Decker was an ineligible receiver. The conversion was wiped out and Detroit had to try again from farther back: the next attempt was intercepted but erased by a Dallas offside penalty, the one after that fell incomplete, and the Cowboys won 20–19.5 The play became a national argument about reporting, and it shows the logic of jumbo: the defense saw six linemen and expected a run; the Lions’ answer was a pass to a lineman.

What to notice: when an offense goes jumbo, watch the referee. If he turns to the press box and announces a number, a lineman has reported eligible, and the defense now has to account for him too.

The defense answers: substitution matching

Personnel is a two-sided game. The offense picks its eleven first, and the defense answers with its own. Defensive groupings are named differently, usually by how many defensive backs (cornerbacks and safeties, the players whose main job is pass coverage) are on the field, because that number is what the defense changes most:

  • Base: four defensive backs, with seven players up front (four linemen and three linebackers in a “4-3,” or three and four in a “3-4”). Built to stop the run.
  • Nickel: five defensive backs. The fifth, called the nickel back or slot cornerback, usually replaces a linebacker and covers the receiver lined up in the slot (between the outside receiver and the line).
  • Dime: six defensive backs, for obvious passing downs.

A First Look at Defense builds these packages properly.

The defense’s choice follows a simple logic: match bodies to bodies. A wide receiver is fast, so he should be covered by a defensive back, who is also fast. A tight end or fullback is big, so a linebacker, who is bigger and better at taking on blocks, is a reasonable match. More receivers, more defensive backs; more tight ends and backs, more linebackers. That reflex is called substitution matching.

It is a direction, not a one-for-one swap, because the defense has a floor of four defensive backs: two cornerbacks and two safeties. Even 22 personnel sends five eligible receivers out on every play, and any of them can run deep, so a defense without two corners and two safeties has nobody left to stop the long pass. Only where there is no room for a long pass, near the goal line or on the shortest yardage, does a defense go below four. That is why the opening play worked the way it did: the offense swapped two receivers for two big men (11 to 22), but the defense, already at five defensive backs, could only come down one, to four. Against 22 in 2025, defenses kept exactly four defensive backs on about three snaps in four, and went to three or fewer only about one in seven, mostly at the goal line or on third- and fourth-and-short.6

Who makes the call? The “single word” from the defensive bench is the name of the package: “Base!” or “Nickel!”, or a team’s own code word for it. Someone on every defensive staff is watching for the offense’s substitutes the moment the whistle blows and calls out the grouping; the defensive play-caller answers with the package, the substitutes run on, and the green dot and sideline signals do the rest. Coordinators talk about wanting to see the offense’s personnel “as early as possible,” and players notice when that relay is slow.7 It is also why the hybrid players above are worth so much to an offense: the caller has to name the grouping in a second or two, and a player who is both a receiver and a back has no right name.

Defenses also substitute by situation, not just by personnel. Most have a third-and-long package (often dime, with a sixth defensive back and extra pass rushers) and a short-yardage or goal-line package (more linemen, fewer defensive backs) that come on at the whistle whatever the offense sends. The offense’s personnel then decides only the fine-tuning.

def sub_tracking(t_end=24.0, dt=0.1):
    """BDB-style tracking frames for the substitution sequence."""
    rows = []
    times = np.round(np.arange(0, t_end + 1e-9, dt), 2)
    for fid, t in enumerate(times, 1):
        for k, kf in KF.items():
            d, w = at(kf, t)
            side = "def" if k in DEF_LABEL else "off"
            lab = DEF_LABEL.get(k) if side == "def" else OFF_LABEL.get(k, "")
            rows.append(dict(frameId=fid, time=float(t), displayName=k, x=LOS + d, y=BALL_Y - w,
                             side=side, label=lab))
        rows.append(dict(frameId=fid, time=float(t), displayName="football", x=LOS, y=BALL_Y,
                         side="ball", label=""))

Notice two things. First, the order: the offense moved first and the defense second, and the defense had no sensible way to go first, because any guess could be answered by the next substitute. Second, the defense only matched bodies. It still has no idea whether the second tight end is there to block or to run a route, and the offense hasn’t chosen its formation. The defense has spent its answer; the offense keeps its next two moves.

The rules that protect the match

You met the rule behind panel 2 in What a Play Really Is: when the offense substitutes, the umpire stands over the ball and the snap waits until the defense has had a reasonable chance to respond, except in the last two minutes of each half; when the offense doesn’t substitute, there is no pause, and a defense that changes players anyway risks twelve men on the field.8 Three details matter for personnel:

  • Any substitution starts the clock on that protection, even a like-for-like one. Swap a tired receiver for a fresh receiver and the personnel hasn’t changed, but the umpire still holds the ball. That’s why fast-tempo offenses rotate players only at stoppages: one swap and their tempo is gone.
  • Not substituting is a weapon. If the defense is in base against a 12-personnel offense that is throwing well, the offense can stay in 12, play fast, and keep those linebackers on the field. In-Game Adjustments, Tempo, and the Sideline Battle builds that game out.
  • Bluffing has a limit. An offense can’t run a player on to bait the defense into a change and then pull him straight back off: a substitute who comes on and talks to his teammates must stay for at least one play, or the team is warned and then penalized.9 The fakes come in the formation, not in the substitution.
ImportantWhy it exists

Substitution matching exists because offenses get to choose first. The offense commits to its bodies first, in plain view, and in exchange keeps the last word on the formation and the play. The defense gets the last word on bodies, and the rules guarantee it time to use it. So the offense picks personnel hoping the defense’s best answer to who is the wrong answer to where and what.

That’s the bind. If the defense answers two tight ends with nickel, it has a 195-pound defensive back near the line of scrimmage, and the offense can aim its run at him: a 250-pound tight end walls him off, and the back runs around the spot where a linebacker should have been. If it answers with base, it has three linebackers on the field, and the offense can spread the tight ends out and make a linebacker cover one of them down the field. The better the tight ends are at both jobs, the worse the defense’s choice gets. That’s why great two-way tight ends are so valuable, and it’s a big part of why 12 personnel has grown: an offense with two of them can pick whichever mismatch the defense hands it, after the defense has made its choice.

The same logic runs the other way for the defense. A defense with a safety who can tackle like a linebacker, or a linebacker who can cover like a safety, doesn’t have to choose: it can stay in one grouping against everything and take the matchup game away. That’s the idea behind the big nickel, a nickel whose fifth defensive back is a bigger safety rather than a small slot corner, and behind hybrid defenders in general, the defensive mirror of a Deebo Samuel. The 2025 Seahawks, for example, played 220-pound rookie safety Nick Emmanwori as a nickel defender.11 A First Look at Defense tells that story.

Three things jump out.

The common groupings are weak clues. In neutral situations, offenses passed on about 56% of plays from 11 personnel and about 49% from 12. That’s a nudge, not a signal. If you predicted “pass” every time you saw 11 and “run” every time you saw 12, you’d be right about 54% of the time on those plays, barely better than the 53% you’d get by saying “pass” on every one of them. That’s deliberate: the groupings an offense uses most are the ones it has built to do both. A team whose 11 personnel was 80% pass would be giving away the game. Even four receivers (10 personnel) was only about 55% pass: spreading the defense out also thins the number of defenders near the ball, which makes running easier.

The heavy groupings are stronger clues. From 22 personnel, offenses passed only about 36% of the time, and from jumbo about 30%. That is also where the rare pass is most dangerous, because the defense has loaded up for the run: the logic of play-action (faking a handoff before throwing).

Personnel predicts the formation as well as the play. From 11 personnel, offenses lined up in the shotgun on about 70% of plays; from 13, only about 30%. Heavy personnel usually means the quarterback under center, close to the ball. You can use that as a check on your count: if you counted three tight ends and the quarterback is five yards deep in the shotgun, count again, or expect something unusual.

How much does personnel add?

Here is a blunt test. Take every neutral play from 2023 to 2025 and make the simplest guess the situation allows: for each down and distance (first or second down; 1–3, 4–6, 7–10 or 11+ yards to go), say whichever of run or pass is more common there. That guesser is right about 60% of the time. Now let it see the personnel as well. It is still right about 60% of the time. On 11 and 12, which are more than eight snaps in ten, the more common call simply doesn’t change. Let it see one more pre-snap fact, whether the quarterback is in the shotgun, and it jumps to about 66%.

For most of the decade the story is stability: 11 personnel on about 60% of plays, 12 on about 20%, 21 on about 8%, everything else sharing the rest. By the late 2010s, three receivers was simply what an offense looked like, and Sean McVay’s Rams were the extreme case of that habit.

Then 2025 moved. Eleven personnel fell to 58.8%, its lowest share of the decade, and 12 rose to 24.4%, its highest. Thirteen personnel jumped from 3.7% to 5.1%. Next Gen Stats, measuring it its own way, found offenses with fewer than three wide receivers on 41.7% of 2025 plays, the first season at or above 38% since it began tracking in 2016, and the share kept climbing as the season went on.13

Why then? No rule change or single coach set it off, and nobody can prove a cause from a chart. The explanation most analysts give is an arms race. Through the late 2010s and early 2020s, defenses got smaller and faster to stop the pass: more nickel and dime, and more looks with two safeties deep (which A First Look at Defense introduces as two-high). A defense like that has fewer big bodies near the line, a “light box,” and it was winning: getting to quarterbacks faster and covering better. Offenses answered by getting bigger, to force defenses into base, where “they generally do not want an undersized extra DB to get steamrolled by a tight end,” as an NFL.com Next Gen Stats analysis put it, and then running at whatever was left, often with the quarterback back under center.14 And as you saw above, defenses answered that by playing more base in 2025. Each side’s move invites the other’s. The Modern Era tells the whole arms race, and Working with nflverse Data in Python shows you how to rebuild these charts yourself.

NoteGo deeper: where the numbers come from

Every chart in this chapter is a course calculation from the nflverse participation data (load_participation()), which lists the personnel on each play, joined to nflverse play-by-play.15 Three cautions if you reproduce them. First, the source changes between 2022 and 2023: Next Gen Stats supplied 2016–2022 and FTN Data supplies 2023 onward, and the personnel strings change format (“1 RB, 2 TE, 2 WR” before, every player by position after), so the code normalizes them before counting; the personnel shares agree across the break. Second, players are counted by roster position, not by role. A snap with a second quarterback or a defensive player in the offense (a string like “2 QB, 1 RB, 1 TE, 2 WR”) is labeled by its backs and tight ends, here “11,” even though only two receivers are out there. That is about 1% of plays, too few to move the charts, and the course keeps the simple backs-then-tight-ends count so its numbers match the course fact sheet. Third, the participation file for a season arrives only after the season ends, so 2026 numbers won’t exist until early 2027. Published series from other providers (Next Gen Stats, Sharp Football, PFF) count slightly differently; never put two providers’ numbers on one axis.

NoteFilm room: Los Angeles Rams 2017–2019

When the Rams hired Sean McVay in January 2017, he was 30, the youngest head coach in modern NFL history.16 His offense became famous for using one grouping, 11 personnel, almost all the time: about 81% of plays in 2017, 93% in 2018 and 73% in 2019, against a league norm of about 60%.

Why would a coach give up the personnel game entirely? Because he was playing a different one. If the offense never substitutes, the defense never gets a reason to, so it sits in nickel all day: in 2018, defenses had five or more defensive backs on about 94% of the Rams’ plays. That suited McVay twice over. A nickel defense has one fewer big body near the line, so his running game was always aimed at a lighter front. And a grouping that never changes tells the defense nothing: in neutral situations in 2018 the Rams passed on 56% of their 11-personnel plays, almost exactly the league rate. McVay put his variety into formation, motion and play-action instead, and it worked: the 2018 Rams reached Super Bowl LIII.

What to notice: an offense that never substitutes isn’t being lazy. It’s denying the defense the first clue.

NoteFilm room: San Francisco 49ers, 21 personnel and Kyle Juszczyk

Kyle Shanahan’s 49ers have been the league’s leading 21-personnel team for most of his tenure: first in the league in 21 personnel in 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021, 2024 and 2025, and second in the other seasons from 2017 to 2025, typically around a third of their plays, against a league share of about 8%. The player who makes it work is fullback Kyle Juszczyk, who signed in March 2017 on a contract that made him, at the time, the highest-paid fullback in the league.17

Juszczyk can block a linebacker on a run, line up in the slot, or split out wide like a receiver. When he lines up wide, the defense that matched 21 personnel with its base package has to send someone with him: a cornerback, which leaves a linebacker covering a receiver somewhere else, or a linebacker, which takes a defender away from the ball (Drill 2 below draws exactly this). What to notice: when the 49ers come out with a fullback, find him after the huddle breaks. Where he lines up tells you which matchup they’re hunting.

Watch for it on Sunday

TipWatch for it: call the personnel
  • Between plays, watch the sidelines, not the replay. When the broadcast shows a wide shot, count who jogs off and who runs on. Two receivers off and a tight end and a fullback on is a change from 11 to 22.
  • Count backs, then tight ends, and say the number out loud. Backs stand behind or beside the quarterback; tight ends are the big players at the end of the line or just behind it, usually with a hand down. Receivers are what’s left: 5 minus the other two (4 minus, if there’s a sixth lineman).
  • Look for a sixth lineman. A 300-pounder standing where a tight end usually stands means jumbo. Listen for the referee’s “reporting as eligible.”
  • Then watch the defense. Did a defensive back run off or a linebacker run on? Base, nickel or dime? If the offense substituted and the defense didn’t, the defense is making a bet.
  • Make your first guess from the numbers (neutral downs, 2023–2025): 11 about 56% pass, 12 about 49%, 13 about 45%, 22 about 36%, jumbo about 30%. Treat 11 and 12 as coin flips with a lean; treat 22 and jumbo, especially on third-and-short, as run unless something says otherwise. Then adjust for down and distance, where the quarterback stands, and what you know about the team.
  • Write it down. Add a “personnel” column to your prediction log: grouping, your guess, the result. After a half, compare your hit rate on the heavy groupings with your hit rate on 11.

Count both teams’ personnel. What’s wrong for the defense, why didn’t it fix it, and what does the offense probably want?

TipAnswer

What’s wrong: nothing changed. The offense is still in 12 personnel (two gold rings), and the defense is still in base, with three linebackers, on third-and-7, a passing down. Fourteen seconds is a no-huddle snap: the offense didn’t substitute.

Why the defense didn’t fix it: it couldn’t, safely. With no offensive substitution the umpire had no reason to stand over the ball. A defense that starts running a linebacker off and a defensive back on while the offense is set risks twelve men on the field at the snap, five yards on third-and-7.

What the offense wants: a pass, probably to a tight end. The offense spent two plays teaching the defense that this grouping runs, which kept it in base. Now both tight ends stand off the line, split out beside Z, and linebackers have to cover them away from the ball.

If the defense saw this coming, its answer was the situational substitution from earlier in the chapter: send its third-down package on at the whistle, before the offense hurried to the line, and live with the risk of a run against a lighter defense.

Takeaways

  • Personnel is two digits: running backs, then tight ends. Receivers fill the rest of the five changeable spots (5 − backs − tight ends), or four of them in jumbo, where a sixth lineman takes one.
  • Personnel counts who is on the field, not where they stand, and the gap between personnel and formation is where offenses build mismatches. Hybrid players blur the count on purpose.
  • The offense commits first, and the defense matches in direction (more receivers, more defensive backs; more tight ends and backs, more linebackers) down to a floor of four defensive backs. The rules give it time whenever the offense substitutes, and none when it doesn’t.
  • Personnel shifts the odds more than it settles them. On ordinary first and second downs it barely improves a down-and-distance guess; its weight shows in heavy groupings and short yardage, in the formation it predicts, and in the matchups it sets up.
  • The league’s mix moves with the defense. 11 personnel rose through the early 2010s, held near 60% for a decade, then fell to a decade low in 2025 as offenses went bigger against lighter defenses, and defenses answered with more base.
