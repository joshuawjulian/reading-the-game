def dynamic_units(line=(-21, -10, 0, 10, 21), floaters=(-19, -2.5, 2.5, 19), kr=((R(2), 2.0), (R(10), -3.0)),
                  k_d=-6.0, cover_labels=False, labels=None):
    """Both units lined up for a dynamic kickoff under the 2026 rules: the kicker alone at his own 35
    (drawn a few yards back, at the start of his run-up), ten teammates with a foot on the receiving
    team's 40, five receiving players on their 35, four floaters behind them, two returners deep.
    The default front line is legal under 6-1-3(b): one player inside the hashes (B3), one between the
    hash and the numbers on each side (B2, B4) and one outside the numbers on each side (B1, B5)."""
    labels = labels or {}
    lab = lambda k, default="": labels.get(k, default)   # noqa: E731
    kick = [K_("K", k_d, 0.0, lab("K", "K"))]
    kick += [K_(k, 24.4, w, lab(k, k if cover_labels else "")) for k, w in COVER]
    ret = [RT_(f"B{i + 1}", R(35) + 0.6, w, lab(f"B{i + 1}")) for i, w in enumerate(line)]
    ret += [RT_(f"F{i + 1}", R(31.5), w, lab(f"F{i + 1}")) for i, w in enumerate(floaters)]
    ret += [RT_(f"KR{i + 1}", d, w, lab(f"KR{i + 1}", "KR")) for i, (d, w) in enumerate(kr)]
    return kick, ret

    """Regular-season kickoffs, onside kicks flagged, with the yard line where the next drive started."""
    d = pbp(seasons, columns=COLS)
    d = d[d.season_type == "REG"].sort_values(["game_id", "play_id"]).reset_index(drop=True)
    nxt = d.groupby("game_id")[["posteam", "yardline_100", "play_type"]].shift(-1)
    k = d[d.kickoff_attempt == 1].copy()
    nx = nxt.loc[k.index]
    k["onside"] = k.desc.str.contains("onside", case=False, na=False)
    k["returned"] = k.kickoff_returner_player_id.notna()
    k["from35"] = k.desc.str.contains(r" from \w+ 35 ", regex=True, na=False)
    ok = (~k.onside) & (nx.posteam == k.posteam) & (k.return_touchdown != 1) & nx.yardline_100.notna() \
        & (nx.play_type != "kickoff")
    k["start"] = np.where(ok, 100 - nx.yardline_100, np.nan)        # the receiving team's own yard line
    k["fielded"] = np.where(k.from35, 65 - k.kick_distance, np.nan)  # where it was fielded (negative = end zone)

                             ["onside", "touchback at the 20", "touchback", "returned", "short of landing zone"],

    return k, d

Your team has just kicked a field goal. The kicker trots back to his own 35, and something strange happens: ten of his teammates jog past midfield and stop on the other team’s 40, five yards from a row of opponents who are just standing there. What a kickoff is, and why the game has one

A kickoff is the kick that starts each half and follows every touchdown and field goal: the team that just scored kicks the ball, off a tee, from its own 35 to the other team. It is the most common kind of free kick, a kick that puts the ball in play without a snap. The other free kicks are the kick a team makes after giving up a safety (from its own 20, and it may punt it instead of using a tee) and the rare fair-catch kick: after a fair catch, a team may try a field goal from that spot with nobody rushing, worth three points if it goes through.1

On every free kick, each team has a restraining line: a line it may not cross until the ball is kicked (and, since 2024, until the ball lands). Where those lines are drawn is the whole story of this chapter.

The two groups of eleven have names. The kicking team is the kick coverage unit: its job is to get down the field and tackle whoever catches the ball. The receiving team is the return unit: one or two returners and the nine or ten players who block for them. Both units are mostly backups (linebackers, safeties, tight ends, running backs), the players with a mix of size, speed and tackling ability, coached by the special teams coordinator.

ImportantWhy it exists

A kickoff is a way of handing the ball over after a score that is not free. The team that just scored gives the ball away, but gets to decide how: deep, short, bouncing, or into the end zone. The team receiving it gets a chance to gain field position with a return. Without a kickoff, every drive after a score would start at a fixed spot, and one of the game’s few contests of speed in open space would disappear. That contest is also the problem: open space plus speed means violent collisions, and the history of the kickoff since 2009 is the league trying to keep the first part without the second.

The thing to hold on to is that a kickoff is a trade of field position. As Downs, Distance, and Field Position showed, yards are worth points: a first-and-10 at your own 20 is worth about 0.8 expected points, at your own 30 about 1.5, at your own 35 about 1.7 (nflverse, 2023-2025). Across that stretch of field each yard is worth about 0.07 points, so five yards is worth about 0.3.2 Every kickoff decides where on that curve the other team starts.

The kickoff you remember, and why the league kept changing it

For most of the last fifty years a kickoff looked like this. The kicker teed the ball up (at his own 35 from 1974, the 30 from 1994, and the 35 again from 2011) and ran up to it with his ten teammates in a line beside him.3 The receiving team lined up ten yards away, at the kicking team’s 45, with its returners back near the goal line. At the kick, everyone ran: the coverage sprinted downfield, and the receiving team’s front line turned and ran back toward its own goal line to set up blocks for the return. For years those blockers formed a wedge, three or four big men shoulder to shoulder in front of the returner, and coverage players were coached to throw themselves at it.

Look at what that means physically. A coverage player has run 35 or 40 yards at full speed. The blocker he meets has turned around and is running back at him. Two players closing at something like 15 yards a second (roughly 30 miles an hour) collide, often helmet first. No other play in football routinely lined up that many collisions at that speed.

The data caught up with the intuition. The league’s injury studies found concussions were far more likely on kickoffs than on ordinary plays: in 2017, about five times as likely, per play.4 So the rules moved, one patch at a time. The patches were written by the league’s competition committee, the group of team executives and coaches that studies the game and proposes rule changes for the owners to vote on:

  • 2009: wedges limited to two players.5
  • 2011: the kickoff moved from the 30 to the 35. A kicker five yards closer drives more kicks into the end zone, and a kick that stays there is a touchback: no return, no collisions. Touchbacks jumped from 16% of kickoffs in 2010 to 43% in 2011.6
  • 2016: the kickoff touchback moved from the 20 to the 25, to make kneeling more attractive.7
  • 2018: a full redesign. No running start (the coverage had to stand still, within a yard of the ball, until the kick); five coverage players on each side of the ball; eight of the receiving team’s players inside a 15-yard zone near the ball (the rules called it a setup zone too, but it is not today’s setup zone); wedges banned outright; no blocking in that zone until the ball was touched or hit the ground.8
  • 2023: a one-year patch let the returner fair-catch a kickoff anywhere inside his own 25 and take the ball at the 25, the same as a touchback.9

Every patch worked the same way: it made the kickoff safer by making it disappear. By 2023 only about a quarter of kickoffs were returned, the fewest on record, and the play was close to a formality: a kick into the end zone, a knee, the ball at the 25.10 There was open talk about abolishing it.

Every change before 2024 pushed the return rate down, and the bottom panel shows the cost: once the kick moved to the 35 in 2011, with the touchback at the 20, drives after kickoffs started around the 22; when the touchback moved to the 25, they started around the 25. The league was buying safety with boredom. The 2024 change is the first one that sent both lines up.

The dynamic kickoff, piece by piece

The fix came from outside the NFL. The XFL, a spring league, opened in 2020 with a kickoff invented by its director of football operations, Sam Schwartzstein, a former Stanford center who simulated the idea more than a thousand times before the league used it.11 His insight was to keep the return but take away the run-up: line the two teams up close together, and freeze them until the ball comes down.12 When the competition committee went looking for a way to save the play, NFL special teams coaches, among them the Cowboys’ John Fassel, presented the XFL model to the committee, and on March 26, 2024 the owners adopted an NFL version for 2024 as a one-year trial.13 It is called the dynamic kickoff. Walk through it from left to right.

The kicker is alone. He kicks from his own 35, as before, but he is the only kicking-team player on his side of the 50, and he may not cross midfield until the ball touches the ground or a player.14 In practice he becomes the last line of defense: a safety with a kicking leg.

The coverage starts on the other team’s 40. His ten teammates line up with one foot on the receiving team’s 40, 25 yards in front of the tee. That is the kicking team’s restraining line. They may not move until the ball hits the ground, or hits a player in the landing zone or end zone. Coaches number them outward from the ball, L1 to L5 on the kicker’s left and R1 to R5 on his right, and this chapter does the same.

The blockers start five yards away. The receiving team’s restraining line is its own 35. Behind it is the setup zone, the five yards between the 35 and the 30, where at least nine receiving players line up. In 2024, seven of them had to have a foot on the 35 and the other two lined up behind them, outside the hash marks. The league has loosened that every year since: in 2025 six on the line and up to three behind; from 2026, only five on the line and up to four behind. The players behind the line are called floaters. The rule still spreads the front line across the field: at least one player inside the hash marks, one between the hashes and the yard numbers on each side, and one outside the numbers on each side. And it stops a team from bunching its floaters: picture the setup zone cut into three strips (left of the left hash, between the hashes, right of the right hash); no more than two floaters may stand in any one strip, and a team using all four must put at least one outside the hashes on each side.15 If a team puts ten players in the setup zone instead of nine, at least six of them must be on the 35, which is a tell you can count before the kick. The setup-zone players are frozen too, until the ball lands or is touched.

Why keep loosening it? The league said the 2026 change was meant to “improve safety and make returns more competitive.”16 The mechanism is easy to see. A player on the 35 is committed: he stands face to face with a cover man and will block whoever comes at him. A floater starts two or three yards deeper, so he can wait for the catch and then pick his man: join a double team, or slide over to the side the return is going. More floaters means more blockers who can choose, which makes the return harder for the coverage to read before the kick and gives the return team more ways to build an extra blocker where it wants one.

The returners wait in the landing zone. The landing zone is the area between the receiving team’s goal line and its 20. Up to two returners stand there, and they are the only players besides the kicker allowed to move before the ball comes down.

The kick has to land in the landing zone. That is the rule that makes the whole thing work, and it is enforced with field position, not flags. A kick that lands short of the landing zone, or goes out of bounds, gives the receiving team the ball at its own 40. A kick that lands in the landing zone is live: the ball is still in play and belongs to nobody until someone possesses it. The receiving team has to field it, because the kicking team may recover it too, once it has hit the ground or a receiving player in the landing zone.17 There is no kneeling on a ball that lands there. (A returner may still signal for a fair catch on a kick he catches in the air, but on a kickoff that only ends the play where he catches it, somewhere inside his own 20, so nobody does.)

A kick that carries into the end zone can be returned or downed. On a kickoff the returner downs the ball by kneeling with it in the end zone (09-01 used “downing” for the kicking team stopping a punt; here it is the receiver’s choice), and that is a touchback. Where the ball goes after a touchback depends on how the ball got into the end zone:

  • Landed in the end zone on the fly (or went out the back): the touchback is at the 35, a generous spot. The league set it that high deliberately, so that kicking it into the end zone costs the kicking team field position (more on this price below).
  • Landed in the landing zone and then rolled or bounced into the end zone: the touchback is only at the 20. The kick did what the rules ask, landing where it could be returned, so the receiving team can’t escape with the generous spot. If a roll-in came out to the 35, every kicker aiming at the goal line would be risking a gift on any kick that ran a yard long, and kickers would stop aiming there.

The last row is the newest wrinkle, and it shows how carefully teams read these rules. When a penalty on the receiving team moves the kickoff to the 50, the kicker is suddenly 15 yards closer. Read across the row: in both 2024 and 2025, kicking the ball out of bounds on purpose put the receiving team at its 25, while a touchback put it at its 30 (2024) or 35 (2025). The loophole was there from the start, but the 2025 touchback move doubled its value from five yards to ten, and that season Dallas tried it against Kansas City.18 For 2026 the league closed it: a touchback on a kick from the 50 now gives the receiving team the ball at its 20, five yards worse for it than the 25 an out-of-bounds kick still gives, so a deep kick into the end zone is the better choice again.19

ImportantWhy it exists

The 2018 rules had already taken away the running start. What they had not changed was the distance: the coverage still started level with the ball, 40 or more yards from where the blocks would happen, and had the whole flight of the ball plus a long sprint to build speed before contact. The dynamic kickoff moves the starting line. The coverage now stands 25 yards downfield, five yards from the players who will block it, and nobody may move until the ball comes down. So when two players meet, each has run a few yards, not forty. The return survives because the kick has to land where it can be returned, and the touchback is set high enough that kicking into the end zone costs the kicker. The trade-off: the play looks strange, kick strategy became complicated (the rest of this chapter), and the coverage team now has very little time to beat blocks.

Did it work? In the 2024 season, the league reported that the concussion rate on kickoffs fell 43% against the 2021-2023 average, while returns rose 57%.20 That is the combination every earlier change had failed to deliver.

NoteMadden vs. real life

In older video games the kickoff is a meter: press the button, pick the power, watch a cutscene. On a real NFL field in 2026 it is a designed play on both sides. The kicking team has a plan for exactly where the ball should come down and whether it should bounce first; the return team has a blocking scheme, called in the huddle like a run play and named by direction (“middle right,” “return left”), that depends on where it expects the kick. Special teams coordinators spend as much time on kickoffs now as on any unit they coach.

Three differences matter, and each one has a “why”:

  1. Where the collisions happen. On the left, contact comes around the receiving team’s 25 or 30, after both groups have run a long way. On the right, it comes near the receiving team’s 35, almost where both groups started. Less running before contact means less speed at contact, and the energy in a collision grows with the square of speed: halve the closing speed and you cut the energy to a quarter.
  2. Who decides when the play starts. On the left, the kick started everything. On the right, the ball landing starts everything. That shifts the drama from the kicker’s leg to where, and how, the ball first comes down: how deep, and whether it is caught or bounces. That is the subject of the next sections.
  3. What a return looks like. On the left, a return was a sprint behind a wall of blockers who had turned their backs to the coverage. On the right, it is much closer to a running play: blockers facing the defenders they will block, a few yards apart, with a ball carrier coming at them from behind.
CautionCommon misconception

“The new kickoff is safer because there are fewer returns.” The opposite: there are far more returns than in any season since 2010 (the chart above), and the injury rate went down. The old rules made the play safer by eliminating it; the new rules made the play itself safer.

Kick to tackle: how a return is blocked now

Here is one return from start to finish, drawn as four moments: the ball in the air, the catch, the blocks, the tackle. Read the panels first; the rest of this section explains what you are seeing. One term first: the time the ball spends in the air, from the kick until it comes down, is its hang time. Under the old rules a long hang time gave the coverage more time to run. Under the dynamic kickoff the coverage may not move until the ball comes down, so hang time buys the coverage nothing; it only gives the returners time to settle under the ball.

T_CATCH = 3.9          # hang time: the ball is caught at the 2 about 3.9 seconds after the kick


def wait_until(p, key, t):
    """Delay that starts `key`'s next action at absolute time t (gridiron delays are relative).
    Note: reaches into gridiron's private Play._compile(); fine for this chapter, fragile if the library changes."""
    kf, _, _ = p._compile()
    return max(0.0, t - max(0.0, float(kf[key][-1, 0])))


def to(p, key, pts, kind="path", t=None, speed=None):
    """Move `key` through absolute (d, w) points as a path, block or run, optionally starting at time t."""
    delay = 0.0 if t is None else wait_until(p, key, t)
    if kind == "block":
        start = np.array(p._cur(key))
        return p.block(key, pts=[tuple(np.array(q) - start) for q in pts], delay=delay, speed=speed)
    return {"path": p.path, "run": p.run}[kind](key, pts, absolute=True, delay=delay, speed=speed)


ACTORS = {"B3": "S", "B4": "D", "F3": "D", "KR2": "KR", "K": "K"}


def return_play():
    """A middle-right return against a dynamic kickoff (2026 alignment, legal front line), from kick to tackle.
    Ten blockers (nine in the setup zone plus the second returner) for eleven cover men (ten plus the kicker):
    the return leaves the backside man (L5) and the kicker unblocked. B3 seals R1 inside, B4 and F3 (both
    setup-zone players, so the double team is legal) drive R2 outward, and the second returner, who may not
    join a double team, takes R3, the man F3 would otherwise have had, with a kick-out."""
    kick, ret = dynamic_units(floaters=(-19, -4, 6, 19), kr=((R(4), 4.0), (R(10), -3.0)), labels=ACTORS, k_d=-1.0)
    p = Play(kick, ret, los=KICK_X, to_go=None)
    tc = T_CATCH
    # the ball is in the air: only the kicker and the returners may move
    p.path("K", [(13.0, 0.0)], absolute=True, speed=5.0)            # may not cross midfield (d = 15) yet
    p.path("KR1", [(R(2), 2.0)], absolute=True, speed=1.0)          # drifts under the kick
    p.path("KR2", [(R(9), -1.0)], absolute=True, speed=1.0)
    # caught at t = 3.9: everyone else is released. Cover man -> where he is engaged, his blocker(s) -> where
    # they meet him (blockers take a short drop step to set, so most blocks land around their 33-34),
    # then the pair drifts by (dd, dw).
    eng = {
        "L4": ((31.0, -18.4), [("B1", (32.2, -18.9))], (0.3, 0.0)),
        "L3": ((31.0, -13.2), [("F1", (32.2, -13.5))], (0.3, 0.0)),
        "L2": ((31.0, -8.4), [("B2", (32.2, -8.7))], (0.3, 0.0)),
        "L1": ((31.0, -3.4), [("F2", (32.2, -3.7))], (0.3, 0.0)),
        "R1": ((31.0, 1.6), [("B3", (31.8, 2.7))], (0.2, -0.9)),                         # sealed inside
        "R2": ((31.2, 8.6), [("B4", (32.3, 9.6)), ("F3", (32.4, 7.4))], (0.2, 0.6)),     # doubled, driven out
        "R4": ((31.0, 18.2), [("F4", (32.2, 18.5))], (0.3, 0.0)),
        "R5": ((31.0, 22.4), [("B5", (32.2, 22.2))], (0.3, 0.0)),
    }
    for c, ((d, w), blockers, (dd, dw)) in eng.items():
        to(p, c, [(d, w)], t=tc, speed=5.5)
        to(p, c, [(d + dd, w + dw)], speed=0.5)
        for bk, (bd, bw) in blockers:
            to(p, bk, [(bd, bw)], kind="block", t=tc + 0.15, speed=3.0)
            to(p, bk, [(bd + dd, bw + dw)], kind="block", speed=0.5)
    # R3 reads the return and works toward the lane; the second returner leads up and kicks him out
    to(p, "R3", [(29.6, 13.6)], t=tc, speed=5.5)
    to(p, "R3", [(31.4, 12.9)], speed=0.9)
    to(p, "R3", [(31.7, 14.7)], speed=1.2)
    to(p, "KR2", [(R(17), 5.5), (32.5, 12.0)], kind="block", t=tc - 0.4, speed=7.0)
    to(p, "KR2", [(32.8, 13.8)], kind="block", speed=1.2)
    # the two unblocked men: L5 pursues flat from the backside, staying behind the ball; the kicker is the
    # returner's man, waiting past the line of blocks
    to(p, "L5", [(27.0, -9.0), (27.8, 2.4)], t=tc, speed=5.2)
    to(p, "K", [(25.0, 3.0)], t=tc + 0.1, speed=4.2)
    to(p, "K", [(27.6, 4.4)], speed=2.0)
    # the returner: up through the lane between the seal (S) and the double team (D + D), tackled past it
    to(p, "KR1", [(R(14), 3.8), (R(26), 4.8), (32.0, 5.0), (29.5, 5.0), (27.8, 4.6)], kind="run", t=tc, speed=8.2)
    return p


def kick_tracking(p, t_catch=T_CATCH, catcher="KR1"):
    """Simulated tracking (BDB format) with the kick's flight added. gridiron's ball follows a snap, so the
    ball rows are rebuilt: tee -> catch along a straight ground track, then in the returner's arms."""
    df = p.to_tracking()
    df["_id"] = df["displayName"].astype(str)
    catch = p.position(catcher, t_catch)
    b = df["side"] == "ball"
    for i in df.index[b]:
        t = df.at[i, "time"]
        if t <= 0:
            d, w = 0.0, 0.0
        elif t < t_catch:
            d, w = catch * (t / t_catch)
        else:
            d, w = p.position(catcher, t)
        df.at[i, "x"], df.at[i, "y"] = p.to_abs(d, w)
    df.loc[b, "event"] = None
    df.loc[b & np.isclose(df["time"], 0.0), "event"] = "kickoff"
    df.loc[b & np.isclose(df["time"], round(t_catch, 1)), "event"] = "kick_received"
    return df

A return under the dynamic kickoff is a run play with a twist: the “snap” is the moment the ball comes down, and nobody knows exactly when or where that will be until the kicker kicks. Start with the arithmetic, the same hat math a run play uses.

The coverage team has eleven potential tacklers: ten on the 40, plus the kicker. The return team always has ten blockers: everyone except the player who catches the ball. Usually that is nine in the setup zone plus the second returner; a team that uses one returner puts ten in the setup zone instead. Eleven hats against ten blockers means one hat will always be unblocked, and a return scheme starts by choosing which. The usual answer has two parts:

  • The kicker is the returner’s man. He starts 25 yards behind everyone else, so nobody blocks him; the returner is taught to beat him one on one (“make the kicker miss”). That is why the kicker became a last-line tackler, and why kickers started preparing for contact: the Ravens’ Justin Tucker put on weight before the 2024 season for exactly this reason.21
  • One cover man on the backside goes unblocked, the one farthest from where the return is going, because he has the longest way to run. In the panels that is L5, on the far left.

That frees one blocker to make a double team at the point of attack, the spot the return is aimed at. There is one rule to respect: only players who lined up in the setup zone may take part in a double team (and the old wedge, two or more blockers shoulder to shoulder moving forward together, is still banned, a 15-yard penalty).22 So the second returner never joins the double team. Instead a setup-zone player doubles, and the second returner takes the man that player would have blocked.

What to take from the four panels:

  • Nothing happens for almost four seconds. For the whole hang time the blockers and the coverage stand five yards apart doing nothing. When you watch on Sunday, use those seconds: count the floaters, find the double team before it happens (two blockers lined up close to one cover man), and guess the return’s direction.
  • The blocks are run-game blocks. The blocker who keeps his man to the inside, away from the lane, is making a seal block. The two who take one man together are a drive double team (coaches say a “deuce”), the same block as on a power run: there is no second-level defender here for one of them to climb to. The second returner leading through the hole and hitting the first defender to show is a lead block that ends as a kick-out. A punt return, by contrast, is usually built around a return wall set up along one sideline, because punt blockers have much more time and space.
  • The scheme decides who is unblocked, and the coverage knows it. Good coverage units teach the players farthest from the ball to pursue flat along the field toward the ball while staying behind it, so they close the cutback lane if the returner reverses field (coaches call this keeping lane integrity). They are the men the return chose not to block, and if they run straight upfield instead, the cutback is open.
  • The ceiling is low and the floor is high. With the coverage only five yards away when the ball comes down, most returns are stopped somewhere between the 25 and the 35. Big returns still happen, but they need a missed tackle by one of the unblocked men: there were 6 kickoff-return touchdowns in the 2025 regular season and 7 in 2024, fewer than in 2010 (25, the last year kicked from the 30).23 Penalties take away more: blockers start face to face with cover men who rip and swim past them, so holding and blocks in the back (10 yards each) are common, and early movement by either unit is a 5-yard foul that can force a re-kick.24 In 2026 the league moved four officials onto the receiving team’s goal line on kickoffs, looking back at the blocks, because holding and illegal blocks were being missed from the sidelines.25

The two unblocked men still run 20-plus yards before they reach the returner, so the play is not free of high-speed collisions. The rules took the speed out of the other nine matchups, and the league’s injury numbers (above) measure the play as a whole. With contact at five yards and no wedge, staffs also changed who plays: special teams coordinators began filling return units with bigger players who are comfortable blocking what amounts to a run scheme, tight ends, linebackers and even defensive linemen.26

ImportantWhy it exists

Why double-team anybody when you are already a blocker short? Because a one-on-one block between a blocker standing still and a cover man running at him is a coin flip, and the return only needs one lane. Spending two blockers on the cover man aligned in the lane turns that coin flip into a near-certainty, and the cost (an unblocked man on the far side) is paid in time: he has 20-plus yards farther to run. Double teams are still restricted (setup-zone players only) and wedges are still illegal. What changed in 2024 is that with everyone starting five yards apart, a double team at the point of attack became practical: the two blockers start close enough to the cover man to reach him together.

The kicker’s menu: touchback, landing zone, or squib

Now the question from the start of the chapter. Under the 2025-26 rules the kicker has four basic choices:

  1. Kick it into the end zone. The returner will usually kneel, and the receiving team starts at its 35.
  2. Kick it deep into the landing zone, ideally near the goal line, so that the returner fields it at the 2 or 3 and has 30-plus yards to run before he reaches blocks the coverage reached after five, or so that it bounces and rolls into the end zone (a knee there gets only the 20). Kickers also aim at a corner of the landing zone: the sideline acts as a twelfth defender, and it tells the return which way it has to go. They can’t aim too close, though, because a kick that rolls out of bounds puts the ball at the 40.
  3. Kick it low so it hits the ground early and skips through the landing zone, a squib kick (coaches also call the line-drive version a bounce kick): hard to field cleanly, it releases the coverage the moment it lands, and it is usually aimed away from the most dangerous returner.
  4. Kick it somewhere else: short of the landing zone or out of bounds, which is dead and gives the receiving team the ball at the 40. Nobody does this on purpose (except, in 2024-25, from the 50).

Why does a deeper kick do better? Because the coverage always starts in the same place. Wherever the kick comes down, the cover men are released from the 40 and reach the blockers in a second or so; the only thing the kicker controls is how far the returner has to run to get there. A returner who fields the ball at his 2 has about 33 yards to cover before he reaches the line of blocks, so those blocks have to hold for three or four seconds. One who fields it at his 15 reaches the blocks at full speed before they have broken down.

Two more things jump out.

In 2025, every returnable kick was a better deal for the kicking team than a touchback. Kicks fielded at the goal line led to drives starting around the 28; kicks fielded at the 10, around the 31. All of them are short of the 35 the touchback would have given, and at about 0.07 points a yard the gap is worth something on every kick. The nflverse play values say the same thing in one line. Measured, as EPA always is, against the value of the moment just before the kick, in 2025 a kickoff that was returned was worth, on average, +0.19 expected points to the receiving team, and a touchback +0.47. A kick into the landing zone that bounced into the end zone and was downed at the 20 was worth -0.38. Those numbers already include the risk: the occasional long return and return touchdown, and the occasional fumble.

The league set the touchback at the 35 on purpose. In 2024, with the touchback at the 30, kicking into the end zone was the safe, nearly break-even choice, and most teams took it: 64% of kickoffs were touchbacks. Moving it to the 35 made the touchback a gift of field position, so kickers stopped giving it. The touchback rate fell to 21% in 2025, and the return rate went from 32.9% to 74.6%.27 That is the touchback acting as a price, set by the league to buy returns.

So why does anyone still kick it into the end zone? Because a landing-zone kick has its own risks: a kick that comes up short of the 20 is a disaster (the ball at the 40), and a kick fielded at the 15 gives the returner a running start at blocks that are still holding. Some kickers trust their accuracy more than others, and some coverage units are better than others. Late in a half, the clock changes the math too (Drill 2 below).

The squib deserves a word of its own. Under the old kickoff a squib was often aimed at a front-line blocker, who had to field it. Under the dynamic kickoff that is a giveaway: a kick that first touches a setup-zone player (at their 30-35) or the ground short of the 20 is dead, and the ball goes to the receiving team at its 40. So today’s squib is a low line drive that must first touch the ground inside the 20, and it is aimed away from the dangerous returner, at the up returner or at the gap between the two. Kansas City’s special teams coordinator, Dave Toub, put the idea in a sentence before the 2024 season: “We’re trying to get the ball on the ground away from the returners as quick as possible.”29 Because it is live the moment it lands, the coverage is released while the returner is still chasing the ball. You see it most at the end of a half and against returners who are simply too good to kick to (in 2025 the best of them, with 25 or more returns, averaged around 30 yards a return).30 Its risks are the mirror of its advantages: it gives up a few yards of field position, and a squib that first hits the ground short of the 20 puts the ball at the 40.

The returner’s decision: kneel or run?

The returner has a menu too, and it hinges on one detail that the broadcast rarely explains: where the ball first hit the ground. He knows because he is standing under it; the officials know because, in 2026, four of them stand on the goal line for every kickoff.31 From the couch, watch the bounce on the replay, and watch the returner: one who lets a bouncing ball roll into the end zone, picks it up and runs is almost always reacting to a roll-in.

  • If the kick came down in the end zone, kneeling gives his team the ball at the 35. Running it out has to beat the 35 to be worth it, and in 2025 it usually didn’t: the 125 kicks returned from the end zone started, on average, at the 26, and only 9% reached the 35. (Those figures pool end-zone landings with roll-ins, which play-by-play doesn’t separate, so read them as a rough guide.)32
  • If the kick came down in the landing zone and rolled into the end zone, kneeling only gets the 20. Now running it out is almost always right.
  • If the kick came down in the landing zone and stayed there, he has to field it and return it; if he lets it sit, the kicking team may fall on it.

Watch for returners getting this wrong. A returner who catches the ball two yards deep in the end zone and runs it out into a wall of coverage at the 22 has cost his team about 0.9 expected points against a knee.

The clock adds one more wrinkle. The game clock does not start on a kickoff until the ball is legally touched in the field of play, so a touchback (or a knee in the end zone) takes no time off, while a return runs the clock from the moment the returner touches the ball.33 Drill 2 turns on this.

The edge cases. The rulebook answers the questions a careful watcher asks next:34

  • A kick lands in the landing zone and then rolls out of bounds. That is still a kick out of bounds: the ball goes to the receiving team at its 40, unless a receiving player touched it last.
  • A returner muffs a kick in the landing zone. The ball is live, and the kicking team may recover it. If it does, it keeps the ball where it recovered it.
  • A returner catches the ball at his 3 and backs into the end zone, where he is tackled. Because he, not the kick, carried the ball back over the goal line, that is a safety, unless his momentum from the catch carried him in.
  • A free kick after a safety. The kicker kicks from his own 20, but his ten teammates still line up on the receiving team’s 40, so they are 40 yards ahead of him rather than 25. A kick out of bounds or short of the landing zone gives the receiving team the ball 30 yards from the kick, at midfield.
  • Overtime. Overtime starts with an ordinary kickoff under the same rules.

Onside kicks and the hands team

An onside kick is the kicking team’s attempt to get the ball back. An onside kick becomes a free ball once it reaches the receiving team’s restraining line, 10 yards away (or once a receiving player touches it), and either team may recover it. So the kicker hits a short, hard, bouncing kick, usually toward one sideline, and his teammates try to get to it after it passes the 10-yard mark. The classic technique is to drive the top of the ball into the ground so it takes one big hop, high enough to come down where the kicking team’s players are arriving; on a declared onside kick the rules let the ball lean against the tee, or be held, which helps the kicker strike it that way.35

The receiving team’s answer is the hands team: when it expects an onside kick, it replaces its usual blockers with its surest-handed players (receivers, tight ends, running backs and defensive backs), lined up close to the ball, whose only job is to catch or smother a bouncing kick.

The onside kick has a different alignment from the dynamic kickoff: the kicking team lines up on its own 35 beside the ball, not on the receiving 40, and the receiving team ten yards away, much like the old kickoff.36 It has to: the target is only ten yards from the tee, and every kicking player must be behind the ball when it is kicked, so coverage players standing 25 yards downfield would be past the spot they are trying to reach. Under every version of the dynamic kickoff, an onside kick also has to be declared to the officials before the kick. In 2024 it was allowed only for a team trailing in the fourth quarter; from 2025 any time a team is trailing; and from 2026, changed after the 2025 season, at any time at all, even when tied or ahead.37 Declaring and then booming it deep is not an option: a declared onside kick that goes untouched past the receiving team’s 15-yard onside setup zone is a 15-yard unsportsmanlike-conduct penalty, and the receiving team takes the ball.

That word “declared” changed the play completely. A surprise onside kick, the most famous trick play in the kicking game, is now illegal: the receiving team always knows it is coming, so the hands team is always on the field. Add the lost running start (gone since 2018), and the recovery rate collapsed. Under the dynamic kickoff, declared onside kicks were recovered by the kicking team 6% of the time in 2024 and 10% in 2025 (about 50 tries a season, so the figures are noisy).38 In October 2025 the league’s head of football operations, Troy Vincent, said that “when you start getting a less than 5% recovery rate,” it may be time for the owners to “revisit” the play.39 He was looking at the start of the 2025 season: through Week 7, kicking teams had recovered 1 of 21 declared onside kicks, about 5%. The rest of the season was kinder, which is how the full-season figure ended near one in ten.40 A proposed alternative, letting a trailing team keep the ball by converting a fourth-and-long play from scrimmage instead, has been voted down or withdrawn several times.41

Why allow an onside kick “at any time” in 2026? It does not bring the surprise back, since it still has to be declared. What it adds is the option for teams that are not trailing. The clearest case is a tie: a team kicking off late in a tied game, or to start overtime. The overtime rules guarantee each team a chance to possess the ball, and a kickoff counts as the receiving team’s chance, so a kicking team that recovers an onside kick to open overtime has used up the other team’s guaranteed possession.42 It is a long shot either way, but it now exists.

CautionCommon misconception

“An onside kick is a desperation play that works about one time in five.” It rarely worked even that often: from 2010 to 2017 kicking teams recovered roughly one onside kick in seven, and only surprise kicks did much better.43 Under the declared-onside rules, it works roughly one time in ten or worse, because the receiving team is never surprised and the kicking team has no running start.

Field position, kickoff by kickoff: what it adds up to

Pull the threads together. In 2025 the average drive after a kickoff started at the 30.3, against 25.1 in 2023 (onside kicks and return touchdowns excluded).44 That league-wide shift helps both teams equally, since both teams receive kickoffs; it changes how games are played (drives start closer to scoring range), not who wins them.

What decides games is the gap between two teams’ kickoff units. Here the arithmetic is simple. A team kicks off about 87 times in a season. The best kicking unit of 2025 held opponents about 2.4 yards below the league average after its kickoffs, worth about 14 points over the season at 0.07 points a yard; the worst gave up about 2.7 yards more than average, costing about 16. Between the two, that is roughly 30 points, several field goals, earned on a play most viewers use to get a snack.

There is a quieter consequence too. A drive that starts at the 30 instead of the 25 is that much closer to field-goal range. Fourth-down and end-of-half decisions in Clock and Game Management start from where the drive started, and the dynamic kickoff moved that starting point up the field.

NoteFilm room: New Orleans Saints 2024

What happened: in the first season of the dynamic kickoff, many teams simply kicked into the end zone and took the touchback at the 30. The Saints’ Blake Grupe went the other way: every one of his first nine kickoffs of the season landed at or inside the 5-yard line.45 Over the season, the Saints’ opponents started their drives after kickoffs at the 28.2 on average, the best in the league; the league average was the 29.6.46

What to notice: on a Saints kickoff, watch the ball, not the returner. Does it come down near the goal line, not the 15? Does it bounce before it is fielded, so the coverage is already moving while the returner chases it? That combination, a kick that comes down deep in the landing zone and is hard to field cleanly, was the blueprint other teams copied once the touchback moved to the 35.

NoteFilm room: Chicago Bears 2024, Week 1

What happened: in the first regular-season week of the dynamic kickoff, the Bears popped a good return against Tennessee by having two blockers double-team one cover man and pulling another blocker across to pick off a cover man who would otherwise have been unblocked. Analysts singled it out, and at least nine other teams used double teams on returns that same opening week.47 Under the current rulebook only setup-zone players may take part in a double team, so a scheme like this builds its extra hat from the setup zone, not from the second returner.

What to notice: on any 2025 or 2026 return, look for two receiving players lined up close together across from one cover man, or one blocker who moves sideways at the catch instead of straight ahead. That pair is the point of attack, and the returner’s lane will be beside it.

NoteFilm room: New Orleans Saints, Super Bowl XLIV (2009 season)

What happened: the Saints opened the second half of Super Bowl XLIV, on February 7, 2010, with a surprise onside kick. Thomas Morstead’s short kick bounced off a Colts player and New Orleans recovered, then drove for a touchdown in a 31-17 win.48 Sean Payton’s call, known as “Ambush,” is probably the most famous onside kick ever.

What to notice: everything that made it work is now illegal or gone. The Colts’ return team was set up for a deep kick, with no hands team on the field, because nobody expected an onside kick to open a half while a team was trailing by only four. Under the declared-onside rule that surprise cannot happen; the officials would tell the receiving team, and the hands team would trot on.

TipWatch for it

On Sunday: the field-position ledger. Kickoffs come in bunches (after every score and to start each half), so there is time to read each one.

Before the kick

  • Count the receiving team’s players on its 35 and the floaters behind them. Under the 2026 rules expect five on the line and up to four behind. Is there a pair of floaters close together across from one cover man? That is a likely double team, and the return will probably go beside it.
  • How many returners? Two deep usually means a standard return. One deep means ten in the setup zone, so count six on the 35; the team may expect a short or bouncing kick.
  • Late in a half, or with a dangerous returner: expect a squib, or a kick toward a corner, away from him.

While the ball is in the air

  • Where does it come down? End zone in the air (kneel = the 35), landing zone and roll-in (kneel = the 20), landing zone (field it), or short (ball at the 40).
  • Did it bounce first? A bouncing ball releases the coverage while the returner is still fielding it. How deep was it caught? The deeper the catch, the longer the blocks must hold.

After the play

  • Write down the starting yard line. Do it for every kickoff by both teams, and average each column at the end of the game.
  • Compare with the league average: about the 30 in 2025. A team whose kickoffs are consistently two yards better than its opponent’s is gaining more than half a point a game (two yards, times about 0.07 points a yard, times the five or so kickoffs each team makes), quietly.
  • Did a returner run a ball out of the end zone and get stopped short of the 35? That is a mistake worth noting. Was a good return called back for holding? Note that too.

TipAnswer

Declare the onside kick. If you kick deep with no timeouts, the other team takes over and kneels; each kneel keeps the clock running and uses up to 40 seconds of play clock, so the game ends without you touching the ball again. Your only hope is to recover the kick. The receiving team knows it is coming (the kick had to be declared), so it puts its hands team on the field: receivers, tight ends, backs and defensive backs, players chosen for their hands rather than their blocking. Your kicker will aim a hard, hopping kick toward one sideline so his fastest players can get to it just past the 10-yard line. Your chances are poor: under the declared-onside rules, kicking teams recovered 6% of onside kicks in 2024 and 10% in 2025. And recovering is only step one: you would still need a touchdown and a two-point conversion in under a minute with no timeouts, so your real chance of winning is far smaller than the recovery rate. Poor beats zero, though. With more time and timeouts (say 3:30 and three timeouts) the answer flips: kick deep, play defense, and use the timeouts to get the ball back with good field position, because a failed onside kick hands the other team the ball near midfield. Where exactly the line falls is a win probability question.

Takeaways

  • A kickoff is a free kick that trades field position after a score. For fifteen years the league made it safer by making it disappear, until only about a quarter of kickoffs were returned in 2023.
  • The dynamic kickoff (2024) took away the distance instead: the coverage stands on the receiving team’s 40, five yards from blockers in the setup zone, and nobody but the kicker and returners moves until the ball lands in the landing zone. Returns rose and kickoff concussions fell.
  • The rules keep moving. 2025: touchback to the 35, onside kicks declarable whenever trailing. 2026 (changed after the 2025 season): onside kicks declarable at any time, only five receivers on the restraining line with up to four floaters, and touchbacks on kicks from the 50 spotted at the 20.
  • The touchback math drives everything: in 2025 a returned kick started, on average, around the 29 to 31, short of the 35 a touchback gives, and the deeper the kick was fielded the worse the return did, because the coverage always starts at the 40. So kickers aim deep into the landing zone, returners should usually kneel on a ball that lands in the end zone, and they should run out one that rolled in.
  • A return is now a run play: ten blockers for eleven cover men, so the scheme leaves the kicker for the returner to beat and one backside cover man free, and spends the spare blocker on a double team (setup-zone players only) at the point of attack.
  • Onside kicks must be declared, so the hands team is always waiting and the surprise onside is gone. Recovery rates fell to around one in ten or worse.
