# --- scenes: simulated tracking plus extra actors (officials), as a video or a frame strip ------
def kf_interp(kf, t):
    kf = np.asarray(kf, dtype=float)
    return np.array([np.interp(t, kf[:, 0], kf[:, 1]), np.interp(t, kf[:, 0], kf[:, 2])])


def draw_scene(ax, play, trk, t, window, lateral, extra=None, decor=None, trails=True, clock=True):
    """One moment of a play on `ax`: players (and ball) from tracking `trk`, extra actors from keyframes."""
    ylo, yhi = sorted((play.ball_y - lateral[1], play.ball_y - lateral[0]))
    fld = Field(ax=ax, orient="vertical", los=play.los, window=window, to_go=play.to_go, lateral=(ylo, yhi))
    no_numbers(ax)
    ppy = fld.pts_per_yard()
    if decor:
        decor(fld, t)
    times = trk.groupby("frameId")["time"].first()
    fid = int((times - t).abs().idxmin())
    cur = trk[trk.frameId == fid]
    hist = trk[trk.frameId <= fid] if trails else None
    draw_frame(fld, cur, hist, ppy)
    for key, (lab, kf) in (extra or {}).items():
        ts = np.linspace(min(np.asarray(kf)[:, 0].min(), 0), t, 30)
        pts = np.array([kf_interp(kf, s) for s in ts])
        xs, ys = zip(*[XY(fld, play, d, w) for d, w in pts])
        if trails:
            ax.plot(xs, ys, color=ZEBRA, lw=0.9, alpha=0.35, zorder=3)
        official(ax, xs[-1], ys[-1], lab, ppy, r=1.15)
    for art in list(ax.patches) + list(ax.texts) + list(ax.lines):
        art.set_clip_on(True)                   # players who ran out of the window don't spill outside it
    if clock:
        ax.set_title("snap" if abs(t) < 1e-6 else f"{t:+.1f} s", fontsize=8.6, color=style.INK_2)
    return fld

Fourth quarter, 1:41 left in a one-score game. A receiver dives along the sideline, the ball pinned to his chest, and tumbles out of bounds. The nearest official, a man in a black cap twenty yards downfield, waves both arms across his body: incomplete. The home crowd roars at the stadium screen, which is replaying the catch from a low angle. The losing coach grabs the red flag from his sock, then stops; he isn’t allowed to throw it. Thirty seconds later, with nobody on either sideline having asked, the official in the white cap walks to the middle of the field, clicks on his microphone and says: “After review, the ruling on the field is reversed. The receiver had control of the ball with both feet down in bounds. The pass is complete.” Who decided that? Why couldn’t the coach challenge? And what exactly did “control with both feet down” have to do with it? This chapter is about the people who run the game and the small set of rules that decide, on almost every close play, who has the ball.

You’ll be able to…

  • Name the seven officials, find each one on a wide shot, and say which fouls he is most likely to throw a flag for.
  • Say, for any close play, whether it can be reviewed and by whom: a coach’s challenge, a booth review, replay assist, or nobody.
  • Rule on a catch the way the replay official will (control, feet, then a football act), and say why the league rewrote the rule in 2018.
  • Apply the rules of the ball: down by contact, forward progress, fumble versus incomplete pass, the fumble through the end zone, and backward versus forward passes.
  • Call intentional grounding before the flag lands, using the tackle box and the line of scrimmage.
  • Decode a referee’s announcement and a virtual measurement, and predict “stands”, “confirmed” or “reversed” before he says it.

Seven officials, seven jobs

An NFL game is run by seven officials on the field, each wearing a number and, on his back, letters that name his job (R, U, DJ, LJ, SJ, FJ, BJ). A seven-person crew sounds like a lot until you count what happens on one snap: 22 players, most of them in contact, spread across 53 yards of width and up to 50 of depth, in about five seconds. So the crew splits the field and the players into pieces. Each official has a starting spot, a few players he is responsible for (his keys), an area to cover once the ball moves, and a short list of fouls that are his to call.

A few things in that table explain a lot of what you see on Sundays.

The wing officials own the line of scrimmage. The down judge and line judge stand exactly on the line at the sidelines so they can look straight down it, the only place from which you can see who is offside and which receivers are on the line (the formation rules need seven). Most pre-snap fouls come from these two. After the snap they drift downfield and mark where a runner’s forward progress stopped: the official you see jog in and point his foot at the spot.

The umpire lives in the trenches, and he moved. For most of football history the umpire, the official responsible for the interior line, stood four or five yards behind the defensive line, in the middle of the linebackers, which is where the holding happens. It was also where umpires kept getting run over. In 2010 the NFL moved him to the offensive backfield, opposite the referee, where he watches the same linemen from behind.2 The trade was visibility for safety: from behind you see a lineman’s hands on a jersey less clearly. (Its side effect was a slower snap, because the umpire also spots the ball after most plays and now had farther to walk; the footnote has the brief exceptions the league tried for hurry-up situations.)

The deep officials are pass officials. The side judge, field judge and back judge are the reason a deep throw can be judged at all. Each takes a share of the receivers, keeps them between himself and the sideline or the end line, and rules on pass interference and illegal contact, catches and out of bounds. The seventh official, the side judge, was added in 1978, the same year the league loosened the rules on pass defense to open up the passing game: more passing meant more eyes downfield.3

Finding them on a wide shot. The letters are too small to read on most camera angles, so use geography. Behind the offense there are two officials: the one in the white cap is the referee, the other is the umpire. On the line at the two sidelines, the one by the chain crew and its sticks is the down judge, the other the line judge. Of the three deep officials, the one in the middle is the back judge; the deep wing on the chain crew’s side is the side judge, and the one on the far side is the field judge.

The referee is the boss, and the voice. The referee wears the white cap and leads the crew. His spot, behind and to the throwing-arm side of the quarterback, lets him watch the one player the rules protect most: he throws most roughing-the-passer flags and decides whether a quarterback who lost the ball was throwing it or fumbling it. Any official can throw a flag; the crew gathers, the one who saw the foul tells the referee, and the referee announces it. A flag “picked up” after that conference usually means another official had a better look.

NoteMadden vs. real life

In a video game a foul is a fact: the code knows a lineman held, and a flag appears. On a real field, a foul exists only if an official in the right spot sees it. A hold on the far side of a run play can be in no official’s view, and a hold on the play side might be seen by two. That is why “they’re letting them play” is sometimes true, and why the same contact draws a flag in front of the line judge and not behind the umpire’s back. Officiating is a sample, not a census.

The crew in motion

Starting spots are only half of it. Watch a wide shot of a deep pass and you’ll see the crew move as a unit: the referee stays with the quarterback; the wing officials read the receivers on their side through the first few yards and then drift downfield, with the line judge usually staying on the line until the ball is thrown so he can judge whether it was thrown from beyond it; and the deep officials backpedal to stay deeper and wider than the receivers they’re judging, so that every catch is seen from the side or facing the receiver, never from behind him.

Who the officials are

NFL officials are mostly part-time, with other careers, working under a labor agreement with their union, the NFL Referees Association; the league’s full-time experiments of 2017, 2019 and 2020 were each dropped. Every official is graded by the league office on every play, and the grades matter: during the regular season a referee leads the same crew week after week, but in the playoffs the crews are broken up and rebuilt from the best-graded individuals at each position, and crew rosters change between seasons as officials retire or move. In 2026 a contract standoff had the league preparing replacement officials (and a one-year rule giving New York wider powers to fix their mistakes) until a seven-year deal was agreed in May, so the regular officials are working this season.4

Even the referee’s microphone has a history: referees were first wired for sound in 1975, under the league’s supervisor of officials Art McNally, who also oversaw the first replay system in 1986 and in 2022 became the first official in the Pro Football Hall of Fame.5

Replay: three doors into the booth

Officials miss things: they watch a five-second play at full speed from one angle. Replay exists to fix the misses that matter most without stopping the game every few minutes, through three separate doors, each with its own rules about who may open it.

Who is in the booth. Two groups of people watch the video. In every stadium a replay official (with an assistant) sits in a booth upstairs with the broadcast feeds; he is an official, not a team employee. In New York, the league’s officiating command center (named Art McNally GameDay Central) watches every game at once. When a review happens, the final decision belongs to the league’s senior vice president of officiating or a designee in New York, consulting the replay official and the referee, who watches on a field-level monitor (a tablet at the sideline). So: the stadium booth starts most reviews, New York decides them, and the referee announces them.6

Each door was added to fix a problem the last one created:7

  • 1986–1991: the first try. An official in the press box could stop the game to review a play; it was slow, overturned little, and was voted out.
  • 1999: the challenge flag. Replay came back with a twist: the coaches would decide which plays to review, by throwing a red flag, and pay for a failed challenge with a timeout. The booth kept control of the final two minutes of each half. The reason: a review stops the clock for a couple of minutes, and a successful challenge costs nothing, so in a close finish a coach could have used a challenge as a free timeout.
  • 2011 and 2012: automatic reviews of every scoring play and every turnover, so a coach no longer had to spend a challenge on the biggest calls.
  • 2017: New York decides. The final say moved from the referee, who had watched replays under a curtained “hood” on the sideline, to New York, so the same few people judge every review.
  • 2019: pass interference, for one season (the story is in Contact Rules and Penalties). Almost nothing was overturned.
  • 2021: replay assist. The booth began fixing clear-cut objective facts on its own, without a formal stop, and its powers grew in 2024, 2025 and 2026 (below).

Every review uses the same standard: the ruling on the field is changed only on clear and obvious visual evidence. That produces the three outcomes in every announcement. Reversed means the video shows the call was wrong. Confirmed means the video shows it was right. Stands means the video doesn’t show enough either way, so the call on the field survives. For the game, “confirmed” and “stands” are the same: the play counts as called, and a coach who challenged loses his timeout either way. The difference is what the video showed, and that is what you can predict: when the camera can’t see the key moment (a ball at the bottom of a pile, a knee hidden by a body), the answer is almost always “stands”.

Door 1: the coach’s challenge

A coach’s challenge is the head coach asking for a review by throwing a red flag onto the field before the next snap. The rules make it scarce on purpose:8

  • Each team gets two challenges a game. Since 2024 it gets a third if either of the first two succeeds; before that, both had to succeed.
  • A team must have a timeout left to challenge, and it loses that timeout if the challenge fails.
  • A coach may not challenge after the two-minute warning of either half, in overtime, or any play the booth reviews automatically (a score, a turnover). A coach who throws his flag anyway is charged a timeout, or 15 yards if he has none left, but the booth still reviews the play if it would have anyway.
  • Only certain rulings are reviewable: whether a pass was caught, whether a runner was down, in bounds or across the goal line, the spot relative to the line to gain or the goal line, who recovered a loose ball, too many men on the field. Judgment fouls, holding or interference, are not. A flag elsewhere on the play doesn’t block a challenge: a coach can challenge a catch on a play that also had a holding call, and the foul is enforced (or declined) after the ruling is settled.

That third rule has a story. On Thanksgiving 2012, Houston’s Justin Forsett was clearly touched down early in what became an 81-yard touchdown run against Detroit. Lions coach Jim Schwartz, furious, threw his red flag, even though every touchdown was already reviewed by the booth. Under the rule of the time, an illegal challenge drew a 15-yard penalty and cancelled the automatic review, so the touchdown stood unreviewed, and Houston won 34–31 in overtime. The next spring the league changed the penalty to a charged timeout and kept the review.9

Why make challenges expensive? Because a free challenge would be thrown on every close play, and each review costs two or three minutes. The timeout charge turns a challenge into a bet (the chance of winning times what the reversal is worth, against the chance of losing times what a timeout is worth later), which the game-management staff upstairs must make in the few seconds before the next snap. It’s also why an offense that likes the call hurries to the line: once the ball is snapped, the play can’t be challenged (In-Game Adjustments and Tempo).

And it’s why challenges are rarer than you’d guess. In 2025 coaches threw 151 challenges in 272 regular-season games, about 0.28 per team per game, and won 52% of them; over 2016–2025 the success rate was 46%.10 A coach who is right about half the time, and is only betting on plays he’s fairly sure of, is telling you how hard it is to see a play clearly from the sideline in ten seconds.

Door 2: booth review

A booth review is started by the replay official in the stadium (or by New York), not by a coach. It covers:

  • every scoring play (touchdowns, field goals, safeties) and every try;
  • every turnover as ruled on the field (an interception, a fumble recovered by the defense or knocked through its end zone, a muffed kick recovered by the kicking team), and every ruling that the offense came up short on fourth down;
  • every reviewable play after the two-minute warning of each half and in overtime.

Most booth reviews never stop the game: a touchdown is looked at during the celebration, and if the video agrees, the game goes on. If the booth wants a closer look, it buzzes the referee and the game stops. A replay review of either kind ends the same way, with the referee at the middle of the field reading New York’s decision.

A reversal also fixes the clock. The clock is reset to the moment the ball should have been dead and then runs or stops as the correct ruling dictates: an incompletion reversed to a catch in bounds puts the ball where the play should have ended, with the clock running as it would have. After the two-minute warning, a reversal that turns a stopped clock into a running one also brings a 10-second runoff, which either team can avoid only by spending a timeout. Those runoffs, and the end-of-half arithmetic around them, belong to The Clock, the Two-Minute Drill, and Fourth Down.11

Door 3: replay assist

The newest door is the quietest. Replay assist lets the booth tell the crew about a clear-cut objective fact before the next snap, usually with no stoppage you’d notice: the ball was spotted a yard short of the line to gain, the runner’s knee was down before the ball came out, the receiver’s second foot landed out of bounds, two seconds should go back on the clock. It began in 2021 as a way to fix the obvious without a coach gambling a timeout on it.

If the booth can fix a wrong spot for free, why would a coach ever challenge one? Because replay assist acts only when the answer is clear at once, in the few seconds before the next snap. If the booth stays quiet, a coach who thinks a longer look would change the call still has his red flag; the rules say replay assist never takes that away.

Since 2024 it has had a little power over flags, always limited to the objective piece of a foul. In 2024 it could wipe out a roughing-the-passer flag for a hit to the head when the video showed no contact with the head or neck, a grounding flag when the passer was clearly outside the pocket, and a late-hit flag when the runner was clearly still in bounds; the judgment part of roughing (was the hit late?) is still not reviewable. In 2025 it added five more: a hit to a defenseless player’s head or neck, a face mask, a horse-collar tackle, tripping, and roughing or running into the kicker, each only when the video plainly shows the key contact never happened. It cannot add a foul that nobody called. And in 2026 its staff may consult with the crew on ejections for flagrant acts: football acts (a hit during the play so violent it warrants ejection, such as a launch into a defenseless receiver’s head) and non-football acts (a punch away from the play). Some reports describe that as replay ejecting players; the league’s official wording is only that it may consult.12

What the NFL still doesn’t have is a sky judge: an official in the booth with the power to call or wipe out any foul in real time. Spring leagues have used one (the USFL’s sky judge wiped out a wrong face-mask flag in a 2023 playoff game), and the idea has been proposed in the NFL since at least 2019.13 The objections are practical: it would slow the game, and it would make every play (on which, coaches like to say, somebody is holding) a candidate for a flag. So the league has expanded replay one narrow, objective slice at a time.

Two patterns are worth carrying into a game. Catch-or-no-catch rulings make up about 40% of all the recorded reviews, which is why the next section spends so long on the catch. The catch

Nothing in the rulebook has caused more arguments than the definition of a catch, because of the stakes. If it was a catch and the ball then comes loose, it’s a fumble: a live ball anyone can recover. If it wasn’t, it’s an incomplete pass: dead, and the offense keeps it. So the rule has to separate possessing a ball from merely touching it, at full speed, often while someone is hitting the receiver.

Today’s rule, rewritten in 2018, says a player has made a catch when, in bounds, he does three things in order:14

  1. Control. He secures control of the ball in his hands or arms before it touches the ground.
  2. Feet (or a body part) down. He touches the ground in bounds with both feet, or with any part of his body other than his hands (a knee, a hip, an elbow).
  3. Time. After the first two, he makes an act common to the game: tucks the ball away, turns upfield, takes an extra step, reaches the ball toward the line, wards off a defender. Or he keeps control long enough that he could have.

Three clarifications do most of the work on replays.

  • Slight movement isn’t a loss of control, and a bobble he then secures is fine, as long as the ball never touches the ground in between.
  • The ground matters only before gate 3. If he finishes gate 3 on his feet (a third step, a turn, a reach) and then falls and the ball comes out, it was a catch and then a fumble, or simply a catch if he was down by contact. But if he is falling while he catches it, so that he hits the ground before he has had time for any football act, the ground is part of the catch: he must keep control when he lands. If the ball pops loose and touches the ground then, the pass is incomplete.
  • A tie goes to the offense. If an offensive and a defensive player catch the ball at the same moment and both keep it, it’s the offense’s ball. If one had it first and the other wrestled in, it belongs to the first.

An interception is judged by exactly the same three gates.

Why the rule was rewritten

For most of the 2010s the rule had an extra clause that produced some of the most hated rulings in league history. A receiver who went to the ground “in the process of the catch” had to keep control of the ball after hitting the ground, the so-called survive the ground requirement, no matter how many steps he had taken or how obviously he had possessed it. The clause was meant to make the rule objective: instead of asking officials to judge whether a player had possession long enough, it gave them a test they could see on video. The trouble was that the objective test kept ruling against what everyone watching had seen. Three plays made it untenable:15

  • Calvin Johnson, Lions at Bears, September 12, 2010. Trailing 19–14 with 31 seconds left, Johnson caught a pass in the end zone, landed with both feet and his backside, and put the ball down on the turf as he rolled to get up. Ruled incomplete; the booth upheld it. The Lions lost by five, and “the Calvin Johnson rule” entered the vocabulary.
  • Dez Bryant, Cowboys at Packers, divisional playoffs, January 11, 2015. On fourth-and-2 late in the game, Bryant caught a deep ball, took three steps as he fell, reached for the goal line, and the ball moved when it hit the turf at the 1. Ruled a catch, reversed on Green Bay’s challenge; the Packers won (the Film room below walks through it).
  • Jesse James, Patriots at Steelers, December 17, 2017. With 34 seconds left and Pittsburgh down 27–24, James caught a short pass a few yards from the goal line, went to a knee untouched (so he wasn’t down: in the NFL a runner needs contact, as the next section explains), and lunged across the goal line; the ball moved as it hit the turf. Ruled a touchdown, reversed by the booth. The Steelers lost the game and, as it turned out, home-field advantage in the playoffs.

The owners approved a rewrite 32–0 in March 2018. Here is precisely what changed. Under the old rule, a receiver who went to the ground “in the process of the catch” had to survive the ground however many steps or reaches he had made on the way down: falling cancelled them. Under the new rule, a football act made before the ball comes loose (a third step, a reach for the line, or time enough to make one) finishes the catch, even if he is falling, and only a receiver who hits the ground before he has made one must still survive it. (The 2018 announcement said “football move”; the rulebook says “act common to the game”.) The league’s football chiefs said publicly that Bryant’s and James’s catches would now count.16

Would Calvin Johnson’s? Try it with the gates. Control: yes, high in the end zone. Feet: both feet, then his backside. Time: the argument. He went to the ground as he caught it, so he must keep control through the landing unless he had time for a football act first. If you see him land, hold the ball, and only then use it to push himself up, he had the time, and it’s a touchdown today. If you see one continuous fall with the ball coming loose as part of it, it’s still incomplete. The league never ruled on it publicly, and that is the honest answer: it’s a judgment about time.

Name the trade: the old rule was objective and produced absurd results; the new one matches what people see, at the price of a judgment (“was that lunge a football act or part of the fall?”) at its center.

CautionCommon misconception

“The ball touched the ground, so it’s incomplete.” The ball is allowed to touch the ground during a catch, as long as the receiver keeps control of it, with his hands under it or the ball pinned to his body. What it can’t do is help him: a ball trapped against the turf with no hand under it, or one that pops loose when he lands before he has had time for a football act, is incomplete.

Sidelines and end zones

Two places put extra weight on gate 2. At the sideline, a receiver needs both feet (or another body part) down in bounds with control; one foot, as in college football, isn’t enough. Since 2008 there has been no “force-out” ruling either: a receiver pushed out of bounds in the air before getting both feet down has not caught the ball, however sure the official is that he would have landed in bounds (unless he had control and was held up and carried out).17 And because a toe-tap leaves no time for a football act, he must keep control as he falls out; a ball jarred loose when he hits the ground out of bounds is incomplete.

In the end zone, the catch is the touchdown, so the receiver must complete all three gates there; a runner who already has the ball only needs it to touch the plane of the goal line (the end zone is generous that way). A receiver who completes the catch short of the line and then reaches across has scored, which is how Jesse James’s play would be ruled today.

Down, progress and the whistle

Most plays end with a ball carrier going down, and the NFL’s definition differs from college’s. A runner is down by contact when any part of his body other than his hands or feet touches the ground and he has been touched by an opponent. The order of those two can go either way. If nobody touched him, he isn’t down: a running back who slips on wet turf, or a returner who kneels to field a bouncing kick, may get up and keep running. But if a defender touches him while he is on the ground, he is down right there, which is why defenders are coached to “touch him down” rather than assume the play is over. In college football the runner is down as soon as his knee touches, contact or not.18 (A runner who slides feet-first or simply gives himself up is down where he does it; that’s how quarterbacks protect themselves.)

The order of events matters more than anything else on these plays. If the ball comes out before the knee (or elbow, or hip) touches after contact, it’s a fumble: live, and anyone’s. If the knee touches first, the runner was down and the ball coming out afterward doesn’t matter. On television these are separated by a few frames of video, which is why they’re among the most-reviewed rulings in the data above.

Forward progress decides where the play ended. You met it in Downs, Distance, and Field Position: the ball is spotted at the farthest point it reached toward the goal line before the runner was stopped, not where he ended up after a pile drove him back. The same idea tells officials when to blow the whistle. A runner wrapped up and held upright, his legs still churning, has had his forward progress stopped even though he hasn’t gone down, and the officials will whistle the play dead rather than let a pile of defenders keep shoving him backward (or strip the ball from a man who has stopped moving forward). It also protects runners at the goal line: a back who gets the ball across the plane and is knocked back has scored. (Only being driven back counts: a quarterback who retreats on his own and is sacked is spotted where he goes down.) And it affects the clock: a runner stopped in bounds and then shoved out of bounds was down in bounds, so the clock keeps running, a detail that matters in the two-minute drill.

The whistle, and the 2008 lesson

The whistle is the one tool officials can’t take back. Once it blows, the play is dead, whatever happens next. (When one is blown by mistake with the ball still live, the team with the ball can usually take it where the whistle killed the play or replay the down; a pass in the air is simply replayed.)19 That used to put replay in an impossible position: if an official ruled a play over and the video showed a fumble, replay could fix the ruling but couldn’t know who would have recovered the ball.

The play that made everyone face this came on September 14, 2008. Denver trailed San Diego 38–31 with 1:17 left, second-and-goal at the 1. Jay Cutler dropped back, and the ball slipped out of his hand as he started to throw; a Charger fell on it. Referee Ed Hochuli ruled it an incomplete pass and blew his whistle. Replay showed a fumble, but because the whistle had already killed the play, the Chargers couldn’t be given the ball. Denver kept it at the 10, where Cutler had lost it, scored on fourth-and-goal with 29 seconds left, went for two, and won 39–38.20 Hochuli, one of the most respected referees of his era, publicly apologized.

Two things changed. In 2009 the owners passed what was quickly nicknamed the “Hochuli rule”: replay may now award the ball to the team that recovered it when the recovery is clear and in the immediate continuing action, even after a whistle.21 And the officials’ habit changed: on a bang-bang fumble, they are taught to hold the whistle, let the players finish the scramble for the ball, and rule a fumble when in doubt, because replay can turn a fumble back into a dead ball cleanly but can’t recreate a recovery that never happened. That is why you now see a defender scoop up a loose ball and run the other way while everyone else has stopped.

Pass or fumble: the quarterback’s arm

A close cousin of down-or-fumble is pass-or-fumble, and it comes up every time a pass rusher hits a quarterback as he throws. The rule is about the arm. Once the quarterback, holding the ball, starts his arm moving forward to throw, anything that comes out is a forward pass, and if it hits the ground it’s incomplete. If he loses the ball before his arm starts forward, or while he is pulling it back down to tuck it after deciding not to throw, it’s a fumble.

The second half of that sentence was not always true. Until 2013 the rulebook had the tuck rule: if the quarterback started forward and then brought the ball back toward his body, it was still a pass until he had tucked it all the way in. It is remembered for the snowy January 2002 playoff in Foxborough, when Tom Brady lost the ball under Charles Woodson’s hit, the Raiders recovered, and replay called it an incomplete pass; New England kept the ball and won in overtime. The league abolished the rule in 2013.22 Today the same play is a fumble.

Who reviews it depends on the call on the field. When the field rules a fumble that the defense recovered, it’s a turnover, so the booth reviews it automatically. When the field rules an incomplete pass, there’s no turnover to trigger the booth, so before the two-minute warning the defense’s coach usually has to throw his red flag (unless replay assist steps in on the possession), which is the moment you see a coach grab it on a strip-sack ruled incomplete. Either way, watch the arm, not the ball: if the arm was coming forward with the ball in the hand, it’s a pass.

A fumble through the end zone

Now the harshest rule in the book. When a fumble goes out of bounds, the general rule is generous to the team that fumbled: it keeps the ball. If the ball went out going backward, it’s spotted where it went out; if it went out going forward, it comes back to the spot of the fumble, so that nobody gains yards by fumbling forward on purpose.

The exception is the opponent’s end zone. A fumble through the end zone, a ball fumbled forward into the end zone that goes out of bounds there, out the side or out the back, is a touchback: the defense gets the ball at its own 20, even though no defender ever touched it.23 A fumble that stays in the end zone is simpler: if the offense falls on it there, it’s a touchdown (on fourth down or late in a half, only the man who fumbled may, as the box below explains); if the defense does, it’s a touchback. And the mirror image works against the offense in its own end zone: a ball it fumbles there that goes out of bounds (or one it fumbles in the field that it sends back into its own end zone and out) is a safety, because the offense supplied the push that put the ball there.

Why would the rules punish the offense so hard? The logic is about territory, not possession. The end zone belongs to the defending team, and when a ball goes dead there because of something the other team did (a punt rolls through, a kickoff sails out the back), it’s a touchback, like the touchbacks on punts. A fumble into your end zone and out is treated the same way. The rule is consistent; it just doesn’t feel fair, because the defense did nothing to earn it. It is one of the most criticized rules in the book; the competition committee looked at softening it in 2017 and again in February 2024 and kept it both times, arguing that the ball carrier is responsible for the ball and that nobody had a workable alternative. It is unchanged in the 2026 rulebook.24

The rule shapes technique: it’s why a ball carrier stretching for the pylon is taking a real gamble, and why defenders near the goal line punch at the ball instead of wrapping up.

ImportantWhy it exists: the fourth-down fumble rule

On September 10, 1978, with the Raiders trailing the Chargers in the final seconds, Ken Stabler, about to be sacked, fumbled the ball forward; teammates batted and kicked it toward the goal line, and Dave Casper fell on it in the end zone for the winning touchdown. The “Holy Roller” was legal then. The next season the league made it impossible: on fourth down, and on any down after the two-minute warning, only the player who fumbled may recover and advance the ball. If a teammate recovers it, the ball comes back to the spot of the fumble (or the spot of recovery, if that is behind it).25 The rule exists to stop an offense from fumbling forward on purpose when it has nothing left to lose.

Backward passes and forward passes

Football allows exactly one forward pass per play, and it must be thrown from behind the line of scrimmage. It allows any number of backward passes (also called laterals), from anywhere, by anyone, at any time. The two are treated very differently when they hit the ground:

  • A forward pass that hits the ground is incomplete. The ball is dead, and the offense keeps it.
  • A backward pass that hits the ground is still live, exactly like a fumble. Either team may recover it, and either team may advance it.

Which is which? The test is the ball’s direction: compare the yard line where it left the passer’s hand with the yard line where it is first touched (or first hits the ground). If the second is closer to the opponent’s goal, the pass was forward. If it’s the same yard line or behind, the pass was backward, so a pass thrown perfectly parallel to the line counts as backward.26 Notice that it’s the ball’s path that counts, not the players’ positions on the field. (This chapter calls the yard line where the ball leaves the passer’s hand the release line; that’s our label for the diagrams, not a rulebook term.)

The forward pass’s two limits are fouls of their own, and the line judge watches for the first. A forward pass is illegal if the passer’s entire body and the ball are beyond the line of scrimmage when he lets go, which is the call you see on a quarterback who scrambles up to the line and flips it at the last moment: one foot still behind the line keeps it legal. A second forward pass on the same play is illegal too. Either costs a loss of down and 5 yards.

Why does a dropped backward pass stay live? Because the rules treat it as the normal case. A backward pass is just a handoff through the air: the ball stays a runner’s ball, and if he doesn’t catch it, it’s loose, like any fumble. The forward pass is the special case, the one the rules ration (one per play, from behind the line) and give a special ending: when it fails, the play stops and the offense keeps the ball, paying with the down. The result shows up in three places on a broadcast:

  • Pitches and option plays. A pitch on the option is a backward pass. Drop it and you’ve fumbled, in effect, which is why option teams drill the pitch relentlessly.
  • Screens. Most shotgun screens are forward passes, caught closer to the line than the five or six yards deep where the quarterback let go. But a bubble or swing screen to a player who has drifted back level with the quarterback may be backward, so defenders are coached to scoop up every dropped screen and run: if it was backward, the ball is theirs. (The illegal-man-downfield rule applies only to a forward pass thrown beyond the line, so on a true backward pass linemen may already be blocking downfield.)
  • Last-play desperation. The multi-lateral kickoff return or final snap, the ball pitched backward again and again, is legal because every pitch is backward.

The most famous backward pass in NFL history came in a wild-card game on January 8, 2000, on a kickoff with 16 seconds left: Tennessee’s Frank Wycheck threw the ball across the field to Kevin Dyson, who ran 75 yards for the winning touchdown. Replay couldn’t show that the throw had gone forward, so the ruling of a legal lateral stood, and the “Music City Miracle” became the standard illustration of the rule.27

Intentional grounding and the tackle box

Quarterback Play introduced the throwaway and the rule that limits it. Here is the full rule, and the reasoning behind it.

Intentional grounding is a foul when a passer who is facing an imminent loss of yardage because of the rush throws a pass that isn’t in the direction and vicinity of an eligible receiver. (The rulebook says originally eligible: eligible when the ball was snapped, so a lineman who has wandered downfield doesn’t count as a target.) Three conditions, all needed:28

  1. Pressure. He must be facing a likely sack. A quarterback under no pressure who throws the ball into the turf has wasted a down, but he hasn’t grounded it.
  2. No realistic target. The pass isn’t thrown near an eligible receiver. “Near” is a judgment, and throwing the ball well over the head of a covered receiver in the right part of the field counts.
  3. Inside the tackle box. The tackle box, which the rulebook calls the pocket area, is the region between the outside edges of where the two offensive tackles lined up, running from the line of scrimmage all the way back to the offense’s own end line. It’s fixed by where the tackles aligned at the snap: a tight end lined up outside a tackle doesn’t widen it, and it doesn’t move when the quarterback does.

The exception is what makes the box matter: a passer who is or has been outside the tackle box may throw the ball away freely, as long as it lands at or beyond the line of scrimmage (extended to the sidelines), including out of bounds past it. Since the 2024 rulebook he counts as outside the box if any part of his body, or the ball, is outside it. And “has been” means what it says: a quarterback who escapes the box and then drifts back inside it keeps the exception for the rest of the play.

The penalty is built to cost about what the sack would have: loss of down and 10 yards from the previous spot, or loss of down at the spot of the pass if that is farther back, and a safety if he throws it from his own end zone. The other exception is the spike: a quarterback who throws the ball straight into the ground immediately after the snap, to stop the clock, loses only the down (more in The Clock, the Two-Minute Drill, and Fourth Down).

Why draw the line at the box? A sack is something the defense earned. If a quarterback could always throw the ball into the ground to avoid one, the pass rush would lose much of its value, so inside the pocket, where the defense has him trapped, he must at least throw toward someone. A quarterback who has escaped the pocket has beaten the rush himself; letting him throw it away (provided it reaches the line, so he can’t just dump it at his feet) rewards the escape. The 2024 wording tilted that balance a little further his way.

The best-known grounding call of recent decades shows the safety clause at work: on New England’s first offensive play of Super Bowl XLVI, February 5, 2012, Tom Brady, pressured in his own end zone, threw deep down the middle to nobody. Grounding from the end zone is a safety, and the Giants led 2–0 before the Patriots had gained a yard. They won by four.29

The change is a nice lesson in two words that sound alike. Precision is how finely you can measure; accuracy is whether you measured from the right place. The chains were not very precise (a clip laid by hand, a chain pulled taut by eye), and they were slow. The cameras are extremely precise. But they measure from the spot, and the spot is still a human judgment: an official running alongside the play, often looking through a pile of bodies, deciding where the ball’s nose was when forward progress stopped. A measurement precise to a fraction of an inch, taken from a spot that could be off by a foot, is only as accurate as the spot. That is why the spot itself can still be reviewed, but only in narrow cases: on a play that’s challenged or booth-reviewed, or through replay assist, the booth can move the spot when the video clearly shows where the ball was relative to the line to gain or the goal line. (Who decides to measure at all? The referee. A team’s captain can ask him for one, but it’s his call.)31

CautionCommon misconception

“The cameras decide first downs now.” They decide the measurement. The officials still spot the ball by eye, and the yellow line on your screen is still the broadcaster’s drawing, not an official mark. When a spot looks wrong, the cameras can’t help; only the replay booth’s look at the spot can.

Reading the referee’s announcement

Every announcement follows the same grammar, and once you know it you can usually finish the referee’s sentence for him. For a foul, the order is: what the foul was, who committed it (offense or defense, and the player’s number), how much it costs and from where, and what happens to the down. For a review it’s: the outcome (stands, confirmed, reversed), the reason in a sentence, and the result (the new down, distance and spot, and anything about the clock).

The enforcement words (spot foul, declined, automatic first down) are taught in Contact Rules and Penalties. Before the announcement, the referee’s arm signal tells you most of it, and a few signals cover most flags: a hand gripping the other wrist for holding; both hands on the hips for offside or encroachment; forearms rolling over each other in front of the chest for a false start; both open hands pushed forward from the shoulders for pass interference; one wrist chopping the other above the head for a personal foul; and both arms, held parallel, waved diagonally across the body for intentional grounding.32

In 2025 an average game had 12.8 accepted penalties, and the crews’ averages ran from 10.2 (Brad Allen’s crew) to 14.6 (Adrian Hill’s).33 That spread sounds large until you set it against the noise. A single game’s total varies a lot (the standard deviation was about 4.3 penalties), so a crew’s 16- or 17-game average would wander about ±2.2 around the league average by luck alone. Most crews sit inside that band. And the ranking usually doesn’t hold: the correlation between a crew’s 2024 and 2025 averages was 0.28, and across the ten seasons from 2016 to 2025 the year-to-year correlation was usually weak (median 0.28), occasionally strong (it ranged from -0.35 to 0.76). Only 12 of the 16 crews landed in the same half of the table both years.

So crews differ a little, and inconsistently. Teams are hardly more predictable: a team’s own rate of accepted fouls has a median year-to-year correlation of 0.32 over the same seasons. Most of a single game’s flag count, in other words, is noise: who happened to hold on which snap, in front of which official. And some of the variation is the league’s doing, not the crews’: the league-wide average swings with points of emphasis, from 11.2 to 13.5 per game across 2016–2025, which is why a preseason memo telling officials to watch for a particular foul can move the numbers for every crew at once.

Watch for it on Sunday

TipWatch for it: the ref’s mic

Every time a flag flies, or a ruling is close, make two calls before the referee turns on his microphone:

  1. Name the official. Who threw it? A flag from the line of scrimmage at a sideline is the down or line judge (pre-snap foul, likely false start, offside or illegal formation). A flag from deep downfield is a side, field or back judge (likely pass interference or illegal contact). A flag from the backfield, near the quarterback, is the referee (roughing the passer, grounding) or the umpire (holding in the middle).
  2. Name the foul or the ruling, and its result: how many yards, from where, and the down that follows.
  3. On every review, predict the outcome: reversed, confirmed or stands. First ask who started it (a coach’s flag, or the booth: after the two-minute warning, a score, a turnover). Then ask which angle would settle it. If the decisive moment is hidden (a pile, a body in the way), say stands.
  4. On every close catch, count the gates out loud: control, feet, time.

Keep a tally for one game, and add your review predictions to your prediction log. You’ll miss plenty at first; by the end of a season the official-then-foul habit will have you announcing it before the referee does.

Takeaways

  • Seven officials split the field and the players: the referee (white cap) the quarterback, the umpire the interior line, the down and line judges the line of scrimmage and sidelines, the side, field and back judges the receivers downfield. Where a flag came from tells you what it is likely to be.
  • Replay asks two questions in order. Is the ruling reviewable at all (judgment fouls aren’t)? Does the booth review it automatically (scores, tries, turnovers, short on fourth down, after the two-minute warning, overtime)? If not, only a coach’s challenge, paid for with a timeout, can open it. Replay assist runs alongside on clear objective facts and the objective part of a short list of flags. There is no sky judge.
  • Reversals need clear and obvious evidence, so a hidden moment usually “stands”; “stands” and “confirmed” leave the play exactly as called.
  • A catch is three gates: control, feet down, then time. Since 2018 a football act made on the way down finishes the catch; only a receiver who hits the ground before he’s had time for one must survive the ground. The rule now matches what people see, at the price of a judgment about time.
  • The order of events decides possession, and direction and territory decide the rest. Ball out before the knee is a fumble; an NFL runner needs to be touched to be down. A forward fumble out of bounds comes back to the spot of the fumble, but into the opponent’s end zone and out it’s a touchback. A pass that doesn’t go forward is a backward pass and stays live if dropped. Grounding is about the box and the line: once outside the box, a throwaway just has to reach the line.
  • Cameras measure, people spot, and crew differences in flags are small and unstable from year to year.
