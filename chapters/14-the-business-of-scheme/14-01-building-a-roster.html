def smooth(x, y, at, h=0.45):
    """Gaussian kernel average of y against log(pick): picks change fast at the top, slowly at the end."""
    lx, la = np.log(np.asarray(x, float)), np.log(np.asarray(at, float))

    return (w * np.asarray(y, float)[None, :]).sum(1) / w.sum(1)

The problem: 53 players, one budget, and a draft

An NFL general manager has three constraints that no fantasy league or Madden franchise mode quite captures.

The cap is hard. Every team gets the same salary cap for each league year, the NFL’s business year, which starts in mid-March. It is set from a share of league revenue: $279.2 million for 2025 and $301.2 million for 2026, the first time it has passed $300 million.1 Unlike baseball or the NBA, there is no luxury tax that lets a rich owner pay his way over it. A dollar spent on one player is a dollar that can’t be spent on anyone else, so every contract is a decision about the whole roster.

The roster is big and the game is specialized. A team carries 53 players, dresses 48 on game day, and needs starters at about 25 different jobs once you count the kicker, punter and long snapper. A left tackle can’t play cornerback. So the budget can’t simply follow “buy the best players”; it has to buy a team, with a backup at every spot the season will expose.

Most talent arrives through a draft you don’t control. The worst teams pick first, and rookies are paid on a fixed scale. That makes the draft the cheapest source of good players, and it means a team’s cost of talent depends on when it acquired a player as much as on how good he is.

Put those together and the GM’s real question isn’t “who is good?” It is “where does the next dollar, or the next pick, buy the most winning?” The rest of this chapter is the toolkit for answering it.

ImportantWhy it exists: the salary cap

The cap is a deal between the owners and the players’ union, renewed in each collective bargaining agreement (the current one, signed in 2020, runs through the 2030 season). The players get a fixed share of league revenue, at least 48 percent, paid out through the cap and benefits; the owners get cost certainty and a league in which a small-market team can afford the same roster as a big-market one.2 The trade-off is the one this chapter keeps returning to: under a hard cap, overpaying anyone is never free, because someone else on the roster pays for it.

NoteMadden vs. real life

In Madden’s franchise mode, cutting an expensive veteran usually frees his whole salary. In the NFL, a release can cost cap space this year, because of the bonus money already paid (the dead money section below shows why). And no real GM can trade three backups for a star the way the game’s AI often accepts: picks and players are priced by charts every front office knows.

That chart is positional value as the market sees it: how much a position, not a particular player, contributes to winning, and so how much it’s worth paying for. Quarterback is alone at the top, at 22.4% of the cap for a top-ten deal. Then come the premium positions: edge rusher and wide receiver, then a cluster of interior defensive linemen, offensive tackles and cornerbacks at about 9 to 11 percent. Below them come guards and centers at about 8.5 percent, then safeties, off-ball linebackers, running backs and tight ends at roughly 5.5 to 7 percent. (Interior defensive line is high because the market pays a few rare interior pass rushers like edge rushers; a run-stuffing tackle is paid far less.) Each step has a reason.

Quarterback touches every snap. He decides where the ball goes on every pass and many runs, and Expected Points showed that passing plays carry more value per snap than runs and swing far more widely. A position that controls the highest-leverage action on every play is worth more than one that matters on a third of them.

The premium group decides the passing game. An edge rusher who wins one-on-one ends drives with sacks and hurried throws; the offensive tackles are the men assigned to stop him. The old idea was that only the left tackle mattered, because he guards a right-handed quarterback’s blind side, the side he can’t see. The market no longer believes it: 4 of the ten richest tackle contracts belong to right tackles. A receiver who wins against man coverage gives the quarterback somewhere to throw; and a cornerback who can cover him alone frees the rest of the defense to help elsewhere. Every one of those players wins or loses a matchup on most passing downs, and they are scarce: you can’t develop a 6-foot-5, 265-pound man who runs a 4.5 forty from the seventh round.

The bottom group is either replaceable or rarely decisive. Running back is the classic case. Most of a run’s yards are created by the blockers and the scheme before the back touches the ball (13-02’s rush-yards-over-expected showed how little separates most backs once you account for the blocking), backs wear down quickly, and the college game produces good ones every year. Off-ball linebackers and safeties matter, but they win fewer one-on-one matchups that decide plays, and they are easier to find.

CautionCommon misconception

“Running backs don’t matter.” Positional value is about price, not about whether a position matters. Saquon Barkley ran for 2,005 yards for the 2024 Eagles and was central to a Super Bowl run (the Eagles case study tells it). The argument is narrower: the gap between a great back and a decent one is smaller than the gap between their salaries, so the money usually buys more at another position. The Eagles could pay Barkley because they had built cheaply elsewhere, which is the real lesson of positional value.

Does that mean positional value is a myth? No. It means price and value are different things, for three reasons.

  1. The cap is zero-sum. Spending 20 percent of the cap on a quarterback means spending 20 percent less on everyone else. A team that pays one star well and fills the rest with bargains can end up anywhere.
  2. Contracts pay for the past. Teams sign big deals with players who were great, and pay them through their late twenties and early thirties, when many decline. A big share can be a reward for seasons already played.
  3. The best bargains are on rookie contracts. The cheapest good players in the league are young ones on the rookie wage scale, which is the subject of the draft section. A team with a great young quarterback spends less on the position than a team overpaying a mediocre veteran.

How a contract really hits the cap

A headline says “five years, $150 million.” Almost none of that number tells you what the team will actually carry on its cap in a given year, or what it would cost to get out of the deal. Four ideas do.

The cap hit is what a contract counts against the cap in one league year: the player’s base salary for that year, plus the year’s share of his signing bonus, plus any roster, workout or incentive bonuses counted that year. It is an accounting number, not the cash paid. The broadcast graphic shows it because it’s the number that limits the team.

Signing-bonus proration is the rule that creates the gap between cash and cap. A signing bonus is paid to the player up front (often within months), but for cap purposes it is spread evenly over the years of the contract, up to a maximum of five. That is why teams love signing bonuses: the player gets the cash now, and the team spreads the cap cost into future years, when the cap will usually be higher, so the same fixed charge is a smaller share of it.

Guaranteed money is money the team must pay even if it releases the player. It comes in flavors: fully guaranteed at signing (owed no matter what), injury-only (owed if he is cut while hurt), and money that vests, becoming fully guaranteed on a set date, often a few days into the new league year, if he is still on the roster. Agents and reporters often quote the total of all of these, which is why “total guarantees” in a headline can be far larger than what the player is truly certain to receive.

Dead money is the bill for leaving early. If a team releases or trades a player, every year of signing-bonus proration not yet counted lands on the cap at once, along with any guaranteed salary still owed. That money pays for nobody on the field.

That gap is why every big contract is built around a large signing bonus: it front-loads the player’s money and back-loads the team’s cap cost. Typically the bonus plus the first year or two of salary are fully guaranteed at signing, here $75 million, and the later years are not, which is what makes them “team-friendly”: the team can walk away from them.

Restructures and void years: borrowing from the future

The middle panel is the most common move in the cap world, the restructure. A team needs cap space this year, so it asks a player it plans to keep to turn most of this year’s base salary into a signing bonus. The player loses nothing: he gets the same money sooner, it becomes guaranteed, and the extra dead money it creates makes him harder to cut, which is job security. The team spreads the new bonus over the remaining years and cuts this year’s cap hit sharply. The catch is that later cap hits rise by the same total, so a restructure is borrowing, not saving.

Void years stretch the loan. They are fake final years added to the contract that cancel automatically before anyone plays them. Their only purpose is to give the bonus more years to spread across, which lowers each year’s charge. When they cancel, whatever proration was assigned to them becomes dead money all at once. The league tolerates them because they move cap charges without erasing any: every bonus dollar still counts against some year’s cap. Tom Brady’s last Tampa Bay contracts were the famous example: after he retired in February 2023, the Buccaneers carried about $35 million of dead money for a quarterback who was no longer playing.3

Cutting a player, and why June 1 matters

The bottom panel is the release. After year 2, the player’s last three years carry $30 million of proration that hasn’t been counted. Cut him in March and all $30 million is dead money this year, so the release saves only $5 million of cap (his $35 million cap hit minus $30 million of dead money) even though it saves $25 million of cash. That is why you hear “they can’t afford to cut him”: sometimes cutting a player costs nearly as much cap space as keeping him.

The June 1 rule softens it. If a player is released after June 1, or released earlier with a post-June 1 designation (each team gets two a year), only the current year’s proration counts this year and the rest moves to next year. The team gets more room now and pays the remainder later. The catch is timing: a designated player’s full cap hit stays on the books until June 2, so the designation doesn’t help a team that needs room in March, when free agency opens.

Two more rules shape how much room a team really has. From the start of the league year until the season, only a team’s 51 largest contracts count in full (the top-51 rule), so cutting a player saves his cap hit minus the cost of the minimum-salary player who moves up into the top 51. And unused cap space can be carried over into the next league year, which is why some teams deliberately “bank” room.4

NoteFilm room: Denver Broncos, 2024

The move: Denver released quarterback Russell Wilson two years after trading for him and signing him to a five-year, $245 million extension. The release created $85 million of dead money, the largest amount in league history at the time.5

Why March: on the fifth day of the 2024 league year, another $37 million of Wilson’s future salary would have turned from guaranteed-for-injury into fully guaranteed. Cutting him on the first day of the league year kept that money off the bill. Why two years: the release carried a post-June 1 designation, so the charge was split across two league years, and Denver chose to take the larger part, a record $53 million, on the 2024 cap and $32 million on 2025.

What to notice: why a team would accept that. Wilson’s remaining guaranteed money had to be paid either way, so keeping him cost the same money plus a roster spot for a player the coaches had benched. Dead money is a sunk cost: it is spent whatever you do next, and good front offices decide on what each choice costs from here. Denver spent 2024 with a rookie quarterback, Bo Nix, on a rookie contract, and made the playoffs; in 2025 it went 14–3 and earned the AFC’s top seed while still carrying the second half of the Wilson charge. A painful cap year is survivable when the replacement is cheap.

Free agency: unrestricted and restricted

When a contract expires, what happens depends on the player’s accrued seasons, the seasons in which he spent at least six regular-season games on full pay status (on the active roster, inactive, or on injured reserve).

  • An unrestricted free agent (UFA) has four or more accrued seasons. He may sign with anyone, and his old team gets nothing back except possibly a compensatory pick a year later (explained below). This is the market most fans picture: the first days of the league year in March, when the best players sign big deals, usually agreed during a two-day negotiating window just before it that broadcasters call “legal tampering”.
  • A restricted free agent (RFA) has exactly three. His team can offer him a one-year tender at one of several set prices. He may then sign an offer sheet with another team, but his old team can match it; if it doesn’t, it receives a draft pick whose round depends on the tender. Offer sheets are rare, because teams don’t like giving up picks to pry a player loose. (With fewer than three seasons, a player can be kept for a minimum salary and can’t negotiate elsewhere at all.)

Why free agency exists: it is the players’ half of the 1993 bargain that also created the cap (see 11-02). What it costs teams: free agents are usually paid for what they did on their last team, at the peak of their market, and a bidding war means the team that “wins” is the one that valued him most, often too much. Analysts call that the winner’s curse, and it’s why patient teams treat free agency as a way to fill specific holes rather than to build a roster.

The franchise and transition tags

What if the player is a star and the team can’t agree on a long-term deal? It can tag him.

The franchise tag is a one-year contract that a team may impose on one expiring player a year. Its price is set by formula: roughly the average of the five largest cap numbers at his position, or 120 percent of his previous salary, whichever is higher. The usual non-exclusive tag lets other teams bid, but his team may match, and if it doesn’t it receives two first-round picks, a price that almost always ends the conversation; the pricier exclusive tag forbids talking to other teams at all.

The transition tag is the cheaper version, priced from the top ten salaries at the position. Other teams can sign the player to an offer sheet, and his team gets only the right to match, with no compensation if it lets him go.6

Why tags exist: to let a team keep a great player for one more season while the two sides keep negotiating; a tagged player and his team have until mid-July to agree on a long-term deal, or he plays the year on the tag. What a tag costs: a full year of top-of-market salary, fully guaranteed, with no cap-friendly bonus structure, and a player who usually feels he has been denied his long-term security. Tagging the same player again costs more each time. Washington tagged quarterback Kirk Cousins in 2016 and again in 2017, then let him walk; Minnesota signed him in 2018 to a three-year, $84 million contract that was fully guaranteed, the first multi-year fully guaranteed deal for a quarterback and a direct result of the leverage two tags had given him.7

Because the tag is priced by position, it is also where scheme meets money. In 2014 the Saints tagged Jimmy Graham as a tight end, at about $7 million; Graham argued that he lined up in the slot or out wide so often that he was really a receiver, whose tag was about $12.3 million. An arbitrator ruled that a tight end detached from the line in the slot is still doing a tight end’s job, and Graham lost.8 The label on a player’s role is worth millions, which is the idea Scheme and Fantasy picks up.

Compensatory picks

A team that loses more good free agents than it signs gets a consolation prize. The compensatory pick formula looks at every unrestricted free agent who left or arrived the previous year, ranks each by his new contract’s average salary, playing time and honors, and awards up to 32 extra picks league-wide, at the end of rounds 3 through 7, at most four per team. Since 2017 the picks can be traded.9 The idea is to repay a team for talent it developed and then lost on the open market.

Why it matters for strategy: the formula nets the two lists. Every qualifying free agent a team signs cancels out one it lost, roughly matched by contract size. Only players whose contracts ran out count; a player who was released doesn’t count on either side. So a team can choose to let its own free agents walk, sign only veterans other teams have cut, and collect the picks.10 The Baltimore Ravens are the model: they have received more compensatory picks than any other franchise since the system began in 1994, and their roster is built on letting expensive veterans walk at the right time.11

Trades and the trade deadline

A trade moves a contract, not just a player. The acquiring team takes over his remaining salary and cap hits; the trading team keeps any signing-bonus proration it has not yet counted, as dead money. When a broadcast says a team “ate” money to make a deal work, that is what it means: it took a dead-money charge so that the other team would take the player. Trades also cluster around the new league year in March, because a trade agreed after the season can’t be processed until then, and because that is when next year’s cap numbers start to count.

The trade deadline is the last day of the season on which trades are allowed: since 2024, the Tuesday after Week 9.12 After it, a contender can add only free agents, waiver claims and its own practice squad. So contenders use the deadline to rent a veteran for a playoff run, paying with future picks, and sellers turn a player who won’t be part of their next good team into a pick that might be.

Waivers

When a team releases a player with fewer than four accrued seasons (and, after the trade deadline, any player, so that a contender can’t sign a veteran whom a rival releases to get around the deadline), he goes on waivers: for about a day, every other team may claim him and take over his contract as it stands. If several teams claim him, the one with the worst record wins. A player nobody claims becomes a free agent and can choose his next team. Waivers matter most on cut-down day in late August, when hundreds of players are released at once and every front office combs the list for someone better than its own 53rd man.

NoteFilm room: Los Angeles Rams, 2021

The build: the Rams of 2021 are the boldest trade-built champion of the cap era. In March they made official the trade for quarterback Matthew Stafford, sending Detroit Jared Goff, a 2021 third-round pick and their first-round picks in 2022 and 2023. What to notice: the currency. The Rams paid in future draft picks, which a team with a short window values least, and spent them on premium positions (a quarterback, a cornerback, an edge rusher) rather than on depth. The bill came later: the Rams did not make a first-round pick in any draft from 2017 through 2023 (their first after Jared Goff in 2016 was Jared Verse, 19th overall in 2024), and went 5–12 in 2022 with an injured, expensive roster. They drafted their way back (Puka Nacua was a 2023 fifth-round pick) and were back in the playoffs in 2023. A trade-built roster wins a window; it doesn’t build a pipeline.

The draft: the cheapest good players in football

The rookie wage scale and the fifth-year option

Before 2011, top draft picks negotiated like free agents, and the price had run away: the 2010 first overall pick, quarterback Sam Bradford, signed a six-year deal worth up to $78 million with $50 million guaranteed before playing a down, more than many proven veteran quarterbacks earned. The 2011 collective bargaining agreement ended that with the rookie wage scale. Every draft slot now carries a set four-year contract value, falling steeply from the first pick to the last, with little left to negotiate. Drafted players cannot sign an extension until after their third regular season.14

First-round picks come with one extra year of team control. The fifth-year option lets the team add a fifth season at a set price, decided after the player’s third season (by early May). Since the 2020 agreement the option is fully guaranteed once exercised, and its price depends on how much the player has played and whether he made Pro Bowls, so teams now decline it for players they aren’t sure of.

Together these rules create the rookie-contract window: the years in which a good young player is paid far less than a veteran of his quality. For a star it usually lasts three seasons, not four or five, because the first moment an extension is allowed (after year 3) is also the moment his team rushes to lock him up before his price climbs further. For most positions the window is a nice bonus. For a quarterback it is the most valuable asset in the sport, as Figure 71.3 showed, and teams with a young starter build their entire cap plan around it.

Draft value charts: what is a pick worth?

To trade picks, teams need a common price list. The original draft value chart was built for the Dallas Cowboys in the early 1990s, under head coach Jimmy Johnson, from past trades and judgment: at Johnson’s request, Cowboys vice president Mike McCoy drew it up before the club’s run of draft-day trades in 1991.15 The Jimmy Johnson chart gives the first pick 3,000 points, the 16th 1,000, the 32nd 590, the 64th 270 and the 100th 100. Teams still use it or close cousins, and a trade that “balances on the chart” is still how deals get done. (Separately, each team ranks the prospects themselves on its draft board, its own ordered list of players; the chart prices the slots, the board decides whom to take in them.)

But the chart describes what teams pay for picks, not what the picks produce. In 2005 two behavioral economists, Cade Massey and Richard Thaler (Thaler later won a Nobel prize for work on how people make predictable mistakes), asked whether the prices were right. They measured each pick’s surplus value: what a player produces minus what he is paid. Their answer, published as “The Loser’s Curse,” was that teams badly overvalue the top of the draft. A top pick is more likely to become a star, but he is also paid far more, and teams are overconfident in their ability to tell the stars apart. By surplus value, they found, picks gained value through the first round, because a pick’s cost fell faster down the round than the production teams could expect from it, and the best value in the draft sat in the second round.16 The curse of the title: the worst team earns the first pick, and the first pick is overpriced.

Their data came from before the rookie wage scale, when top picks were far more expensive. We can redo the idea for the current era with contract data. For every player drafted from 2011 to 2020, take what the market paid him on his next contract (as a share of the cap) as the measure of what he turned out to be worth, and take his rookie salary as what he cost. Surplus is the difference. Why the next contract? Because when a rookie deal ends, 32 front offices that watched him for four years bid on him, and the price they settle on is the best available summary of how good he was; a player nobody would pay counts as zero. The two halves come from different years on purpose: value from the 2011–2020 classes, because later classes haven’t all reached a second contract yet, and cost from 2021–2025 rookie deals, because those are today’s prices. The two curves agree on the first pick and disagree about almost everything after it. Ignore the blue dip at picks 2 and 3: it is 20 players, 6 of them quarterbacks, and 7 of them were paid less on their next contract than on their rookie deal. With ten players a pick, a couple of busts move the line. The real pattern is that the surplus curve is nearly flat from pick 2 to the mid-teens, while the Johnson chart falls by half over the same stretch. Measured against an average top-ten pick, the Johnson chart prices the 32nd pick at 32 percent and the 64th at 15; by surplus they keep about 61 and 43 percent. The bottom panel shows why: the rookie scale makes the first pick cost about 4.4% of the cap a year, about 7 times what a third-round pick costs, but what the players turn out to be worth falls far more gently. The modern data softens Massey and Thaler’s “curse”: the wage scale made the top picks cheaper, so picks no longer gain value as you move down the first round and the very top is no longer a bad deal. But their main finding survives: the Johnson chart drops far too fast after the first few picks, so a team trading down for more picks is usually buying more value than the chart says, and a team trading up is usually paying more.

Two caveats keep this honest. “Next contract” is a rough measure of value: it misses players who were excellent and then hurt, and it credits players paid on reputation. And the top of the curve rests on very few players, mostly quarterbacks: 7 of the ten first overall picks from 2011 to 2020.

So when does the disagreement not matter? In two places. At the very top, both price lists agree that No. 1 is special. And when the player is a quarterback the team is genuinely sure of, because a franchise quarterback on a rookie deal is worth more than either chart’s price, so a team convinced it has found one can rationally pay almost anything; that is why the biggest trade-ups are for quarterbacks. Everywhere else, and above all in trades that bundle several picks or future first-rounders, the choice of chart decides who won. The catch in the quarterback exception is that the conviction is often wrong, as the next film room shows.

A few words on those cards are terms of art. A lineman’s anchor is his ability to stop a bull rush, sinking his weight and holding his ground instead of being driven back into the quarterback (Pass Protection). An edge rusher’s get-off is his first-step explosion at the snap, and bend is his ability to dip his shoulder and turn the corner around a tackle at full speed without slowing down (The Pass Rush). Processing, for a quarterback, is how quickly he reads the coverage and knows where to throw. Motor is effort that doesn’t fade late in games; hand timing is when a tackle shoots his punch, too early and the rusher swats it away, too late and he is already beaten; catch radius is how far from his body a receiver can still catch the ball. Hand size is the quarterback’s only real stand-in number because scouts believe a bigger hand holds a cold, wet ball better and fumbles less. Notice that the first trait on almost every card is one no stopwatch measures.

The combine and pro days

Film is graded through the fall. In February and March come the measurements. The NFL Scouting Combine brings more than 300 invited prospects (319 in 2026) to Indianapolis, where it has been held since 1987, for a week of medical exams, interviews with teams, body measurements (height, weight, arm length, hand size) and timed drills.18 Each drill stands in for a football trait:

  • 40-yard dash, with a 10-yard split: long speed for receivers, backs and corners; the split, the first ten yards, is the closest number to a lineman’s or edge rusher’s burst off the snap.
  • Vertical and broad jump: lower-body explosiveness.
  • Bench press (reps of 225 pounds): upper-body strength endurance, mostly for linemen.
  • Three-cone drill and 20-yard short shuttle: change of direction, the ability to stop, bend and restart, which matters for edge rushers turning the corner and corners mirroring routes.

A pro day is the campus version, held in March and April for a school’s prospects with NFL scouts and coaches watching. Players who skipped drills at the combine run them here, usually on their own familiar turf and often with friendlier timing, so experienced evaluators treat pro-day numbers as slightly optimistic.

RAS: putting the numbers on one scale

A 4.6 forty is slow for a cornerback and astonishing for a 300-pound tackle, so raw numbers can’t be compared across positions. RAS, the Relative Athletic Score created by the analyst Kent Lee Platte, solves that by converting each measurement into a percentile among every player measured at the same position since 1987, then combining them into one score from 0 to 10. A 5 is average for the position; 8 is very good; 9 and above is elite.19

But those bars are 62 and 26 players, so each is uncertain by about 19 percentage points either way, and the gap could be much smaller than it looks. A fair reading: athleticism still helps a little after teams have priced it in, and nobody should bet heavily on the size of that help. Athletic scores tell you who can play the position at NFL speed; they can’t tell you who processes a coverage, plays with his hands, or keeps improving. So treat RAS as a filter, not a ranking: a very low score at a premium position is a real warning, and a very high score on a player with thin film is a bet on coaching. Some players beat their numbers for a career. Others, the “workout warriors,” turn a great combine into a high pick and never play like it.

Scheme fit: the same player, different value

Here is where X’s and O’s come back. Scheme fit is how well a player’s body and skills match what a particular scheme asks of his position. Because schemes ask different things, the same prospect can be a first-round talent on one board and a fourth-rounder on another, and a smart team shops where its scheme makes it the only serious buyer.

def go_to(p, key, end, kind="block", via=(), speed=None, delay=0.0):
    """Move `key` through absolute (d, w) points `via` to `end`, as a block, path or run."""

The edge has the same split. A 4-3 defensive end plays with his hand in the dirt and must hold up against tackles in the run game, so teams like him heavier, around 255–280 pounds; a 3-4 outside linebacker stands up, rushes from wider and sometimes drops into coverage, so he can be lighter and quicker, around 240–260. But defenses played their base personnel on only about 30 percent of snaps in 2025, and spent the rest with five or more defensive backs,21 so whatever the base front is called, the live split today is less “4-3 end or 3-4 linebacker” than “run-down edge or pass-rush specialist”.

Cornerbacks: press or off

Cornerbacks split by technique, not by man versus zone. A team whose corners play press, jamming the receiver at the line, wants long, physical corners with the hips to turn and the long speed to run with a go route, whether they then play man or bail into a zone. Pete Carroll’s Seattle teams of the 2010s, the league’s signature press Cover 3 defense (Coverage Fundamentals), drafted outside corners with long arms and nearly all of them six feet or taller.22 A team whose corners play off coverage, with a cushion and their eyes in the backfield, can live with shorter corners who read the play and break on the ball quickly. The clearest case is the Cover 2 squat corner, who sits about five to seven yards off, keeps the flat and hands the deep ball to a safety: Ronde Barber, listed at 5 feet 10, played that role in Tampa Bay’s Tampa 2 on his way to the Hall of Fame.23 Figure 71.12 shows both jobs.

Game-day actives. A team may dress 48 of its 53, provided at least eight are offensive linemen (otherwise 47); the rest are declared inactive about 90 minutes before kickoff. Who sits is a weekly game-plan decision. A team facing a run-heavy opponent might dress a fifth defensive tackle instead of a sixth receiver, and the inactive list on Sunday morning is one of the best free clues to the plan.

The practice squad. Each team keeps 16 players on its practice squad (plus one exemption for an international player). They practice with the team but are not on the 53, and any other team may sign one to its 53 at any time. A practice squad elevation moves one of them up for a single game: a team may elevate two a game, each player at most three times a season, and he reverts to the practice squad afterward without going through waivers. That is how a team covers a Saturday injury without cutting anyone, and it’s why a “practice squad” kicker can make a game-winning field goal.26

Injured reserve (IR) takes an injured player off the 53 so his spot can be filled. During the season a player placed on IR must sit out at least four games, and a team can bring back a limited number of players a year (eight); everyone else stays out for the season. The rule creates a real decision: put a player with a six-week injury on IR, freeing his spot for a healthy body, or keep him on the 53 and play a man short for a month and a half.

The emergency third quarterback. Since 2023 a team may dress a third quarterback from its 53 without counting him among the 48, and he may enter only if both of the other quarterbacks are injured or ejected. The rule was a direct answer to the 2022 NFC Championship Game, in which San Francisco lost its starter to an elbow injury and then its backup to a concussion; with a running back as the only other option, the injured starter went back in, barely able to throw.27

Watch for it on Sunday

TipWatch for it: the cap check

Pick one team before its game (Over the Cap and Spotrac have free team pages) and do a five-minute cap check. On an Over the Cap team page, the cap hit is the Cap Number column, and a Dead Money & Cap Savings section shows, for each player, what cutting him would leave as dead money and save, with separate “Cut (pre-June 1)” and “Cut (post-June 1)” views. Use the pre-June 1 view for March decisions.

Before kickoff

  • Find the five biggest cap hits. Are they at premium positions (QB, edge, receiver, interior pass rusher, tackle, corner)? A top-five hit at running back, linebacker or safety is a team paying for a player, not a position.
  • Find the dead money. A large number means a recent mistake or a recent release; it tells you which era of the roster the team is paying for.
  • Is the quarterback on a rookie contract? If so, look for the team spending the savings: expensive veterans elsewhere, a deadline trade. If not, look for bargains and late-round starters around him.
  • Check the inactives (posted 90 minutes before kickoff) and any practice-squad elevations announced the day before. They tell you the game plan’s priorities and who is hurt.

During the game

  • When a veteran with a big cap hit plays poorly, ask the cap question: what would releasing him save, and how much dead money would it leave? A small dead-money figure next to a big cap hit is a player likely to be released or asked to take a pay cut in March.
  • When a young player on a rookie deal plays well at a premium position, you are watching his team’s cap plan for the next three years.

The prediction: before the season ends, name the one contract the team will restructure (a star it plans to keep, with a large base salary next year) and the one it will cut (an older player at a lower-value position whose cap hit far exceeds his dead money). Check in March.

The team must clear $14 million before free agency opens in March. Which moves clear it most cheaply? On the radio, one caller says to cut the quarterback, the single biggest saving on the board; another says to cut the 32-year-old left tackle. Why are both wrong?

TipAnswer

Release the running back and the linebacker ($20 million saved); don’t touch the quarterback or the left tackle.

Start with the cuts that are cheap and sensible. The running back saves $12 million against $2 million of dead money, at the position the market values least, at an age when backs decline fast. That isn’t quite enough, so add the linebacker ($8 million saved, $1 million dead), another low-value position and another player past 30: $20 million cleared. (The top-51 rule trims that a little, since two minimum-salary players move up into the 51 that count, but it still clears 14.) Both are pre-June 1 releases on purpose: a post-June 1 designation would leave their cap hits on the books until June 2, after free agency has opened.

The other lever is a restructure. Turning $30 million of the quarterback’s $40 million base salary into a bonus spread over his three remaining seasons counts $10 million a year, so next year’s hit falls by $20 million. The cost is the future: his next two cap hits each rise by $10 million, and his dead money grows by $20 million on top of the $36 million already there, which makes him almost impossible to cut. Fine for a quarterback the team is sure of; a trap for one it isn’t. Most teams would make the two cheap cuts and save the restructure for when they need room to add someone.

Now the callers. Cutting the quarterback saves $16 million but leaves $36 million of dead money and no starter, and replacing a starting quarterback costs far more than $16 million. The left tackle is the subtler trap: $15 million saved, but tackle is a premium position, replacements are expensive, and the quarterback needs protecting. If he is still good, the hole costs more to fill than the cut saves. The edge rusher and cornerback are young premium players with heavy dead money, extension candidates rather than cuts; the receiver, at 30, is more likely to be asked to take a pay cut.

TipAnswer

It is outside zone, and the team takes Prospect B. Every lineman’s first step is sideways and to the right, with the back aiming wide and cutting up: that is outside zone. The right guard’s job is to reach a 3-technique who lines up outside him while the center steps with him on a combination block and then climbs to a linebacker. That is a race, and B’s quickness (a 4.45 short shuttle is excellent for a guard) and film (“quick to reach and climb”) are exactly the traits it rewards.

The cost is in pass protection. B gives ground to big bull rushers, and the team will have to cover for it: help from the center on obvious passing downs, a quicker passing game, and play-action and boots that move the launch point away from the defensive tackle. A wide-zone team can live with that because its run game and its play-action both ask him to move, not to stand still and absorb a 330-pound man head-on.

Prospect A is the better gap lineman: heavy, long, with a stout anchor, and able to move a defender off his spot on a down block, the power-team skill. On a gap team’s board he may be the higher-rated player, and on this team’s board he is a worse fit who would spend his first year trying to win races he isn’t built for. That’s scheme fit: the same pick, two right answers, depending on what the team runs. It also explains why a wide-zone team can often draft B a round later than his talent suggests. Fewer teams are shopping for him.

Takeaways

  • Positional value is real, but price is not value. The market pays quarterbacks most by far, then edge rushers and receivers, then interior pass rushers, corners and tackles. Yet a team’s share of spending on any position barely predicts winning (r near zero, 2014–2025), while quarterback play predicts it strongly (r about 0.7). Value comes from getting play above your price.
  • A contract is a cap schedule, not a number. Signing bonuses are paid up front but prorated over up to five years; restructures and void years borrow cap space from the future; releasing a player accelerates his remaining proration as dead money, which is why cutting a star can save almost nothing.
  • Every acquisition route has a currency. Free agency costs cap space at peak prices; tags buy one year at a top-of-market price; trades cost picks and move contracts; compensatory picks reward letting players go; waivers and the practice squad are how teams find the 53rd man.
  • The draft is the cheapest source of good players, and the Johnson chart overprices the top. Surplus value (production minus rookie cost) falls far more gently than the chart, so trading down usually buys value and trading up usually pays a premium, except, sometimes, for a franchise quarterback.
  • The rookie-contract window is the biggest edge in roster building. A star quarterback on a rookie deal, like Purdy in 2022–2024, frees a fifth or more of the cap to spend on everyone else.
  • Scheme decides who is worth drafting. Wide-zone and gap teams want different linemen, two-gap and one-gap fronts want different tackles, press and off teams want different corners. RAS and the combine filter for athleticism; film and fit decide the pick.
