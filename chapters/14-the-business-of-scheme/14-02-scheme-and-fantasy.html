Scheme and Fantasy: Reading Usage, Not Box Scores

Fantasy points are the last link in a chain the play-caller forges: who is on the field, who runs a route, who the ball is aimed at, and how often the team throws at all

def neutral(df):
    """The neutral-situation filter for PROE: downs 1-3, win probability 20-80%, not the last 2 minutes of a half."""
    return df[(df.down <= 3) & df.wp.between(0.2, 0.8) & (df.half_seconds_remaining > 120) & df.xpass.notna()]


def team_table(df):
    g = df.groupby(["season", "posteam"])
    games = g.game_id.nunique()
    tg = df[(df.play_type == "pass") & df.receiver_player_id.notna()].copy()
    tg["pos"] = tg.receiver_player_id.map(POS)
    tgg = tg.groupby(["season", "posteam"])
    return pd.DataFrame({
        "games": games,
        "plays_pg": g.size() / games,
        "proe": neutral(df).groupby(["season", "posteam"]).pass_oe.mean(),
        "proe_se": neutral(df).groupby(["season", "posteam"]).pass_oe.std()
                   / np.sqrt(neutral(df).groupby(["season", "posteam"]).size()),
        "n_plays": g.size(),
        "targets_pg": tgg.size() / games,
        "motion": g.is_motion.mean() * 100,
        "play_action": df[df.play_type == "pass"].groupby(["season", "posteam"]).is_play_action.mean() * 100,
        "under_center": g.qb_location.apply(lambda s: (s.dropna() == "U").mean()) * 100,
        "rb_tgt": tgg.pos.apply(lambda s: (s == "RB").mean()) * 100,
        "te_tgt": tgg.pos.apply(lambda s: (s == "TE").mean()) * 100,
        "n_targets": tgg.size(),
        "n_pass": df[df.play_type == "pass"].groupby(["season", "posteam"]).size(),
    })


TEAMS = team_table(PLAYS)


def receiver_usage(season):
    """Per player and team: dropbacks on the field (participation data), targets, air yards, yards, and the shares.
    Route participation is ESTIMATED as the share of the team's dropbacks the player was on the field for:
    close to true routes for wide receivers, an overstatement for tight ends and backs who sometimes block."""
    part = NFL.load_participation([season]).to_pandas()
    part = part.rename(columns={"nflverse_game_id": "game_id"})[["game_id", "play_id", "offense_players"]]
    s = PLAYS[PLAYS.season == season]
    db = s[s.qb_dropback == 1].merge(part, on=["game_id", "play_id"], how="inner")
    db = db[db.offense_players.notna()]
    on = db.assign(pid=db.offense_players.str.split(";")).explode("pid")[["pid", "posteam", "game_id", "play_id"]]
    games = on[["pid", "posteam", "game_id"]].drop_duplicates()
    team_db = db.groupby(["posteam", "game_id"]).size().rename("team_db").reset_index()
    tg = s[(s.play_type == "pass") & s.receiver_player_id.notna()]
    team_tg = (tg.groupby(["posteam", "game_id"]).agg(team_tgt=("play_id", "size"), team_ay=("air_yards", "sum"))
               .reset_index())
    per_game = games.merge(team_db, on=["posteam", "game_id"]).merge(team_tg, on=["posteam", "game_id"], how="left")
    u = per_game.groupby(["pid", "posteam"]).agg(games=("game_id", "nunique"), team_db=("team_db", "sum"),
                                                 team_tgt=("team_tgt", "sum"), team_ay=("team_ay", "sum"))
    u["db_on"] = on.groupby(["pid", "posteam"]).size()
    me = tg.groupby(["receiver_player_id", "posteam"]).agg(tgt=("play_id", "size"), ay=("air_yards", "sum"),
                                                           yds=("receiving_yards", "sum"), rec=("complete_pass", "sum"),
                                                           td=("pass_touchdown", "sum"))
    me.index.names = ["pid", "posteam"]
    u = u.join(me, how="left").fillna({"tgt": 0, "ay": 0, "yds": 0, "rec": 0, "td": 0}).reset_index()
    u["pos"], u["name"] = u.pid.map(POS), u.pid.map(NAME)
    u["route_part"] = u.db_on / u.team_db
    u["tgt_share"] = u.tgt / u.team_tgt
    u["ay_share"] = u.ay / u.team_ay
    u["wopr"] = 1.5 * u.tgt_share + 0.7 * u.ay_share
    u["yprr"] = u.yds / u.db_on
    return u

Week 1, Sunday night. You check your fantasy team. The tight end you drafted in the sixth round was on the field for 52 of his team’s 61 snaps and caught one pass for 7 yards. Your league-mate’s backup running back played 14 snaps and scored twice, both times from the 1-yard line. On Monday the talk is all touchdowns: the backup is a waiver-wire must, your tight end a bust. A coach looking at the same game would ask different questions. On how many of those 52 snaps did your tight end run a route, and on how many was he blocking a defensive end? Why was the backup the one in the game at the 1-yard line, and is that a role the play-caller will keep giving him? The box score records what happened. Usage (who is on the field, who goes out for a pass, who the ball is aimed at, how often the team throws at all) records what the scheme intended to happen, and intentions repeat from week to week far more reliably than outcomes. This chapter teaches you to read usage, to trace it back to the scheme and the play-caller who produce it, and to predict how it moves when the play-caller changes.

You’ll be able to…

  • Turn a scheme into receiver usage and read the numbers that measure it: route participation, target share, air-yards share, WOPR and yards per route run, and say which of them carry over from season to season.
  • Read a running back’s role (snap share, share of the carries, routes run, red-zone share) and a tight end’s role in 12 personnel (route runner or extra blocker), and spot the goal-line role that makes or breaks a back’s touchdown total.
  • Forecast a player’s volume from his team’s tendencies: pass rate over expected, pace and game script.
  • Explain the common scoring formats (standard, half-PPR, PPR), what each one rewards, and why average draft position works like a market price.
  • Predict how a coordinator change shifts fantasy value, using the 2026 changes in Detroit, Los Angeles (Chargers) and Kansas City as test cases.

NoteHow a fantasy league works (if you’ve never played)

A typical league has 10 or 12 people, each acting as the general manager of an imaginary team. Before the season they hold a draft: they take turns picking real NFL players, round after round (ESPN’s default is 16 rounds in a 10-team league), usually in a “snake” order that reverses every round, so the person who picks first in round 1 picks last in round 2. Each week every manager sets a starting lineup, and the real players’ statistics turn into fantasy points. ESPN’s default lineup is one quarterback, two running backs, two wide receivers, one tight end, one flex (a running back, receiver or tight end, the manager’s choice), a kicker and a team defense, with seven players on the bench.1 Most points wins the week.

During the season, players nobody drafted sit in a pool called the waiver wire (the name and the worst-team-picks-first priority are borrowed from the NFL’s own waivers); a “waiver-wire must” is a pickup worth making now. Fantasy shorthand ranks players by position: “RB1” is the best running back, “a WR2” a receiver good enough to be a team’s second starting receiver. A player is rosterable if he’s worth a bench spot and a weekly starter if he belongs in the lineup every week. Two numbers recur in this chapter: the scoring format (how many points a catch is worth) and the draft price, both explained in Scoring formats and the market.

Reading the diagrams: offense is blue circles (X and Z wide receivers; Y and U tight ends, ringed in gold; F a fullback; Q quarterback; R running back); defense is outlined squares. Solid arrows are routes and runs, short arrows ending in a bar are blocks, dashed lines defenders’ movements. The dark blue line is the line of scrimmage. The charts come from nflverse data; the notes under each one say which seasons and filters it uses.

Outputs and inputs: why the box score misleads

A fantasy point is the end of a chain. For a wide receiver, the chain runs like this: his team runs a certain number of plays (pace); some fraction of them are passes (the play-caller’s tendency, adjusted by the score); on some of those passes he is on the field and running a route rather than blocking or standing on the sideline (route participation); on some of those routes the ball is thrown to him (target share), some of them deep (air-yards share); and on some of those targets he catches the ball, gains yards and, now and then, scores. Every link before the catch is mostly usage, and usage is set by people: the coordinator who designs the plays, the play-caller who chooses them on game day, and the quarterback who decides where the ball goes. The links after it are mostly efficiency, which belongs to the player, plus a large dose of luck.

The reason to care about the difference is that the two halves behave very differently over time. The opportunity numbers (target share, air-yards share, targets per game, WOPR, route participation) all correlate at about 0.64 to 0.70 from one season to the next. Be careful about what the chart does not say. Receiving yards per game (0.62) is almost as sticky, but it isn’t a pure efficiency number: it is volume times efficiency, so it inherits most of its stability from the volume. Catch rate (0.59) is sticky because it is partly a role too: a slot receiver who runs short routes catches a high share of his targets every year, and a deep threat a low one. The numbers that really wobble are the per-chance ones. Yards per target sits near 0.45, and touchdowns per game, the statistic that swings a fantasy week more than any other, correlate at only 0.25.

In plain terms, a receiver’s share of his team’s targets is mostly a fact about his role, and his role is decided before the season by the scheme, the depth chart and the quarterback’s trust. Whether a given target turns into a 40-yard catch or a touchdown depends on the coverage, the throw, the defender’s hand and a dozen things that don’t repeat. One honest caveat: the chart includes only receivers who kept a real role in both seasons, the survivors. A player who lost his job isn’t in it, so treat these correlations as “if the role holds”, which is exactly the question a scheme change raises.2

ImportantWhy it exists

Usage stats exist because fantasy players and analysts kept being fooled by box scores. A season of touchdowns makes a player look like a star; a season of near-misses makes an equally used player look ordinary. The next season, the touchdowns are dealt out again more or less at random, while the opportunity that produced them carries over. Reading usage is the habit of pricing a player on the part of his production that will repeat. The trade-off: usage tells you about the chances, not the talent. A player who gets the ball constantly and does nothing with it will eventually lose the role, so the efficiency numbers still matter. They are just slower to trust.

NoteMadden vs. real life

In Madden, a player’s ratings drive what he does on the field: a 95-overall receiver gets open and makes plays. In a real offense, the ball goes where the play was designed to send it, read by the quarterback in the order the play-caller drew it. A great receiver in a run-first offense that rotates its receivers can finish behind an average one in a pass-first offense that never takes him off the field. The talent is the same; the opportunity is not, and opportunity is drawn on the play-caller’s call sheet, not in the player’s ratings.

Receivers: from snaps to routes to targets

Being on the field is not the same as running a route

Start with the first filter. A player can’t catch a pass on a play he isn’t in for, so the first number to check is snap share: the share of the team’s offensive plays on which he is on the field, published by the league in its official snap counts (free in nflverse as load_snap_counts(), from Pro Football Reference). A team’s top two wide receivers usually play 85-95% of the snaps; a third receiver might play 60-80%, and his number depends on how often the team uses three-receiver sets.

Snap share has a blind spot, and the tight end shows it best. The two-jobs figure draws the same 12 personnel formation twice.

In both pictures the two tight ends play the snap, so their snap counts are identical. In one of them Y is a blocker who never had a chance to catch the ball; in the other he is a receiver running up the seam. The left-hand call is a max-protect shot: the play-caller kept seven men in to buy time for two deep routes, and the price is the tight end’s route. Some calls take a tight end out of the pattern by design, and a play-caller who loves them is a bad landlord for a fantasy tight end. Over a season, a tight end who stays in to block on a third of his team’s dropbacks has a third fewer chances to be targeted than one who goes out every time. The same is true of a running back who stays in to protect the quarterback, a job called pass protection, or who chips a pass rusher before releasing late.

That is what route participation measures: the share of the team’s dropbacks (pass plays, including sacks and scrambles) on which a player runs a route. For a team’s top wide receivers it is usually above 85%, because a wide receiver almost never stays in to block on a pass play. (He still loses a few: on a screen to someone else he blocks for the ball carrier, and on some RPOs he blocks for the run half of the play while the quarterback throws to someone else. That, plus rest snaps, is why even a number-one receiver sits around 90-95%, not 100%.) For tight ends and backs it is where the real information is: two tight ends with the same snap share can have route participation of 50% and 85%, and only the second is a fantasy tight end.

NoteGo deeper: where route counts come from

Routes are not in the official play-by-play. Pro Football Focus (PFF) and FTN count them by watching film of every play, and sell the results. The free nflverse participation data tells you who was on the field for each play, but not who ran a route. This chapter therefore estimates route participation as the share of a team’s dropbacks on which a player was on the field. For wide receivers the estimate is close to the true figure; for tight ends and backs it overstates routes by however often they stayed in to block. When this chapter says “route participation (est.)”, that is the measure. Treat it as an upper bound for anyone who blocks.

Target share: how much of the passing game is aimed at him

A target is a pass thrown to an intended receiver, caught or not. Target share is a player’s targets divided by his team’s targets in the games he played. It is the single most useful receiving number, for two reasons. First, it is a share, so it strips out how much the team throws: 25% of a pass-heavy team’s targets and 25% of a run-heavy team’s targets describe the same role in two different amounts of work. Second, it is a role, and roles are designed. Play-callers feature a receiver in three main ways. They move his alignment (slot, boundary, motion) to get him the best leverage and the matchup they want. They build pre-snap “alerts” to him, so the quarterback throws his way whenever the defense leaves him one-on-one. And they manufacture touches: screens, quick throws and jet or orbit pitches that put the ball in his hands without a read at all. (Simply “making him the first read” is a smaller lever than it sounds, because progressions depend on the coverage: the first read against one coverage is often not the first read against another.) Mike McDaniel’s Miami offenses are the textbook case: built around Tyreek Hill’s and Jaylen Waddle’s speed with motion and quick touches, they aimed about 30% of their targets at Hill and about another 20% at Waddle in both 2022 and 2023.3

That gives target share two layers, and the difference matters later in this chapter. A genuine number-one receiver’s share is mostly his: talent and the quarterback’s trust earn it, and it tends to follow him from season to season (the 0.64 in the chart above). But how a team’s targets split by position, between wide receivers, tight ends and backs, is largely the play-caller’s fingerprint, and it moves when the play-caller does. A new coordinator who feeds his tight end has to take those targets from somebody, usually the second and third receivers first.

In 2025 a typical team’s leading receiver drew about 23% of its targets in the games he played; the true “alpha” receivers (fantasy slang for a team’s dominant number-one target) were near or above 30%. Seattle’s Jaxon Smith-Njigba, the 2025 Offensive Player of the Year, drew 36%, the highest share of any player with a full season, and Detroit’s Amon-Ra St. Brown and Cincinnati’s Ja’Marr Chase each drew about 31-32%. A player in the 15-20% range is a secondary option; his weekly output depends on how the defense plays the primary one.

Air-yards share and WOPR: not all targets are equal

A 2-yard pass in the flat and a 30-yard throw down the sideline each count as one target, but they are not worth the same. The deep one, caught, is worth far more yards and points. Air-yards share captures that: add up the air yards of every pass thrown to the player (how far past the line of scrimmage each traveled in the air) and divide by the team’s total. A slot receiver who catches short passes can have a big target share and a modest air-yards share; a deep threat can have the reverse.

Josh Hermsmeyer, an analyst who built the air-yards site airyards.com and later wrote for FiveThirtyEight, combined the two into one number: WOPR, the weighted opportunity rating.4

\[\text{WOPR} = 1.5 \times \text{target share} + 0.7 \times \text{air-yards share}\]

Target share gets the bigger weight, and the logic is that a target is a chance at a reception, yards and a touchdown all at once, while air yards add the size of the chance. As a scale, a WOPR of about 0.7 or more marks a team’s clear number-one target; 0.4 to 0.5 is a typical number two. In 2025 Smith-Njigba’s was about 0.89 (a 36% target share and half of Seattle’s air yards), the highest in the league. Carolina’s Tetairoa McMillan, the Offensive Rookie of the Year, drew a quarter of the Panthers’ targets and about 43% of their air yards for a WOPR near 0.68, among the league’s top ten: a rookie who was the deep, contested-catch target from his first game.

What does WOPR add, if it is no stickier than target share (0.64 and 0.64 in the chart above)? Less than its fame suggests, for predicting points. In this book’s 78-receiver sample, a player’s 2024 WOPR and his 2024 target share predict his 2025 fantasy points per game (in PPR scoring, explained below) equally well (correlations of about 0.58 and 0.57). WOPR’s real use is description: two receivers with the same 22% target share are different players if one has 35% of the air yards and the other 12%, and WOPR folds that into the same number. Use it to see the shape of a role, not as a better crystal ball.5

Yards per route run: efficiency with the right denominator

Opportunity is half the story; the other half is what a player does with it. The classic efficiency numbers (catch rate, yards per catch, yards per target) all divide by something the player earned only after he got open. Yards per route run (YPRR) divides receiving yards by the number of routes the player ran, every chance he had to get open, whether or not the ball came his way. It rewards both halves: a receiver who earns targets and gains yards on them scores well, and one who runs a hundred routes for nothing scores badly. Around 1.5 is typical for a regular NFL wide receiver; 2.0 is very good; 2.5 or more is elite.

The two numbers together, how often a receiver is out there and how productive he is when he is, form a simple map.

Running backs: snaps, carries, routes and the goal line

A running back’s usage has more parts than a receiver’s, because he does more jobs. Read it in four pieces.

  1. Snap share. Very few backs play every down now. A true “bell cow” (one back who plays nearly every down and gets nearly every carry) plays 75-85% of the snaps; most backfields split, with an early-down back and a passing-down back sharing 50-60% each.
  2. Share of the carries. How many of the team’s runs he gets. This depends on the team’s run rate as well as his role, so check both.
  3. Routes and targets. A back who runs routes on third down and in the two-minute drill gets targets that a pure runner never sees, and in PPR scoring (below) each catch is worth a point. Backs run routes on far fewer dropbacks than receivers, because they often stay in to protect, and protection is the gate to this role: the most common reason a talented receiving back (often a rookie) doesn’t get third-down snaps is that the staff doesn’t trust him to pick up a blitzer (Pass Protection). Not all back targets are equal, either. Designed targets (screens, and option or angle routes built to get the back the ball, the way the Shanahan tree used Christian McCaffrey) are worth more than check-downs thrown only when everything else is covered, and they travel with the play-caller who draws them.
  4. Red-zone share. His share of the team’s carries and targets inside the opponent’s 20-yard line (the red zone), and above all inside the 5. Most touchdowns are scored from close range, so the goal-line role decides a back’s touchdown total more than his talent does.

The last one is where scheme hides the most fantasy value, and two recent backfields show it from both directions.

The same goal-line play has a winner, and he is the quarterback. The tight end in 12 personnel: blocker or receiver?

No position’s fantasy value depends more on scheme than the tight end’s, because no position’s job varies more. In 12 personnel (one back, two tight ends, two receivers) the two tight ends usually have different jobs. One is the in-line tight end, attached to the end of the line, whose first job is blocking; the other is often the move tight end, who lines up in the slot, as a wing or in the backfield and is a receiver first. Offenses used 12 personnel on 24.4% of plays in 2025, a decade high in this book’s count, and fewer than three wide receivers on 41.7% of plays by Next Gen Stats’ count.10 More tight end snaps do not mean more tight end routes: the second tight end in a heavy set is often there to block, which is exactly why the offense put him there instead of a third receiver.

So for a tight end, read three numbers in order.

  • Route participation, not snap share. A tight end who runs a route on 80% or more of dropbacks is used as a receiver; one at 50-60% is a blocker who sometimes goes out. Fantasy points live almost entirely in the first group. In 2025, by this chapter’s on-field estimate, Arizona’s Trey McBride was out there on about 94% of the Cardinals’ dropbacks and Detroit’s Sam LaPorta on about 92% (true route rates are a little lower, since both sometimes block); the Rams, who rotated tight ends through their three-tight-end package, had Tyler Higbee and Colby Parkinson near 56-59%, before subtracting the snaps they spent blocking.11
  • Target share among route runners. Some offenses aim the passing game at the tight end. In 2025 Arizona threw 35% of its targets to tight ends, the most in the league, and Trey McBride alone drew 27% of the Cardinals’ targets, more than any wide receiver on the team.
  • Red-zone usage. Tight ends are big targets in tight spaces, and some offenses build their goal-line pass plays around them. A tight end with few yards but a steady red-zone role is a touchdown bet, with the noise that implies.

Back to the tight end from the opening, on the field for 52 of 61 snaps with one catch. The first question is not whether he is good but how many of those 52 snaps were routes. If he blocked on half of them, the one catch is about what that role produces, and no amount of patience will fix it. If he ran a route on 40-plus and saw only two or three targets, the role is right and the targets are noise worth waiting out.

The Arizona number matters for 2026, because the coordinator who built that offense now calls plays in Detroit. We’ll come back to him.

The left panel is the reassuring one: a receiver’s slice doesn’t depend on whether his team likes to throw. Seattle (PROE about −6) and the Rams (about +5) both funneled their passing games through one receiver, while the Jets, the most run-heavy team in neutral situations, spread theirs thin. The right panel is the one that moves fantasy values. Arizona, the league’s most pass-happy team at about +6, threw about 36 targets a game; Baltimore, at about −10, threw about 24. The same 25% target share is 9 targets a game in Arizona and 6 in Baltimore, a 50% difference produced mostly by the play-caller’s taste, with game script (Arizona trailed a lot) and the quarterback’s legs (Lamar Jackson’s scrambles and designed runs are pass calls and run plays that never become targets) doing the rest.

That gives the basic volume equation for any receiver:

\[\text{targets per game} \approx \text{plays per game} \times \text{pass rate} \times \text{target rate} \times \text{target share}\]

where the pass rate is set by PROE plus the situations the team gets into, and the “target rate” (the share of pass plays that produce a target, rather than a sack, scramble or throwaway) is usually around 85-90%. A coordinator change moves the first three terms at a stroke: his pace, his pass rate, even how often his pass calls end in a target. It moves the last term more selectively. A clear number-one receiver’s share mostly comes with him (that is his role), but a play-caller with a different position split, say one who feeds his tight end, reshuffles the shares of everyone below the top.

Pace

Pace is the number of plays. In 2025 the slowest offenses (Miami, Minnesota and Las Vegas) ran about 55 plays a game; the fastest (Dallas and Chicago) ran about 64-65. Ten extra plays a game is roughly 15-18% more opportunity for every player on the offense, before anyone’s share changes. Pace has two sources: how quickly a team gets to the line and snaps the ball (its tempo, built in In-Game Adjustments and Tempo), and how many first downs it earns, since drives that keep moving produce more plays. A good defense helps too: an offense gets more possessions when its own defense gets off the field fast.

Game script

The score is the last term, and it is the one fantasy players talk about most. Game script is the way the score shapes play calling. A team ahead late runs the ball (the four-minute offense), which keeps the clock moving (an incomplete pass stops it), protects against turnovers and wears down a defense; a team behind throws, because it needs points fast and passes gain more per play. The effect is large enough to swamp a play-caller’s taste.

For fantasy, game script is why a receiver on a bad team can be a good pick (his team will trail, and trailing teams throw) and why a running back on a good team can be one (his team will lead, and leading teams run). It is also why one game’s usage can mislead: a running back who got 28 carries in a 31-3 win got them because of the score, not because his role grew, and the week his team trails he may get 11. When you read a weekly usage line, ask what the score was.

You can forecast the script before kickoff, and the tool is the betting market. A game’s point spread is the bookmakers’ estimate of the winning margin (“Chiefs −7” means Kansas City is expected to win by about seven), and the total (over/under) is their estimate of the combined points. Together they give each team an implied team total: half the total plus half the spread for the favorite, minus half the spread for the underdog. A −7 favorite in a game with a 45-point total is implied to score about 26 and allow about 19. For a whole season, the win totals posted before the season do the same job. In 2025 the spread did a decent job of predicting the script: teams favored by 7 or more led by about 4 points on an average snap, and teams that were underdogs by 7 or more trailed by about 8. Implied totals predicted actual points about as well (big favorites scored about 28 a game, big underdogs about 17).12

What the script changes is more specific than “trailing teams throw.” Those 2025 big favorites ran about 26 times a game and the big underdogs about 23, but both dropped back to pass about 35 times. The favorite’s extra snaps went to the ground, and its extra points went to whoever scores them. So for fantasy, a big spread is mainly a running-back and touchdown signal: the lead back of a heavy favorite gets more carries and more goal-line trips. For receivers, the bad-team bonus is real but smaller than people expect, because a team that trails all day also runs fewer plays and scores less. The cleanest usage numbers come from the stretches when the game was close, the same reason PROE is measured in neutral situations, and why analysts drop garbage time when they measure a team.

CautionCommon misconception

“Pass-heavy teams are always better for receivers.” Separate the team’s taste from its fate. Taste is PROE: how much the play-caller likes to throw when the game is close. It repeats, and it reliably raises volume. Fate is the score: a bad team that trails throws more late, but those targets depend on being behind, come in shorter bursts, and often come against a prevent defense that gives up short catches to protect the lead. They count for fantasy all the same, but they vanish the week the team keeps it close. Project a receiver’s volume from the taste first, then adjust for the likely fate using the betting lines.

Scoring formats and the market

Standard, half-PPR and PPR

Fantasy football began as a touchdown game. The first league, the Greater Oakland Professional Pigskin Prognosticators League, was dreamed up in 1962 by Bill Winkenbach, a limited partner in the Oakland Raiders, and held its first draft in 1963, with scoring based on touchdowns and kicks.13 As statistics became easy to collect, leagues added yards, and the scoring that most hosting sites called “standard” settled into one point per 10 rushing or receiving yards, six per touchdown, one per 25 passing yards and four per passing touchdown, minus two for an interception or a lost fumble (some sites take off only one for an interception).

Standard scoring had a problem: it made running backs who scored touchdowns far more valuable than anyone else, and it made a 9-catch, 70-yard day by a slot receiver worth less than a single 1-yard touchdown run. PPR (point per reception) scoring adds one point for every catch, and half-PPR adds half a point. It spread through serious leagues in the 2000s, and a version of it is now the default at the major hosting sites (full PPR at ESPN, half-PPR at Yahoo).14 The change is not cosmetic: it moves the whole market toward the players whose usage is receptions.

ADP: the market price

ADP (average draft position) is the average pick at which a player is taken across many drafts on a hosting site. It is the closest thing fantasy has to a market price, and it behaves like one: it aggregates thousands of opinions and reacts to news. It comes in two forms. Overall ADP is the pick number across all positions (Barkley’s 17 in 2024); positional ADP is the order within a position (“RB6”: the sixth running back taken). Positional ADP is what you compare with a player’s finish, which is also counted by position.

Overall ADP raises a question that connects straight back to Building a Roster. NFL teams treat running backs as cheap; fantasy drafters take them in the first round. Why does the same position sit at the bottom of one market and the top of the other? Because fantasy has its own version of positional value, and it comes from two things. First, fantasy scores touches and touchdowns, and a lead back gets more of both than anyone but the quarterback. Second, and more important, a player’s draft value is not his points but his points above replacement: what he scores beyond the best player you could get for free or late at the same lineup slot. In a 10-team league that starts one quarterback, two running backs, two receivers and a tight end, the baseline is roughly the 10th quarterback, the 20th running back, the 20th receiver and the 10th tight end, the worst players who still start somewhere. This idea, value over replacement, is the core of value-based drafting, which Footballguys co-founder Joe Bryant popularized in the mid-1990s.15

The useful way to think about a pick borrows the idea of surplus value from Building a Roster. An NFL team doesn’t profit from drafting a good player; it profits from drafting one who outplays the slot he cost. A fantasy pick works the same way. The 5th running back taken who finishes 5th was fairly priced. If prices are set by people reading box scores, they will tend to price last season’s touchdowns as if they will repeat, and touchdowns are the least repeatable thing a receiver does. And the most predictable way roles change is a new play-caller.

ImportantWhy it exists

Every fantasy league is a small market with a fixed supply of players, and ADP is how the market’s beliefs become visible. It exists because drafts on the big hosting sites are public data, and averaging them reveals the consensus. The trade-off for you as a drafter: following ADP keeps you from big mistakes, but it also guarantees you no edge, because you’re paying the consensus price. Edge comes from disagreeing with the market for a reason the market is slow to see, and scheme is the slowest thing for a box-score market to see.

When the coordinator changes

An offensive coordinator is not a neutral pipe between the players and the box score. Each one has a fingerprint: how often he throws, how fast he plays, how much he uses motion and play-action, how much he aims at the tight end or the backs. Those habits travel with him from team to team, more faithfully than almost anything else in football, because they come from what he believes and how he was taught. When a new coordinator arrives, the most useful question is not “is he good?” but “what did his last offense look like, and who on this roster fits it?”

Before you use that fingerprint, check one thing: who calls the plays. A coordinator who doesn’t call plays changes much less than one who does. Use the method below in order.

  1. Who calls plays, and what does the head coach want? The play-caller’s fingerprint matters most, but run/pass identity is often the head coach’s: Jim Harbaugh’s Chargers and Dan Campbell’s Lions both want to run the ball and control the clock, whoever calls the plays.
  2. What did his last offense do? Its neutral PROE, pace, motion and play-action rates, and how it split targets among receivers, tight ends and backs. Separate the scheme from the players: part of any old offense’s numbers belonged to its stars, and those don’t travel.
  3. What roster did he inherit? A fingerprint needs players to fit it: a coordinator who feeds the tight end needs a tight end who can run routes. Count the vacated targets too, the targets that left with departed players: the play-caller’s position split decides where they go. (A quarterback change works through the same terms, the volume and who he trusts, but usually leaves the position split alone.)
  4. Who moves up and who moves down? Translate the changes into shares and volume, position by position, and name a loser as well as a winner: every target share someone gains, someone else gives up.
  5. Check it early, but don’t trust it early (see the misconception box below).

Three 2026 changes make good test cases, because they cover the three kinds of change.

  • Detroit: a new play-caller from a different system. The Lions are on their third offensive coordinator in three years. Ben Johnson left after 2024 to become the Bears’ head coach; his successor, John Morton, lost the play-calling to head coach Dan Campbell midway through 2025 and was let go after the season. The new coordinator is Drew Petzing, Arizona’s coordinator from 2023 to 2025, and he is expected to call the plays.16 Petzing came up under Kevin Stefanski in Minnesota and Cleveland (he was the Browns’ tight ends coach, then quarterbacks coach), a multiple-tight-end, play-action lineage, and his Arizona offenses threw about a third of their targets to tight ends every year, the most in the league in 2025. Detroit also traded running back David Montgomery to Houston in March 2026, leaving Jahmyr Gibbs as the clear lead back.17
  • Los Angeles Chargers: a famous fingerprint arrives. Jim Harbaugh replaced coordinator Greg Roman with Mike McDaniel, fired as Miami’s head coach in January 2026 after four seasons. McDaniel calls the plays, and his Miami offenses used pre-snap motion more than almost anyone, on 68-79% of their plays every season from 2022 to 2025.18
  • Kansas City: a new coordinator, the same play-caller. Eric Bieniemy returned as the Chiefs’ coordinator, replacing Matt Nagy. But Andy Reid has called Kansas City’s plays for almost all of his tenure (the exception was the last weeks of 2017, when he handed them to Nagy), including throughout Bieniemy’s first stint (2018-2022), and said in January 2026 that play-calling remains his job. And not everything moved his way: Detroit is still under center on about 46% of its plays (48% in 2025), nowhere near Arizona’s 28%. Petzing has kept that piece of the offense he inherited, which is a reminder that a play-caller adapts to his line and his quarterback too.

Be honest about the tight end number as well. Arizona’s third of the targets was partly scheme and partly that Trey McBride was simply the Cardinals’ best receiver for three years; the scheme part travels to Detroit, the McBride part doesn’t. And LaPorta drew about 19% of the targets in the nine 2025 games he played, so much of the team’s rise is just a healthy LaPorta, not a new philosophy. (The 2025 “before” dots also mix two play-callers, Morton and then Campbell.) Who gives up the targets? So far, slightly, both receivers: Amon-Ra St. Brown from 31% to 29% and Jameson Williams from 19% to 16%, moves inside the noise for now, but the direction the fingerprint predicts.

The PROE jump (−6 to about 0) is harder to read. It isn’t the score: the Lions trailed on an average snap by about four points, but PROE is measured only in neutral situations, where they were roughly level, and xpass already allows for the score. It is about one and a half noise bars, so it is a lean, pointing toward Petzing’s pass-first Arizona (+6), not proof. The clearest change is not scheme at all: with Montgomery traded, Gibbs took 83% of Detroit’s runs, up from 56%. That is a usage change you could have predicted in March.

Kansas City: same play-caller, different ingredients. Reid still calls the plays, so the theory says little should change, and the PROE barely did. But the Chiefs have been under center on 39% of plays (19% in 2025) and have used play-action on 37% of passes (15% in 2025), both far outside the noise. That pattern fits a team built around a new lead back, Walker, who took 76% of the Chiefs’ runs (and about 16% of the targets). It may also be Bieniemy: a coordinator who doesn’t call plays still builds the weekly game plan, the run-game install and the situational packages, and four games can’t separate his influence from Walker’s. Either way the lesson is the first step of the method: when the play-caller hasn’t changed, look for the roster change and the game plan, not a new fingerprint.

CautionCommon misconception

“Four weeks in, the new offense is clearly X.” Four games are about 240-260 plays and 120-180 neutral-situation plays per team. With that many plays, a team’s PROE has a standard error of about 4 percentage points, so a change of 5 or 6 points can be noise. Rates measured on every play (motion, under center) settle faster than rates measured on a subset (PROE, target shares). Big, consistent moves toward the new play-caller’s old numbers (the Chargers’ motion rate) are believable early; small moves, or moves against the fingerprint, need another month.

The same goes for a player’s own shares. In 2025, a receiver’s or tight end’s target share over Weeks 1-4 correlated about 0.67 with his share over the rest of the season, and the typical miss was about 5 percentage points: enough to tell a 30% alpha from a 15% third option, not enough to tell 22% from 26%.20

Watch for it on Sunday

TipWatch for it on Sunday: the scheme-shift draft

Before the season (or now, for the 2026 test cases), pick one team with a new play-caller and write down a prediction for each skill position. Then check it after four weeks and again at mid-season.

Before kickoff (the forecast):

  • Who calls the plays? If it’s the same person as last year, look for roster changes instead.
  • His last offense’s fingerprint: neutral PROE, plays per game, motion and play-action rates, and the share of targets to RBs, TEs and WRs. Which way does each differ from this team’s last season?
  • Translate it: which position’s share should rise, which should fall, and does the team’s total volume rise or fall? Name the player who gains most and the one who loses most.

During the game (the live check):

  • Who’s on the field on third down and in the two-minute drill? That’s the passing-down back and the receivers the play-caller trusts.
  • Does the tight end release or stay in? Count it for one quarter: on how many dropbacks did he run a route?
  • Who’s in at the goal line? Watch the substitution inside the 5. That player owns the touchdowns.
  • What’s the score? Discount the usage from a blowout: the leading team’s carries and the trailing team’s targets are game script, not role.

TipPredict the play 2: the receiver who changes teams

A wide receiver drew 25% of his old team’s targets as its clear number one. That team ran 64 plays a game and dropped back to pass on 62% of them. He signs with a team whose new play-caller’s last offense ran 58 plays a game and dropped back on 54%. On both teams about 87% of dropbacks end in a target (the rest are sacks, scrambles and throwaways). The new play-caller’s offenses have also thrown about 30% of their targets to tight ends, against 18% on the receiver’s old team, and the new team has a good tight end.

Use the volume equation: roughly how many targets per game should you forecast for him, before and after the move? What in the new offense could cut his share, and what would make you raise the forecast during the season?

TipAnswer

About 7 a game, down from about 8.6. Work the equation one term at a time. Old team: 64 plays × 0.62 pass rate × 0.87 target rate ≈ 34.5 team targets a game, and 25% of that is about 8.6. New team: 58 × 0.54 × 0.87 ≈ 27.2 team targets, and 25% of that is about 6.8. Same player, same share, about a fifth fewer targets: roughly 30 over a 17-game season, and at a typical two-thirds catch rate about 20 fewer catches (20 PPR points) before you count a single lost yard.

The share is the term to argue about. His 25% is his role as a clear number one, and that part tends to travel. But the new play-caller’s position split sends about 30% of the targets to tight ends instead of 18%. Those 12 extra points of share come from somewhere, usually the second and third receivers first, but a number one can lose a couple of points too. A forecast of 22-25% (6-7 targets a game) is the honest range.

Raise it if, after a month, his share is clearly above that range (28-30%, beyond the typical 5-point early miss), meaning the play-caller is building around him more than his history suggests; if the team throws more than its play-caller’s history (a higher PROE, or more snaps from a faster pace); or if the tight end gets hurt. Be careful with extra passing that comes from trailing: that is game script, not taste, and it won’t hold in the weeks the team keeps it close.

Run or pass? Who on the offense is likely to touch the ball, and what kind of fantasy player is B?

TipAnswer

A pass, very likely, and B is the passing-down back. The situation alone makes it a pass: third-and-8 is a passing down at any score, and in 2025 teams trailing by 9 to 15 points in the fourth quarter threw on about four plays in five even before you add the down and distance. The personnel agree: three receivers to one side, the quarterback in the gun, and the defense in dime, expecting a throw.

The back on the field tells you about usage. The team swapped its early-down runner, A, for B, a back it trusts to pick up a blitzer and run a route, which is why B is on the field when the team must throw. Expect B to stay in to block if the defense shows pressure, and to release to the flat or over the middle as a checkdown if it doesn’t. The slot receivers, H and the detached tight end Y, are the other likely targets on a throw aimed just past the line to gain. And if the offense does run, it will be a draw to B: with only five defenders in the box, a draw against dime is the one run coordinators like on third-and-long, and it’s another reason B rather than A is out there.

For fantasy, B is the classic PPR back: few carries, but targets in exactly the game scripts where his team trails. In standard scoring he is barely worth a roster spot; in PPR, on a team that trails often, he can be a weekly starter, the 2025 Kenny Gainwell profile. A, meanwhile, is a game-script player: valuable when his team leads, invisible when it doesn’t.

Takeaways

  • Fantasy points are outputs; usage is the input the scheme controls. Opportunity statistics (target share, air-yards share, WOPR, route participation) repeat from season to season far better than efficiency, and touchdowns barely repeat at all.
  • Read receivers in order: on the field, running a route, targeted, targeted deep, productive per route. Snap share hides blocking snaps; route participation and YPRR fix that, and a productive part-timer whose role grows is the cheapest upside in fantasy.
  • A running back’s touchdowns belong to whoever has the goal-line job. Snap share and carries describe the role between the 20s; red-zone share, and especially who gets the ball inside the 5 and the 2, decides the scoring. In 12 personnel the tight end is a receiver only if he runs routes.
  • Volume = plays × pass rate × target rate × share. PROE sets the team’s taste and moves volume, not a number one’s share; pace adds or subtracts plays for everyone; game script overrides both late in games, and the betting lines forecast it (a big favorite’s extra plays are runs and touchdowns).
  • Know the format and the price. PPR rewards catches and lifts pass-catching backs and slot receivers. ADP is a market price set by points above replacement, which is why fantasy running backs are dear when NFL ones are cheap; value is beating that price, most often by spotting a usage change the box score hasn’t shown.
  • When the play-caller changes, his fingerprint travels; his old stars don’t. Check who calls plays and what the head coach wants, what his last offense did and how much of it was its players, and what roster he inherited; name a loser as well as a winner; then verify with a month of data, trusting every-play rates sooner than shares.
